MIS 320 · Syllabus

Principles of Management Information Systems: the syllabus

Western Washington University, College of Business and Economics. In person, eleven weeks: two lectures a week, one lab a week, three exams of which your best two count. Everything you turn in goes to Canvas.

2Course description

Information technology is changing how organizations work, and organizations need people who understand both what it can do and where it goes wrong. MIS 320 is built to give you that understanding in a business context: not how to build the technology, but how to manage it, which means deciding what gets built, bought, recorded, and measured, and being accountable for the result.

The course runs on a free digital textbook written for it. Every chapter is anchored on a real company at a real moment (Starbucks, Zara, Domino's, Healthcare.gov, Walmart, Nike, the Oakland A's, TikTok, Change Healthcare, Equifax, Netflix), and every chapter reads its case through the same framework: the five components of an information system, hardware, software, data, networks, and people, and the rule that a system is only as good as its weakest one. Topics include the value of information, technology and competitive advantage, business processes, systems analysis, databases, enterprise systems, business intelligence, machine learning, cybersecurity, IT governance, software and user experience, and cloud computing.

The labs give you the hands-on side. Over ten weeks you will use Excel, Tableau, and SQL on the data of one fictional company, Cascadia Outfitters, find a problem hidden in that data three different ways, audit the company's security, evaluate a real interface against professional usability standards, and design an information structure of your own.

3Learning outcomes and how they are assessed

D = lecture discussion · L = labs and lab quizzes · E = exams. By the end of the course you should be able to:

  1. Describe the role of information systems in organizations, their impacts, and their use for competitive advantage (D, E).
  2. Identify the five components of an information system in any system you meet, and apply the weakest component rule to a failure (D, L, E).
  3. Explain when information has value: only when it changes a decision, and only when the change is worth the cost (D, E).
  4. Describe the nature of data, hardware, software, and networks, and how databases store and relate facts (D, L, E).
  5. Describe how information systems are analyzed, designed, built, and maintained, and why projects fail (D, E).
  6. Explain how business intelligence and machine learning turn data into decisions, and what goes wrong when the measure becomes the target (D, L, E).
  7. Summarize how information systems affect security, privacy, accountability, accessibility, and the people who use them (D, L, E).
  8. Construct accurate, well-organized spreadsheet analyses, including conditional logic, pivot tables, and charts (L, E).
  9. Query a relational database with SQL, including joins across tables (L, E).
  10. Use AI tools as a tutor without outsourcing your judgment, and say plainly when and how you used them (D, L).

5The weekly rhythm

The quarter starts on a Wednesday, so Week 1 has one lecture. Every week after that has the same shape:

  • Monday and Wednesday, 80 minutes each: lecture and discussion. Monday is concepts and three short cases. Wednesday is the week's main case, discussed rather than presented. Read the chapter's concept sections before Monday and the case before Wednesday.
  • Thursday or Friday, 80 minutes: lab. Ten labs, one a week, each paired with that week's chapter.
  • A lab quiz on Canvas each week, on that week's lab.
  • Three exams, in class: Monday of Week 5, Monday of Week 9, and the final in Week 11. Exam days are not lecture days for attendance.

7Lectures: discussion and attendance

Attendance at lecture is required. Every session has in-class activities and discussion that cannot be made up from the slides or the book: Monday has a think-pair-share on the chapter's concepts, Wednesday is the case argued out loud. Attendance is taken every lecture day and is worth one point a day, seventeen points in all. Exam days are not attendance days. No partial credit for a day and no make-up for an unexcused absence, because the point is for taking part in something that only happens in the room. Documented absences (illness, university travel, family emergencies) are excused; tell me before class if you can, after if you cannot.

Each chapter ends with five discussion questions that cannot be answered from the text. They are never submitted. They are what Wednesday is for. You are welcome to use AI to prepare for discussion: to test an argument, to find the counter-argument, to check a fact. What you say in the room is yours.

8Labs: 35 points, three ways

Labs are where the ideas become skills, and every lab is worth the same. Within the 35 points:

  • Attendance, 10 points. One point per lab, ten labs, same rules as lecture.
  • Completion and submission, 12.5 points. Labs 1 to 8 end with a certificate you download after clearing every checkpoint, and most also ask for a file (your workbook, your queries). Labs 9 and 10 are reports. Submit exactly what the lab's "What to submit" section lists, on Canvas, by the deadline there. Credit is for a complete, on-time submission; the checkpoints already told you whether your numbers were right.
  • Lab quizzes, 12.5 points. A short Canvas quiz each week on that week's lab: what the tool does, what the numbers meant, and for Labs 9 and 10 what the reading said. Opens after the lab session, due before the next one. You have one week after a quiz closes to review your answers.

Labs are completed individually. Where a lab asks for a formula in a cell, the cell must contain the live formula: a correct formula with a wrong answer earns partial credit; a bare typed number earns none.

9Exams: best two of three

Three exams, in class, 24 points each, mostly lecture and case material with some lab questions, in multiple-choice and short-essay form. The lowest score is dropped; your best two count for 48 points.

  • Exam 1, Monday of Week 5: Chapters 1 to 5 and Labs 1 to 4.
  • Exam 2, Monday of Week 9: Chapters 6, 7, 8, 10, 11 and Labs 5 to 8.
  • Final, Week 11: Chapters 9, 12, 13 and Labs 9 and 10, plus the themes that run through the whole course.

Because one exam is dropped, there are no early or late exams and no make-ups: a missed exam is the one that gets dropped. You must sit at least two of the three to pass the course. The only exception is a documented medical or personal emergency that costs you a second exam; if that happens, talk to me before the exam if at all possible.

10AI policy: it depends on the assignment

AI is a tool this course teaches you to use well, which means the rule changes with what an assignment is for. Lab 1 sets the policy on the first day and every lab and chapter repeats it in a box near the top, using three lights:

LightWhat it meansWhere it applies
GreenAI as a tutor: explain a concept, quiz you, check your understanding, help you prepare an argument.Lecture discussion preparation, reading the chapters, Labs 1 and 6 to 8 as each lab's box specifies.
YellowAllowed with limits stated in that assignment: usually explaining and quizzing yes, producing the deliverable no.Labs 9 and 10, where AI may explain the reading but may not find your examples or write your report.
RedNot allowed, because the skill being built is the one the AI would do for you.Labs 2 to 5 (Excel), and all exams.

The test throughout is the calculator test: if the tool does the thing the assignment exists to teach, it is not allowed on that assignment. Whenever you use AI beyond a grammar check on anything you submit, say so in the submission: which tool, for what part, with what prompt. Undisclosed AI use where it is restricted is academic dishonesty.

11Late work

Late lab submissions are accepted with a penalty of 10 percent per day, to a maximum of nine days; after nine days the work is not accepted. No exceptions to the rubric. Lab quizzes close on their due date and cannot be taken late. Exams follow the policy in section 9.

13The fine print

The rules that settle most arguments before they start.

  • What counts as an excused absence. Illness, university-sponsored travel, religious observance, and family emergencies, with documentation sent by email within one week of the absence. An excused absence keeps the attendance point. Anything else is unexcused, and there is no cap on unexcused absences; each one simply costs its point.
  • Arriving late, leaving early. Attendance is taken once, at the start. If you arrive after it is taken, see me at the end of class; being there for the discussion is what earns the point, and I would rather you come late than not at all. Leaving before the activity forfeits the point.
  • Lab sections. Attend the lab section you are registered in. Attending a different section in a given week is fine if you arrange it with the lab professor beforehand and there is a seat.
  • Lab quizzes. One attempt, timed, closed book except the lab page itself, taken on Canvas by the posted deadline. Quizzes do not reopen. You can review your answers for one week after the quiz closes.
  • Late work is measured by Canvas. The Canvas timestamp is the submission time. Days late are counted in 24-hour blocks from the deadline, weekends included. A file uploaded in the wrong format or to the wrong assignment is late until the right one arrives.
  • Exam conditions. In class, closed book, closed notes, no devices. Arrive on time; a student who arrives after the first student has finished cannot sit the exam, and it becomes the dropped one. The final's date and time are set by the university and cannot be moved.
  • Regrades. Ask within seven days of the grade being posted, in writing, saying which item and why. A regrade reviews the whole item, so the grade can go down as well as up. After seven days the grade stands.
  • Rounding. Final percentages are rounded to the nearest whole number once, at the end, using the usual rule (89.5 rounds to 90; 89.4 does not). There is no extra credit and no end-of-quarter negotiation.
  • Devices in class. Laptops and tablets are welcome for notes and for the in-class activities. Phones stay away during discussion. Do not record lectures without permission.
  • Email. Use your WWU address, put MIS 320 in the subject, and expect a reply within one business day. Questions about a lab go to the lab professor first.
