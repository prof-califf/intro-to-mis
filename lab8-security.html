What you will do in this lab

Three things, and none of them needs a line of code. In Part 1 you take the chapter's CIA triad and use it to say what an attack actually took from a company. In Part 2, the main event, you open the co-op's inbox: seven messages arrived this morning, some of them are attacks, and your job is to tell which by checking what each one claims against what you already know about Cascadia. In Part 3 you decide what you would change on Monday. The checkpoints are one-word answers. What you actually learn is a habit: never act on a message until one of its claims has been checked against a record the sender does not control.

What you know about Cascadia

Part 1The triad: what an attack actually takes

Chapter 10 said security protects three properties of information, and that every attack takes at least one of them. Confidentiality: the wrong people can now read it. Integrity: it was changed and nobody noticed. Availability: the people who need it cannot get to it. The chapter's argument was that a breach is usually an availability problem before it is a confidentiality one, and that the first question a manager asks is not what was stolen but what stopped.

Practice the sorting on the co-op. Four things that could happen to Cascadia this year are below. For each one, name the property that was attacked first. One word, spelled as above. Most incidents eventually damage more than one, so ask what the attacker took at the moment of the attack, not what happened afterwards.

Notice which of the four would be hardest to discover. A locked register announces itself. A posted member list gets found eventually. A reorder point quietly set to zero looks like an ordinary number until the shelf is empty, which is why the chapter called integrity the property nobody checks until it is too late.

Part 2The inbox: seven messages from this morning

Chapter 10's breach began with a stolen password. Most breaches begin one step before that, with a message that persuades somebody to hand something over. The defense that scales is not spotting bad grammar or scary logos. It is this: find the one factual claim the message makes, and check it against a record the sender does not control. The box at the top of this lab is that record. Every message below either survives that check or does not. In the chapter's kill chain, every one of these messages is an attempt at the first stage, initial entry. It is also the cheapest stage to break, because nothing has happened yet.

Seven messages arrived at Cascadia this morning. For each one, decide whether it is an attack or legitimate, and enter that one word as the checkpoint. Do the checking before you decide. Some of these are meant to feel urgent, and one of the legitimate ones is more urgent-sounding than most of the attacks.

Message 1 · to accounts payable

From: billing@nordvik-outdoor-payments.com
Subject: URGENT: Updated remittance details, action required before Friday

"Hello, this is the finance team at Nordvik Outdoor, your US-based climbing hardware supplier. Due to a banking migration we require all invoice payments to be sent to our new account, details attached. Please update before Friday's payment run to avoid disruption to your spring orders."

Message 2 · to member services

From: privacy-request@mail-cascadia-coop.net
Subject: Formal complaint from member #1287, records required

"Member 1287 has filed a formal complaint regarding billing errors on their account. Under our review procedure, please reply with the member's full record, including name, address, and complete purchase history, so we can begin the investigation today."

Message 3 · a text to the Controller's phone

From: +1 (360) 555-0142, unknown number
Subject: (no subject)

"Hey it's me, stuck in the supplier meeting and can't talk. Need you to pick up five $100 gift cards for the staff awards tonight and text me the codes off the back so I can hand them out from here. Will square up with you tomorrow. Thx."

Message 4 · to the buying team

From: shipping@fraservalleyrubber.ca
Subject: URGENT: winter boot shipment delayed 3 days

"Hi, apologies for the short notice. The Fraser Valley Rubber order due Thursday is held at the Blaine crossing and will now arrive Monday. Nothing is needed from you; we just did not want your Bellingham and Spokane stores caught short. Reply if the new date is a problem and we will split the shipment."

Message 5 · to all staff

From: it-support@cascadia-coop-support.com
Subject: Action required: password reset within 24 hours

"As part of our security upgrade, all staff must reset their password within 24 hours using the link below. Accounts not reset by tomorrow will be locked. This is an automated message; do not reply. [Reset my password]"

Message 6 · to member services

From: d.okafor@gmail.com
Subject: Address change, member 1042

"Hi, I'm a Lifetime member (1042) and I've moved from Tacoma to Olympia. Can you update my mailing address so the dividend statement comes to the right place? I'd rather not put the new address in email, so call me at the number you have on file and I'll give it to you then. Thanks, Daniel."

Message 7 · to the Bellingham store manager

From: olympia.store@cascadia-coop.org
Subject: Quick favor: member list for our grand opening

"Hi! I'm the new manager at the Olympia store, we open next month and I'd love to invite your Bellingham members to the launch. Could you export your store's member list with emails and send it over? The GM said you'd be able to help. Thanks so much!"

Look back at what caught each attack. Not the tone, not the spelling, and not the logo. A country, a member number, a store that does not exist, a domain that is nearly right, a request that bypasses a policy already on file. Every one was a claim you could check in under a minute against something the sender did not control. Security people call this out-of-band verification: confirm the request through a channel the requester did not choose. Call the number on file, not the one in the message. Look up the member, do not take the number as given. It is the single habit that would have stopped every message above, and it costs nothing.

Notice also what the legitimate messages had in common. Neither asked you to bypass anything. The supplier asked for nothing at all. The member asked you to follow the policy rather than skip it. Real requests tend to survive verification because they are not afraid of it.

Part 3Monday morning: the one control

Seven messages, five attacks, and every one of them was aimed at a person, not a server. Chapter 10's argument was that most security is management, and this is what that looks like in practice: the decisions that stop these attacks are about policies, habits, and who is allowed to do what, not about code.

You are the security lead, and the board has given you Monday morning and no budget. Pick one control from the list below to put in place first, and be ready to say why that one before the others. There is no checkpoint for this, because it is the part of the job that is actually yours.

  • A verification rule. No bank detail, password, or member record is ever changed or sent on the strength of a message alone. One phone call to a number on file, every time.
  • Multi-factor authentication on every staff login, so that a password stolen by a message like number 5 is not enough by itself.
  • Least privilege. Decide, role by role, who can read the member table at all, so that a request like number 7 has fewer people it could work on.
  • Training. Walk every employee through these seven messages, because the person who has seen the pattern once recognizes it the second time.

Write your choice and your reason in two sentences at the top of your reflection below. Then answer the reflection question.
