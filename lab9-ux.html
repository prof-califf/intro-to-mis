Lab 9 · Week 9

UX Design: The Heuristic Evaluation

Lab session · 80 minPairs with Chapter 12 · Software →

Wednesday's case was a pizza a blind man could not order, and a court deciding that the interface is the part of a system that decides who gets to be a customer. This lab hands you the instrument professionals use to judge an interface before a court has to: ten usability heuristics, written in 1994 and still the standard. You will learn all ten, go deep on three, and find them working, or failing, in the world around you.

Contents Part 1 · Read all ten  ·  Part 2 · Go deep on three  ·  Part 3 · Find examples  ·  Part 4 · The report  ·  What to submit

No Cascadia data this week, and no formulas. The skill is looking. By the end you will have written a short heuristic evaluation report, which is the document a UX designer produces on the first day of any project, and you will not be able to use an app again without noticing what it is doing to you.

Yellow · AI as a study partner, not a spotter

Green: ask an AI to quiz you on the ten heuristics, or to explain one you find confusing. Red: asking it to find your examples, or to write your evaluation. The whole point of this lab is that you noticed something in the world with your own eyes and can defend the call. An example an AI found is not one you noticed, and a paragraph it wrote is not a judgment you made. Name any AI help in your report.

Part 1Read all ten

Start with the summary of all ten heuristics, by the Nielsen Norman Group: 10 Usability Heuristics for User Interface Design. It is one page. Read it twice, once for the list and once for the examples. This week's quiz draws on the full list, not only the three you go deep on.

Key conceptHeuristic evaluation

A heuristic is a rule of thumb: not a law, but a principle that is right often enough to check against. A heuristic evaluation is an expert walking through an interface with the ten rules in hand and writing down where it keeps them and where it breaks them. It costs nothing but attention, which is why it is the first thing done on any project and the thing Domino's never did.

Part 2Go deep on three

Pick the three heuristics that most interest you. For each, read the full article and watch the short video. The videos are two to four minutes; the articles are five to ten. Take notes on what the heuristic is for, because that is what Part 3 asks you to judge.

  2. 2. Match between the system and the real world · article · video
  3. 3. User control and freedom · article · video
  4. 4. Consistency and standards · article · video
  5. 5. Error prevention · article · video
  6. 6. Recognition rather than recall · article · video

Part 3Find one example for each

For each of your three heuristics, find one real system or design that shows it. Digital or not: an app, a website, a parking meter, a microwave, a form, a sign. The world is full of interfaces.

  • At least one example must be good: it clearly satisfies the heuristic.
  • At least one must be bad: it fails the heuristic, or does the opposite.

Evaluate each example against the heuristic's own criteria, as the article states them. You will need to justify the call, so write down which criterion is met or missed, not only that it is. "The app is confusing" is an opinion; "the app changes the label of the same button between two screens, which breaks consistency and standards" is an evaluation.

A test for a good example

Could a reader who has never used the thing understand from your paragraph and your screenshot what it does and why that matters? If the example only works because you were there, find a clearer one. And pick something with a decision behind it: somebody chose that label, that flow, that missing undo button. Your job is to name the choice.

Part 4Write the Heuristic Evaluation Report

One document. It must include:

  • Your name.
  • For each of your three heuristics: its number and title, exactly as Nielsen Norman lists it.
  • For each example: one to two paragraphs explaining why it does or does not satisfy the heuristic's criteria.
  • A screenshot or photograph of each example.
  • Reference links for anything you cite. No particular citation format is required.

Three heuristics, three examples, three short evaluations. Aim for two to four pages including images.

Why this pairs with Robles

Domino's argued there were no rules for its website. The rules on this page were nineteen years old when Guillermo Robles tried to order a pizza, and the accessibility guidelines the court held the company to were older still. A heuristic evaluation is cheap, takes an afternoon, and is the difference between finding the problem yourself and reading about it in a Ninth Circuit opinion.
