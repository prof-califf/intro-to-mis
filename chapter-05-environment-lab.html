The Footprint Machine

Chapter 5 said AI's cost lives in the multiplication, the building, and the numbers nobody publishes. This week you find out how much of that is AI and how much is the rest of your phone. You read four articles about AI's energy and water, then four about things you already do every day: streaming, YouTube, TikTok, and gaming. You build a technology impact calculator with Claude, one round at a time, and put it next to a flight to San Francisco, a burger, and a golf course. Then you sketch what an environmental rule for data centers could actually look like, watch a short film on e-waste, and take a side on whether the rules should target AI data centers or every data center.

1

At home: Read what AI costs

AI data centers are in the news as the environment's newest villain. Before you decide whether that's fair, read what has actually been measured. Below are four articles: one from a company about its own product, one from journalists who did the math themselves, one that went viral, and one that zooms out to every data center in the country. About 30 minutes.

Read each one. You don't need every word. Under each article is a list of the details you'll use later, when you build your calculator in section 3. Check each detail against the article as you read, and keep notes in your own words. (If an article hits a paywall, the details on the card are enough to keep going.)

Units, once: a watt-hour (Wh) is a small amount of electricity, about what a microwave uses in a few seconds. 1,000 Wh is a kilowatt-hour (kWh), the unit on an electric bill. One Wh is 3,600 joules, which matters because one article uses joules.

Article A · The company's own number

Google, August 2025: "Measuring the environmental impact of AI inference", by Amin Vahdat and Jeff Dean.

  • A median text prompt to the Gemini app uses about 0.24 Wh of electricity, emits about 0.03 grams of CO2e, and uses about 0.26 mL of water, about five drops.
  • Google compares the energy to watching TV for less than nine seconds.
  • Google says the energy of its median prompt fell about 33 times in one year.
  • The catch: the water counts what cools Google's own chips, not the water used to make the electricity. Critics pointed that out the same week.
  • "Median" means the middle prompt. Half used more, half used less.

Who's saying it: the company selling the product.

Article B · Journalists who did the math

MIT Technology Review, May 2025: "We did the math on AI's energy footprint", by James O'Donnell and Casey Crownhart.

  • They measured open models, because the big companies wouldn't share numbers.
  • A small text model used about 114 joules per reply. A large one used about 6,706 joules (about 1.9 Wh).
  • One standard AI image: about 2,282 joules (about 0.6 Wh).
  • One five-second AI video: about 3.4 million joules (about 944 Wh), more than 700 times an image.
  • Epoch AI's estimate for a typical ChatGPT message: about 0.3 Wh.
  • Their sample day (15 questions, 10 images, 3 short videos) used about 2.9 kWh.
  • Reasoning models were found to use 43 times more energy on simple problems.
  • Electricity used by data centers was 48 percent more carbon-intensive than the US average.

Who's saying it: independent journalists, with researchers at Michigan and Hugging Face doing the measuring.

Article C · The one that went viral

The Washington Post, September 2024: "A bottle of water per email: the hidden environmental costs of using AI chatbots", with researchers at UC Riverside.

  • Writing one 100-word email with GPT-4 used about 519 mL of water, a little more than a standard bottle.
  • The same email used about 0.14 kWh of electricity.
  • The water number counts cooling water at the data center and water used at power plants to make the electricity. That's why it's so much bigger than Google's five drops.
  • An email was modeled as several model calls, not one prompt.
  • The researcher behind it, Shaolei Ren, has since said the figure shouldn't be read as an accurate measure of today's AI.

Who's saying it: a newspaper and university researchers, estimating from outside, about a 2024 model.

Article D · Every data center, zoomed out

Pew Research Center, October 2025: "What we know about energy use at U.S. data centers amid the AI boom", by Rebecca Leppert.

  • US data centers used about 183 terawatt-hours in 2024, a little over 4 percent of US electricity, projected to reach about 426 by 2030.
  • About 60 percent of a data center's electricity runs the servers; cooling takes about 7 percent at efficient sites and over 30 percent at others.
  • US data centers directly consumed about 17 billion gallons of water in 2023. That leaves out water used to make the electricity.
  • Data centers got about 40 percent of their electricity from natural gas, 24 percent from renewables, 20 percent from nuclear, and 15 percent from coal.
  • Pew's own line: whenever you send an email, stream a show, save a photo to the cloud, or ask a chatbot a question, you're using a data center.

Who's saying it: a nonpartisan research center, mostly summarizing the International Energy Agency.

Write it down
  1. Google says five drops of water a prompt. The Post says a bottle an email. In your own words, what does each one count that the other doesn't?
  2. Which article would you trust most for a number in your calculator, and why? (This becomes writeup item 1.)

The takeaway

All four articles are about the same machine, and their numbers don't line up. Nobody here is lying. They drew the line in different places: one prompt or one email, the chips' water or the power plant's water too, a 2024 model or a 2025 one, measured by the company or estimated from outside. That's section 2.5 in your hands. The only company number is from the company, and it's the smallest. Now hold onto a question the headlines skip: is AI the only thing in that building?

At home: Read what your habits cost

You streamed something this week. You probably scrolled TikTok or watched YouTube, and maybe you gamed. All of it runs in the same kind of building as a chatbot. Here are four articles about the digital habits nobody calls a scandal. About 25 minutes, same routine: read, check the details, take notes.

Article E · Netflix and streaming video

International Energy Agency, 2020: "The carbon footprint of streaming video: fact-checking the headlines", by George Kamiya.

  • One hour of streaming video used about 0.077 kWh (77 Wh) and emitted about 36 grams of CO2 in 2019.
  • That counts the data center, the network, and your screen. Most of it is the screen and the network, not the building.
  • The headline it fact-checked claimed half an hour of Netflix emitted 1.6 kg of CO2. That claim rested on a math error, and the think tank behind it later corrected it about eight-fold.
  • The real number depends heavily on your device, your connection, and the video quality.

Who's saying it: an international energy agency, correcting a viral claim.

Article F · YouTube

University of Bristol, May 2019: "Rethinking digital service design could reduce their environmental impact".

  • Researchers estimated YouTube's carbon footprint in 2016 at about 10 million tonnes of CO2e, about the same as a city the size of Glasgow.
  • That came from about 1 billion hours of YouTube watched every day.
  • Most of the energy was in the network, especially the mobile network, not the data center.
  • One design change, not sending video to people only listening to the audio, could cut 100,000 to 500,000 tonnes a year.

Who's saying it: university researchers, about 2016. Note it's a yearly total for everyone, not a per-hour number.

Article G · TikTok and social media

Global Citizen, March 2022: "How Does Your Social Media Use Impact the Planet? Use This Calculator to Find Out", by Joe McCarthy, reporting a Compare the Market analysis of measurements by Greenspector.

  • Scrolling TikTok: about 2.63 grams of CO2 per minute, the highest of ten apps.
  • Reddit about 2.45 g per minute, Pinterest about 1.3, YouTube about 0.46 g per minute.
  • 145 minutes of TikTok a day for a year comes to nearly 140 kg of CO2.
  • The catch: the per-minute numbers were measured by a French consulting firm in 2021 on one phone, a Samsung Galaxy S7, then repackaged by a price-comparison website.

Who's saying it: an advocacy site, citing a marketing analysis, citing a consultant's lab test. Trace it back before you trust it.

Article H · Gaming

Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory: "The Hidden Cost of Computer Gaming", plus the study's summary (Mills and others, 2019).

  • Gaming in the US uses about 34 terawatt-hours a year, about 2.4 percent of all home electricity, with about 24 million tonnes of CO2.
  • That's about $5 billion a year in electricity, and the same emissions as over 5 million cars.
  • One gaming system can use anywhere from 5 to 1,200 kWh a year, depending on the hardware and the game.
  • Cloud gaming, where the graphics run in a data center and stream to you, uses markedly more energy than playing on your own console or PC. The researchers called it one of their biggest surprises.

Who's saying it: a federal research lab, with the study funded by the California Energy Commission. Note: no per-hour number for cloud gaming.

Write it down
  1. Which source in this section do you trust least, and why? Trace its number back as far as the card lets you.
  2. Before you build anything: guess whether AI or your everyday habits will be the bigger bar in your own day. Write the guess down. You'll check it in section 3.

The takeaway

None of these habits made headlines like AI did, and all of them run in data centers. Streaming, YouTube, and gaming each have a real footprint, sometimes a big one. Notice what changed, though: these numbers are older, they mostly count your screen and the network, and two of them are yearly totals for everyone rather than a cost per hour. Turning them into something you can compare to one AI prompt takes an assumption. In the next section, watch who makes that assumption.

Why use AI for this at all?

You could build this in a spreadsheet, and the no-AI path does. What Claude adds is speed: a working, clickable page in a few minutes, with a chart, from a paragraph of instructions. Analysts now build quick tools like this all the time, and the whole skill is in the instructions and the checking.

Where AI helps

  • Writes working code from plain English
  • Converts units without arithmetic slips
  • Builds a chart you can click
  • Changes the whole page when you change your mind

Where to be careful

  • It can fill a gap with a number nobody gave it
  • It can make an assumption look like a fact
  • A clean chart looks more certain than its sources are
  • It can link a number to the wrong article

And the calculator hides what Chapter 5 cared about most. It counts your share of the building. It doesn't count the building's neighbors, its grid, or the bill two counties over. A personal number can be small while the total lands somewhere real.

At home: Under the hood, how your calculator works, Claude included

You've used eight articles, a United Nations flight calculator, and Claude. Here's what was inside each piece of your calculator. About 5 minutes.

1 The numbers

Every number in your calculator is somebody's choice about where to draw the line. Google counted the water that cools its own chips; the Post also counted the water at the power plant. The IEA's streaming hour includes your screen and your Wi-Fi. The TikTok minute was measured on one phone from 2016. The ICAO flight number counts only the CO2 from the engines, not the extra warming from contrails. Same word, "footprint," different boundaries. That's section 2.5's disclosure gap, and now you've seen that it isn't only an AI problem. The only difference is that the AI numbers are newer and fewer.

2 The arithmetic

The calculator does one thing over and over: cost per use, times how many, times how often. That's section 2.3's multiplication. A per-use number is easy to multiply. A yearly total for everyone, like YouTube's 10 million tonnes or gaming's 34 terawatt-hours, has to be divided by a guess about how much everyone used it before it turns into a per-hour number. Every one of those guesses is an assumption, and the more assumptions a bar rests on, the less its tidy height means.

3 Claude

Claude is a large language model, a full-size version of the small one you built in Lab 2 & 3. It wrote your calculator's code the same way it writes a sentence, by predicting what comes next. That's why it can build a working page in a minute. It's also why it can drop in a number nobody gave it, the way a sentence can drift. Three instructions in your prompts did the guarding: use only my numbers, mark every assumption, and list anything not from my sources. That's the same move as Lab 4's "based only on this data." The sources section is what makes a confident-looking chart checkable.

4 The building

Every row in your calculator lands in the same kind of place: a building full of servers, drawing power from a local grid, cooled with air or water (section 2.1). The building's electric meter doesn't know whether a watt went to TikTok, a Netflix show, a cloud game, or a chatbot. Hold onto that. It's the whole reason for the next section.

At home: Sketch a rule for every data center

Don't want to use AI? Do this instead

Same 20 minutes, no chatbot, on your own. Read GeekWire's article on Washington's House Bill 2515 and Chapter 5, section 2.7. Then write the same four-part rule sketch described below, using only those two sources. Find one objection in the GeekWire article and write one sentence answering it.

AI data centers have been in the news as horrible for the environment. Your calculator says the bill is shared: AI, streaming, YouTube, TikTok, and gaming all run in the same kind of building. So if you were writing an environmental rule, what would a rule for data centers as a whole actually look like? About 20 minutes.

You have three real models to work from:

  • The European Union requires every data center drawing 500 kilowatts or more to report its energy use and other sustainability numbers to a central database, whatever runs inside (Chapter 5, section 2.7).
  • New Jersey signed a law in August 2026 requiring data centers to report energy and water use to state regulators every six months (Chapter 5, section 2.5).
  • Washington, where WWU is, debated House Bill 2515 in 2026. It would have covered facilities using 20 megawatts or more and required yearly reports on energy, water, refrigerants, and air pollution. It passed the House in a scaled-down version and died without a Senate vote. The industry's trade group argued it unfairly singled out data centers, when semiconductor and battery plants use similar amounts of power.

The takeaway

A rule for every data center is not a fantasy. Europe already has one for reporting, and New Jersey just passed one. The hard parts are the cutoff, which decides who's in, and the fight, which Washington's bill lost this year. And notice which argument killed it: that it singled out one industry. That's the same argument this lab has been building, aimed the other way. The cost of a broad rule is real, too: paperwork for companies that never touched AI, and a bigger coalition against you.

At home: Debate

Should environmental rules for data centers target AI, or every data center? Your side is assigned, not chosen: if your birthday is January through June, you're Side A. July through December, you're Side B. Nobody here is defending their own belief. The Gilman Street rules apply.

The headlines say AI data centers are the problem. Your calculator says the building is shared. Section 5's rule sketch had to pick a cutoff. Today you argue for one answer and then switch, the way a legislator would have to before voting in Olympia.

Side A · birthdays January to June

Target AI first

AI is what's driving the growth, AI chips draw several times the power of ordinary servers, and one AI request can cost thousands of times another. Write rules for AI data centers and AI products first, and get them passed.

The cost to name: streaming, gaming, cloud storage, and crypto mining go uncounted, and every company gets a reason to argue its workload isn't really AI.

Side B · birthdays July to December

Every data center

The meter can't tell TikTok from a chatbot. Your calculator puts everyday habits in the same building as AI. Regulate the building, whatever runs inside, the way Europe already does.

The cost to name: paperwork for every tech company, including ones that never touched AI, a bigger fight to pass it, and a rule that's less precise about where the fastest growth is.

Your evidence: four pieces, use all four

Every side gets every card. If a card seems to help the other side, that's your job: find what it means for yours, and what your side would rather nobody noticed.

Evidence 1 · The growth is AI

In one line: data centers are growing fast, and AI is the main reason.

  • The International Energy Agency counted about 415 terawatt-hours for data centers worldwide in 2024 and expects about 945 by 2030, with AI the main driver.
  • Berkeley Lab projects that by 2028 more than half the electricity going to US data centers will be used for AI (MIT Technology Review).
  • AI chips need two to four times as many watts as ordinary ones (Pew).

Articles B and D; Chapter 5, section 2.2

Evidence 4 · The rulebooks

In one line: broad rules exist, and broad rules also lose.

  • The EU requires every data center of 500 kilowatts or more to report energy use, whatever runs inside.
  • New Jersey's August 2026 law requires data centers, not just AI companies, to report energy and water use.
  • Washington's House Bill 2515 (2026) would have covered facilities of 20 megawatts or more. It passed the House scaled down and died in the Senate.
  • The industry's trade group said the Washington bill unfairly singled out data centers when semiconductor and battery plants use similar power.

How the debate runs at home

About 20 minutes, in writing.

  1. Get your side (1 min). Birthday January to June, Side A. July to December, Side B.
  2. Research (10 min). Your four cards, the prompts, then your notes in your own words.
  3. Opening (4 min). Write what you'd say in 45 seconds: your claim in one sentence, your evidence, and the cost of your side's plan.
  4. Switch (3 min). Write the other side's best point in one sentence, as strongly as they'd say it.
  5. What would change your mind? (2 min). Name the specific thing you'd have to see. Real evidence that would move you wins, whichever side you were handed.

To resolve it: your section 5 rule had to pick a cutoff. Name the cutoff you'd use now, what it catches that an AI-only rule misses, and what it costs the companies inside it.
