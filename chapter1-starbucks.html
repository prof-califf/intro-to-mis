Chapter 1

The Value of Information

Anchor case: Starbucks

Week 2 · Monday (with Ch 2)Concepts + case in one session

Starbucks operates tens of thousands of stores, and remarkably few of them are in the wrong place. That is not intuition. Site selection at Starbucks runs on an information system that weighs traffic patterns, transit stops, demographics, daypart demand, and the performance of every existing store nearby, before a lease is ever signed. The company sells coffee. It competes on information.

01Information as an asset

A cup of coffee is worth a few dollars, once. Knowing where ten thousand people will want coffee at 7:40 a.m. for the next ten years is worth a lease, a build-out, and a decade of revenue, or, wrongly guessed, a decade of losses. The information is more valuable than the product, and unlike the product it can be reused: the same model that sites one store sites the next thousand.

That reusability is what makes information economically strange. It is not consumed by use, and it gets more valuable as it accumulates, every store’s results feed back into the model that sites the next one. Assets like that compound, which is why companies that learn faster than their competitors tend to stay ahead even when the competitors copy the visible product.

02When does information actually have value?

Not all of it does, and a working test matters more than a definition. Information has value only when it changes a decision, and its value equals the difference in outcomes between the decision made with it and without it. Three properties drive that value:

  • Accuracy, wrong information that changes a decision has negative value.
  • Timeliness, the perfect answer, delivered after the lease is signed, is worth nothing.
  • Relevance, data that cannot alter any choice you face is cost, however accurate.

Three levels of decisions

Information systems serve decisions, and decisions come in three levels. Operational decisions run the day: how many baristas tomorrow, when to reorder cups. They are frequent, structured, made by a rule you could write down, and therefore automatable. Strategic decisions shape years: enter a market, sign a ten-year lease. They are rare, unstructured, no rule fully captures them, and the system’s job is to inform the human, not replace her. Managerial decisions sit between: allocate this quarter’s budget, staff this region. Watch the pattern at Starbucks: Deep Brew automates the structured operational layer and supports the unstructured strategic one. Matching the system to the decision level is a core design skill, automating an unstructured decision, or hand-working a structured one, are both expensive mistakes.

The test to carry around

Before building or buying any report, dashboard, or dataset, ask: what decision changes if this number moves? If nobody can answer, the information has an audience but no value. You will apply this test to Cascadia’s dashboards in Lab 4, and discover after the exam that a dashboard can pass the test and still miss something that matters.

03Where to put a store

Walk the decision. A candidate corner exists. The old way: a regional manager visits, counts foot traffic for an afternoon, checks the rent, and decides, one person’s sample, one afternoon, one career’s worth of instinct. The information-system way: years of anonymized mobility data showing how many people pass at each hour; transit and parking patterns; incomes and commutes for the surrounding blocks; cannibalization estimates from every existing store in the radius; and the performance of the hundred most similar corners the company already operates. The manager still decides, but the decision is now a judgment on top of evidence rather than instead of it.

Note what the system did not do: it did not decide. This is decision support, and the distinction between systems that inform decisions and systems that make them runs through the whole course, all the way to the AI weeks.

04Deep Brew: information all the way down

Starbucks’s internal AI platform, Deep Brew, pushes the same logic into daily operations: how many baristas to schedule against tomorrow’s predicted demand, what inventory each store needs, what to suggest on the drive-thru screen given the weather and the hour, when an espresso machine’s telemetry says it is about to fail. Each of those was once a manager’s guess. Each is now a prediction feeding a human decision, the store manager can override, and the overrides themselves become training data.

Loyalty completes the loop. The rewards app converts anonymous cash customers into identified, history-attached ones, every order enriches a profile that makes the next prediction better. Remember this mechanism: a member number attached to a sale is the difference between a company that can see its customers and one that cannot. Your lab client this quarter is a co-op whose entire business model rests on it.

05What Deep Brew actually runs, and what it got wrong

It is easy to describe a company's technology in the language of its press releases. It is more useful to look at what shipped, what it cost, and what failed, so this section does that.

The scale problem

Starbucks operates north of 40,000 stores. At that size, a decision that improves a single store by a trivial amount is worth a great deal in aggregate, and a decision that degrades a single store by a trivial amount is a disaster. This is why the company builds prediction into ordinary operations: forecasting hourly demand from historical sales, staffing, expected foot traffic from the app, and local weather, then feeding those forecasts into scheduling, inventory, and equipment maintenance. The espresso machines themselves report their condition, so a failing component can generate a work order before a barista notices anything wrong.

Note where the money goes. The company has publicly discussed annual spending in the hundreds of millions of dollars on enterprise software and infrastructure from outside vendors. When you read in Chapter 6 that Nike spent $400 million on a supply chain system, that figure should no longer sound exotic. It is the going rate for running a large company's nervous system.

The failure worth studying: counting milk

In September 2025, Starbucks deployed an artificial intelligence inventory tool from a vendor called NomadGo across roughly 11,000 stores. The promise was specific and attractive: computer vision counting inventory with about 99% accuracy, up to eight times faster than a person with a clipboard. Counting stock is exactly the kind of repetitive, structured task that automation should own.

In May 2026, Reuters reported that Starbucks had retired the program. The system confused similar milk varieties, missed products sitting on shelves, and in practice slowed employees down rather than speeding them up. Baristas went back to counting by hand.

Read this against the five components

The hardware worked. The software ran. The network delivered. The technology was not obviously broken in any way an engineer would flag. It failed at the People component, in the gap between a demo that counts neatly stacked cartons and a real back room where milk varieties look alike and a barista is already behind. A tool that makes a task slower will be abandoned no matter how impressive the model behind it is.

Keep this in mind whenever you read a company described as a technology leader. Starbucks genuinely is one, and it still shipped an expensive failure to 11,000 locations before finding out. The pattern to notice is not that they failed. It is that the failure surfaced only after deployment, because nothing in the rollout tested the thing that actually mattered: whether a person mid-shift could go faster with it than without it.

06The case on the five components

The Five Components · Starbucks
Hardware

Registers, espresso machines streaming telemetry, phones running the app.

Software

Deep Brew’s models for siting, scheduling, inventory, and offers.

Data

Transactions, mobility patterns, loyalty profiles, machine telemetry.

Networks

Tens of thousands of stores streaming into one shared brain, and predictions flowing back.

People

Managers retain override; their corrections retrain the system.

Strong across all five, which is why it serves as the quarter's reference case. But note the People component: the same company shipped an inventory tool to 11,000 stores that baristas could not use, and had to withdraw it. Being good at this is not the same as being safe from it.

07Vocabulary

Value of information
The improvement in decision outcomes attributable to having it.
Decision support
Systems that inform a human decision rather than make it.
Accuracy / timeliness / relevance
The three properties that determine whether information can carry value.
Feedback loop
Outcomes re-entering the system as data, improving future predictions.
Customer identification
Attaching transactions to a known individual (loyalty, membership), enabling history and prediction.
Structured vs. unstructured decisions
Rule-capturable choices vs. judgment calls; the first automates, the second gets decision support.
Operational / managerial / strategic
The three decision levels, daily running, resource allocation, direction-setting.
Cannibalization
New capacity taking demand from your own existing capacity, invisible without shared data.

08Discussion questions

  1. Apply the “what decision changes?” test to Starbucks’s mobility data. Name the decision, and what deciding wrongly costs. Warm-up
  2. Wrong information has negative value. Construct a concrete siting example where bad data is worse than no data.
  3. Deep Brew predicts; managers decide. Pick one Deep Brew decision you would fully automate and one you never would, and defend the line you drew.
  4. The rewards app buys identification with discounts. What is Starbucks actually paying for, and is the customer’s side of that trade well priced? Hard
  5. The NomadGo inventory tool promised 99% accuracy and eight times the speed of a human, and was withdrawn after nine months. Design the test Starbucks should have run before deploying to 11,000 stores. Who is in it, how long does it run, and what result would have stopped the rollout? Hard
