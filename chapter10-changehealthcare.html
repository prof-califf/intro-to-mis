Chapter 10 · Cybersecurity

Cybersecurity: The Password That Stopped American Healthcare

In February 2024, one login without a second security step let attackers into Change Healthcare, the company that processes about a third of all US medical claims. Within hours, hospitals could not get paid and pharmacies could not confirm prescriptions. This chapter is about what security actually protects, why the damage is a business problem long before it is a technical one, and what people with business degrees actually do in this field.

01The day the claims stopped

Most Americans had never heard of Change Healthcare before February 2024. It is not a hospital or an insurer; it is the plumbing between them, a UnitedHealth Group subsidiary that processes roughly 15 billion healthcare transactions a year and touches about one in every three US patient records. When a doctor's office submits a claim or a pharmacy checks your coverage, that request very often passes through Change Healthcare.

On February 21, 2024, the company discovered ransomware on its systems and did the only thing it could: it took itself offline. The effect was immediate and national. Pharmacies could not verify coverage or process claims for a large share of the country. Hospitals and physician practices could not submit claims, which meant they could not get paid. An American Medical Association survey that spring found that 80 percent of physician practices lost revenue from unpaid claims, and many small practices, which run on thin cash reserves, faced a genuine liquidity crisis: real payroll for real staff, with the money that normally arrived from insurers simply not arriving.

The data theft, when its scale became clear, set a record: about 190 million people's protected health information, the largest healthcare breach in US history. But notice which harm came first and hit hardest. Before a single record was known to be misused, the attack had already frozen the cash flow of a large slice of American medicine. The business disruption was the disaster. The data breach was the aftermath. That ordering is the theme of this chapter.

Why this case, and not an older one

The famous breaches of the last decade, Target and Equifax, were about stolen data. This one is about a stopped business, which is where cybercrime has actually gone. Ransomware does not quietly copy your data and leave; it takes your operations hostage. Understanding that shift is the difference between thinking of security as an IT problem and thinking of it as a survival problem for the whole organization, which is exactly the shift a business degree is supposed to give you.

02What security actually protects

Key conceptThe CIA triad

Security protects three properties of information. Confidentiality: only authorized people can read it. Integrity: it cannot be altered without detection. Availability: the people who need it can reach it when they need it. Older breaches were mostly confidentiality failures, data read by the wrong people. Ransomware is primarily an availability attack: it does not read your data so much as lock it away from you, then charge for the key. Change Healthcare was both at once, availability first (systems down, claims frozen) and confidentiality second (190 million records taken). When you analyze any incident, name which of the three broke, because the defense and the damage differ completely for each.

The triad makes a deeper point: security is a property of information and operations, not of computers. A hospital whose billing system is encrypted by an attacker has lost availability just as completely as if the building had flooded. This is why security stopped being purely an IT topic and became a boardroom one, and why the people who manage it increasingly come from business, not only from engineering.

03The missing control

Here is how the largest healthcare breach in US history began. Attackers logged into a Change Healthcare remote-access portal, a Citrix system, using a stolen username and password. That portal was not protected by multi-factor authentication. There was no second step, no code from a phone, no confirmation prompt. A password was the whole lock.

Key conceptMulti-factor authentication (MFA)

MFA requires more than one kind of proof to log in: something you know (a password), plus something you have (a phone or hardware key) or something you are (a fingerprint). Its entire purpose is to make a stolen password insufficient by itself, and stolen passwords are cheap and everywhere, harvested by the millions and sold on criminal markets. MFA is not exotic, expensive, or new. It is the single highest-value control most organizations can deploy, and its absence on one internet-facing portal is the root cause the company's own CEO named in sworn testimony to Congress.

The attackers were then inside for about nine days before anyone noticed, moving through the network and copying out roughly six terabytes of data before deploying the ransomware that finally made their presence impossible to miss. Two failures, in sequence: a missing lock at the door, and nine days of movement inside that nobody detected. Hold that shape; it is the kill chain, and it is the next section.

04Ransomware as a business

Key conceptRansomware

Ransomware is malicious software that encrypts an organization's data and demands payment for the key. Modern ransomware adds a second threat, called double extortion: the attackers also copy the data out first, so even a victim with perfect backups faces the choice of paying to prevent the data being published. The crucial thing for a business student to understand is that ransomware is an industry, not a prank. Groups operate as ransomware-as-a-service: core operators build the malware and rent it to affiliates who carry out attacks and split the proceeds. It has revenue models, customer support, and, as Change Healthcare learned, dishonest partners.

The aftermath reads like a case study in why paying does not buy safety. Change Healthcare, through a UnitedHealth subsidiary, paid the ALPHV/BlackCat group about $22 million for a promise to delete the stolen data. The core operators then took the entire payment and vanished, an exit scam, without paying the affiliate who had actually done the hacking. That affiliate still had a copy of the data, took it to a second ransomware group, RansomHub, and demanded a fresh ransom. The company had paid $22 million and protected nothing. This is the practical argument, not the moral one, against assuming a ransom payment solves the problem: you are trusting criminals to keep a promise, and the incentive structure among them is not built to protect you.

05The business damage, not the data

Corporate reporting put UnitedHealth's direct costs above $2.8 billion. That number, large as it is, is the smallest part of the harm, because it is only the part that lands on the company big enough to absorb it. Trace the damage outward and it is almost entirely a business-operations story:

Provider cash flow. Thousands of practices and hospitals could not bill, so revenue stopped while payroll and rent did not. UnitedHealth advanced billions in emergency funding to providers precisely because the cash-flow damage threatened to close practices before any data was ever misused. A clinic does not need its patient records leaked to fail; it just needs to miss payroll twice.

Care itself. Prior authorizations, prescription approvals, and eligibility checks run through this plumbing. When it stopped, patients faced delays getting medications and treatments approved. The availability failure reached from a server all the way to a pharmacy counter.

Concentration risk, revealed. The reason one company's outage could do national damage is that the industry had quietly routed a third of its transactions through a single point. Nobody chose that risk deliberately; it accumulated through decades of consolidation. The attack turned an invisible structural fact into a visible one, and "we depend on one vendor for something we cannot operate without" is now a risk that boards across every industry are re-examining.

Key conceptConcentration risk and business continuity

Concentration risk is the danger created when too much of something critical depends on a single point: one vendor, one data center, one system. Business continuity planning is the discipline of preparing to keep operating when that point fails: backups, alternate vendors, manual fallback procedures, tested and ready before the crisis. The Change Healthcare outage was so damaging partly because so few providers had a plan B for a service they had assumed would always be there. Asking "what do we do the day this critical thing is gone?" is business continuity, and it is a business planning question, not a technical one.

06How defense actually works

Key conceptThe kill chain, and breaking it

Analysts describe intrusions as a kill chain: an ordered sequence of stages an attacker must complete, from initial entry, to moving deeper (called lateral movement), to stealing or encrypting the data. The model's value is that the attacker must succeed at every stage while the defender only has to break one, so security stacks controls in layers, defense in depth, assuming each can fail. Two design rules do most of the work. Least privilege: every account gets the minimum access its job needs, so a stolen credential is worth as little as possible. Network segmentation: systems are fenced into zones, so entry into one is not entry into all. Change Healthcare's attackers had nine days of lateral movement, which is a segmentation failure sitting behind the MFA failure. Break either link and the record breach does not happen.

Run the whole chain against a single question: at how many points could this have been stopped cheaply? MFA on one portal. Segmentation to contain the movement. Detection to catch nine days of an intruder. Each is ordinary, none is exotic, and any one of them breaks the chain. Nearly every breach you will read about is a failure to apply known controls, not the work of unstoppable genius. That is oddly good news: it means most security is a management problem, and management is learnable.

07What business people actually do in security

Students often assume cybersecurity is a coding job they are unqualified for. Some of it is. But the field is short hundreds of thousands of workers in the US alone, and a large fraction of the open roles are business roles, because most of what determines whether an organization gets breached is decisions, not code. A few of the jobs a business graduate actually does:

Governance, risk, and compliance (GRC). Deciding which risks the organization will accept, reduce, or insure against, and proving to regulators and auditors that required controls exist. This is spreadsheets, policy, and judgment, not exploits, and it is one of the largest and best-paid corners of the field.

Third-party and vendor risk management. Change Healthcare was a vendor to the entire healthcare system; the Target breach a decade earlier came through an HVAC contractor. Someone has to assess whether the companies you depend on are secure, and that someone reads contracts and audit reports, not memory dumps.

Security awareness and training. Because phishing starts most breaches, the people who design how a workforce is trained and tested are doing frontline security work with no code at all.

Incident response coordination and business continuity. When an incident hits, someone has to run the response as a business operation: who is told, who decides, when regulators and customers are notified, how the company keeps functioning. That coordinator is often a manager, not an engineer.

The CISO. The Chief Information Security Officer sits on or near the executive team and answers to the board. The job is overwhelmingly about communicating risk in business terms, setting priorities against a budget, and building a culture, which is why a growing number of CISOs come from management backgrounds. Andrew Witty, the executive who testified to Congress about this breach, is not an engineer; he is the CEO, and the breach was his problem.

The takeaway for this course: the most important security decisions at Change Healthcare, enforce MFA everywhere, segment the network, keep an acquired company's defenses current, were management decisions that went unmade. You do not need to write malware to prevent the next one. You need to understand the business well enough to insist on the boring controls before the crisis, which is precisely the vantage point an MIS degree provides.

08The other side: who absorbs a healthcare breach

The $2.8 billion UnitedHealth booked is the cost that could be absorbed. Follow the rest of it outward and it lands on people with far less capacity to carry it.

Small medical practices, some of which took emergency loans or closed, because a company they had never chosen to depend on went dark. Patients who waited for medications while an eligibility check sat in a frozen queue. And 190 million people whose Social Security numbers, diagnoses, and treatment records are now somewhere they cannot retrieve, carrying a lifetime risk of identity theft and medical fraud that no credit-monitoring offer erases. Health data does not expire the way a credit card does; you cannot reissue a diagnosis.

Keep this ledger for Wednesday's governance discussion. When a company decides how much to spend on security, much of the downside of underspending is exported, to patients, to small providers, to the public. Economists call that an externality, and it is the core reason security cannot be left entirely to each firm's private cost-benefit math. The company weighs its $2.8 billion; it does not weigh the closed clinic or the stolen medical history, because those never appear on its books. Part of the job of governance, and of regulation, is to represent the people who are not in the room when the security budget is set.

You lead Cascadia Outfitters. Ransomware has encrypted every system: point of sale, inventory, member records, payroll. The stores are effectively closed. The attackers want a payment to unlock everything, and they claim to have copied your member data and will publish it unless you pay. Your backups are three weeks old. You know the Change Healthcare story: a $22 million payment that protected nothing.

Do you pay? Commit to yes or no before you discuss, and name the one fact that would flip your answer.

Think 3 min

Decide, and write the single fact that would change your mind. If nothing would, say why you are that certain.

Pair 5 min

Find someone who chose the opposite. Trade the facts that would flip you. Are they the same fact?

Share 12 min

We will separate the operational question (can we recover without paying?) from the data question (will paying stop the leak?), because they have different answers.

Where this landsPaying is two decisions wearing one label. On operations, good tested backups can make payment unnecessary, which is why prevention and backups are the real answer and the decision is really made months earlier. On the stolen data, the Change Healthcare exit scam is the whole lesson: paying buys a criminal's promise, and the incentives among criminals do not protect you. The point students should leave with is that "do we pay?" is a bad position to be in, and the work that matters, MFA, segmentation, backups, continuity planning, happens long before the screen locks.
