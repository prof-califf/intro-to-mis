Chapter 10 · Cybersecurity

Cybersecurity: The Missing Second Step That Stopped American Healthcare

In February 2024 one login without a second security step let attackers into the company that processes about a third of all United States medical claims. Within hours, hospitals could not get paid and pharmacies could not confirm prescriptions. This chapter is about why the damage was a business problem long before it was a data problem.

Most Americans had never heard of Change Healthcare before February 2024. It is not a hospital or an insurer. It is the plumbing between them, and when it went offline, a third of the country's medical claims stopped moving.

How this chapter runs

Section 03 puts them to work on three attacks that arrived through something the victim did not consider part of its own system. Section 04 is the full case. Section 05 asks who absorbs the damage, and describes the security jobs a business graduate actually does, because most of them are not coding jobs.

Learning objectives

  2. Describe ransomware and double extortion, and explain why paying does not solve the problem.
  3. Read an attack as a kill chain and name the ordinary controls (MFA, least privilege, segmentation, backups) that break it.

01What security actually protects

Every chapter so far has assumed the system works. This one is about what happens when somebody deliberately stops it, and the first thing to fix is the idea that security is something that happens to computers.

Security is a property of all five components at once, and the weakest one decides the outcome. Hardware can be seized or encrypted. Software carries the flaw that lets someone in. Data is what gets copied out. Networks are how an intruder moves from the one machine they reached to the ones they wanted. And people are, in almost every incident you will read about, both the way in and the only reason anyone notices. The chapter's case turns on a missing lock, nine days of unnoticed movement, and a decision nobody had made about how much of a country's medical billing should run through one building. Not one of those is a coding problem.

Key conceptThe CIA triad

Security protects three properties of information. Confidentiality: only authorized people can read it. Integrity: it cannot be altered without detection. Availability: the people who need it can reach it when they need it. Older breaches were mostly confidentiality failures, data read by the wrong people. Ransomware is primarily an availability attack: it does not read your data so much as lock it away from you, then charge for the key. Change Healthcare was both at once, availability first (systems down, claims frozen) and confidentiality second (193 million people's records taken). When you analyze any incident, name which of the three broke, because the defense and the damage differ completely for each.

The triad makes a deeper point: security is a property of information and operations, not of computers. A hospital whose billing system is encrypted by an attacker has lost availability just as completely as if the building had flooded. This is why security stopped being purely an IT topic and became a boardroom one, and why the people who manage it increasingly come from business, not only from engineering.

Ransomware as a business

Key conceptRansomware

Ransomware is malicious software that encrypts an organization's data and demands payment for the key. Modern ransomware adds a second threat, called double extortion. The attackers copy the data out first, so even a victim with perfect backups faces the choice of paying to keep the data off the internet. The crucial thing for a business student to understand is that ransomware is an industry, not a prank. Groups operate as ransomware-as-a-service: core operators build the malware and rent it to affiliates who carry out attacks and split the proceeds. It has revenue models, customer support, and, as Change Healthcare learned, dishonest partners.

The aftermath reads like a case study in why paying does not buy safety. Change Healthcare, through a UnitedHealth subsidiary, paid the ALPHV/BlackCat group about $22 million for a promise to delete the stolen data. The core operators then took the entire payment and vanished, an exit scam, without paying the affiliate who had actually done the hacking. That affiliate still had a copy of the data, took it to a second ransomware group, RansomHub, and demanded a fresh ransom. The company had paid $22 million and protected nothing. This is the practical argument, not the moral one, against assuming a ransom payment solves the problem. You are trusting criminals to keep a promise, and nothing in their incentives protects you.

02How attacks work, and how defense works

Attacks are not single events. They are sequences, and that is the good news, because a sequence can be broken at any point. This section gives you the model analysts use to describe attackers and the handful of ordinary controls that interrupt it, then applies both to the door the case's attackers walked through.

Key conceptThe kill chain, and breaking it

Analysts describe intrusions as a kill chain: an ordered sequence of stages an attacker must complete. It runs from initial entry, to moving deeper (called lateral movement), to stealing or encrypting the data. (You do not need to memorize the stages. You need to see that there are stages, because each one is a place to stop the attack.) The model's value is that the attacker must succeed at every stage while the defender only has to break one, so security stacks controls in layers, defense in depth, assuming each can fail. Two design rules do most of the work. Least privilege: every account gets the minimum access its job needs, so a stolen credential is worth as little as possible. Network segmentation: systems are fenced into zones, so entry into one is not entry into all. Change Healthcare's attackers had nine days of lateral movement, which is a segmentation failure sitting behind the MFA failure. Break either link and the record breach does not happen.

Run the whole chain against a single question: at how many points could this have been stopped cheaply? MFA on one portal. Segmentation to contain the movement. Detection to catch nine days of an intruder. Each is ordinary, none is exotic, and any one of them breaks the chain. Nearly every breach you will read about is a failure to apply known controls, not the work of unstoppable genius. That is oddly good news: it means most security is a management problem, and management is learnable.

The missing control

Here is how the largest healthcare breach in US history began. Attackers logged into a Change Healthcare remote-access portal, a Citrix system, using a stolen username and password. That portal was not protected by multi-factor authentication. There was no second step, no code from a phone, no confirmation prompt. A password was the whole lock.

Key conceptMulti-factor authentication (MFA)

MFA requires more than one kind of proof to log in: something you know (a password), plus something you have (a phone or hardware key) or something you are (a fingerprint). Its entire purpose is to make a stolen password insufficient by itself, and stolen passwords are cheap and everywhere, harvested by the millions and sold on criminal markets. MFA is not exotic, expensive, or new. It is the single highest-value control most organizations can deploy, and its absence on one internet-facing portal is the root cause the company's own CEO named in sworn testimony to Congress.

The attackers were then inside for about nine days before anyone noticed. They moved through the network and copied out roughly six terabytes of data, then deployed the ransomware that finally made their presence impossible to miss. Two failures, in sequence: a missing lock at the door, and nine days of movement inside that nobody detected. Hold that shape; it is the kill chain, and it is the next section.

03Three attacks that came in sideways

Before the case, three short ones, spread across thirty-five years. Each one entered through something the victim did not think of as part of its own system: the network itself, a supplier's software update, and a file-transfer tool bought by somebody else. Read them with one question in mind, because it is the question section 05 will make national: what did the organization actually depend on that it had never listed anywhere?

Mini-case 1 · 1988: the program that was only supposed to count

In November 1988 a graduate student at Cornell released a program onto the internet, which then connected a few tens of thousands of machines belonging mostly to universities, research labs and the military. His stated intention was to measure how big the network was. To spread, the program used known weaknesses in common software, and it included a mechanism meant to stop it copying itself onto a machine that already had it.

That mechanism was wrong. The program reinfected machines repeatedly, and each copy consumed resources until the machine became unusable. Estimates at the time put the number of affected computers around six thousand, roughly a tenth of the internet as it then existed. Nothing was stolen and nothing was read. Machines simply stopped being able to do their work, and administrators disconnected from the network to save themselves, which broke the network further.

Two things came out of it. Its author became the first person convicted under the United States Computer Fraud and Abuse Act. And the incident led directly to the creation of the first coordinated computer emergency response team. It had become obvious that nobody was on the other end of the phone when something like this happened.

Where it came in: the network connection itself. The component: networks. Note what kind of failure this was, because it is the one students expect least. No confidentiality was lost. The harm was availability, and it arrived through the thing everybody had assumed was simply a convenience.

Check yourselfNothing was stolen in 1988 and the damage was still severe. Using the three properties in section 01, explain what an organization loses when a system is merely unavailable, and name one system where a day of unavailability would hurt you more than a leak would.

Mini-case 2 · 2017: the shipping line taken down by Ukrainian tax software

Maersk is the world's largest container shipping company. On 27 June 2017 its staff in Copenhagen watched screens go dark across the building, and within hours the company's network was gone. Operations at seventy-six port terminals were disrupted. For a period, one of the largest logistics operations on earth ran on phone calls and messaging apps.

The malware, later called NotPetya, had not been aimed at Maersk. It had been distributed through a compromised update to an accounting package widely used by companies filing tax in Ukraine. Maersk had an office there. That was the entire connection. Once inside, it spread across the global network and destroyed the domain controllers, the servers that hold the keys to who may log in to what. Without them, rebuilding was close to impossible, because there was no authoritative record of the company's own identity system.

Then the detail that makes this case unforgettable. A domain controller in Maersk's Ghana office had been offline during the attack, because of a local power cut. It held one clean copy. Getting it to the recovery team meant a staff member flying to Nigeria to hand a hard drive to a colleague. The internet connection in Ghana was too slow to send it, and nobody in the Ghana office had a British visa. Maersk reinstalled roughly four thousand servers and forty-five thousand computers in about ten days, and put the cost at around $300 million.

Where it came in: somebody else's software update. The component: software. The survival, though, came down to data: one accidental offline backup, found by luck rather than by plan. Ask yourself which of those two facts a board should find more alarming.

Check yourselfMaersk recovered because of a power cut in Ghana. Describe what the company should have had in place that would have made that luck unnecessary, and say why organizations routinely do not have it despite knowing they should.

Mini-case 3 · 2023: one flaw in a tool most victims had never bought

In the summer of 2023, employees at British Airways and the BBC learned their personal data had been stolen from a piece of software neither company had ever bought. MOVEit Transfer is a product for moving files securely between organizations. It is unglamorous software, used by around 1,700 organizations to shift payroll files, claims data and records between one company and another.

In mid-2023 a criminal group exploited flaws in it. The pattern of the damage is the part worth studying. A UK payroll provider used MOVEit, and its clients included British Airways, the BBC and others. Those companies had not bought MOVEit and in many cases had never heard of it. Their employees' data was exposed anyway, because it had passed through a supplier's supplier. Across the whole event, thousands of organizations and tens of millions of individuals were affected.

For most of the victims there was no negligence to point at in their own house. They had a vendor they had assessed, and that vendor had a vendor. The exposure lived two steps away from anything they controlled or could see.

Where it came in: a vendor's vendor. The component: people. The failure was not technical: it was that almost nobody maintained a list of which outside organizations touched their data, or of the organizations those organizations relied on. Section 05 names the job that exists to fix this, and it is a job a business graduate does.

Check yourselfName three outside companies that hold data about you through an organization you belong to rather than through you directly. Now say how you would find out who their suppliers are, and what that tells you about how hard this job is.

What the three have in common

A network connection, a foreign tax package, and a file-transfer tool nobody remembers buying. In none of the three did the attack arrive through the front door the organization was defending, and in none of the three would a stronger password on the main system have helped.

What they share is a boundary problem. Every one of these organizations had a mental picture of what its system was, and in every case the real system was larger than the picture. The internet was part of Cornell's system. A Ukrainian accounting package was part of Maersk's. A supplier's supplier was part of the BBC's.

Hold that as you read the case, because Change Healthcare is the same problem seen from the other end. It was not the victim that had failed to map its dependencies. It was the dependency, for a third of American healthcare, and almost nobody using it had written that down either.

04Change Healthcare: the day the claims stopped

Start at a pharmacy counter on the morning of 21 February 2024. The prescription is filled and sitting in the bag. The pharmacist scans it, waits for the insurer to confirm coverage, and the screen does nothing. Across the country the same screen is doing nothing in tens of thousands of clinics, hospitals, and pharmacies, and almost none of the people staring at it have heard of the company that just went dark. Change Healthcare is not a hospital or an insurer. It is the plumbing between them: a UnitedHealth Group subsidiary that processes roughly 15 billion healthcare transactions a year and touches about one in every three US patient records. When a doctor's office submits a claim or a pharmacy checks your coverage, that request very often passes through Change Healthcare.

On February 21, 2024, the company discovered ransomware on its systems and did the only thing it could: it took itself offline. The effect was immediate and national. Pharmacies could not verify coverage or process claims for a large share of the country. Hospitals and physician practices could not submit claims, which meant they could not get paid. An American Medical Association survey that spring found that 80 percent of physician practices lost revenue from unpaid claims. Many small practices, which run on thin cash reserves, faced a real cash crisis: payroll for real staff, with the money that normally arrived from insurers simply not arriving.

The data theft, when its scale became clear, set a record: about 193 million people's protected health information by the final count in July 2025, the largest healthcare breach in US history. But notice which harm came first and hit hardest. Before a single record was known to be misused, the attack had already frozen the cash flow of a large slice of American medicine. The business disruption was the disaster. The data breach was the aftermath. That ordering is the theme of this chapter.

Why this case, and not an older one

The famous breaches of the last decade, Target and Equifax, were about stolen data. This one is about a stopped business, which is where cybercrime has actually gone. Ransomware does not quietly copy your data and leave; it takes your operations hostage. Understanding that shift is the difference between thinking of security as an IT problem and thinking of it as a survival problem for the whole organization. That is exactly the shift a business degree is supposed to give you.

The business damage, not the data

UnitedHealth's own reporting put its direct costs for 2024 at about $3.1 billion. That number, large as it is, is the smallest part of the harm, because it is only the part that falls on the company big enough to absorb it. Trace the damage outward and it is almost entirely a business-operations story:

Provider cash flow. Thousands of practices and hospitals could not bill, so revenue stopped while payroll and rent did not. UnitedHealth advanced billions in emergency funding to providers precisely because the cash-flow damage threatened to close practices before any data was ever misused. A clinic does not need its patient records leaked to fail; it just needs to miss payroll twice.

Care itself. Prior authorizations, prescription approvals, and eligibility checks run through this plumbing. When it stopped, patients faced delays getting medications and treatments approved. The availability failure reached from a server all the way to a pharmacy counter.

Concentration risk, revealed. The reason one company's outage could do national damage is that the industry had quietly routed a third of its transactions through a single point. Nobody chose that risk deliberately; it accumulated through decades of consolidation. The attack turned an invisible structural fact into a visible one. "We depend on one vendor for something we cannot operate without" is now a risk that boards across every industry are re-examining.

Key conceptConcentration risk and business continuity

Concentration risk is the danger created when too much of something critical depends on a single point: one vendor, one data center, one system. Business continuity planning is the discipline of preparing to keep operating when that point fails: backups, alternate vendors, manual fallback procedures, tested and ready before the crisis. The Change Healthcare outage was so damaging partly because so few providers had a plan B for a service they had assumed would always be there. Asking "what do we do the day this critical thing is gone?" is business continuity, and it is a business planning question, not a technical one.

05Who absorbs a breach, and who does this work

The $3.1 billion UnitedHealth booked is the cost that could be absorbed. Follow the rest of it outward and it falls on people with far less capacity to carry it.

Small medical practices, some of which took emergency loans or closed, because a company they had never chosen to depend on went dark. Patients who waited for medications while an eligibility check sat in a frozen queue. And 193 million people whose Social Security numbers, diagnoses, and treatment records are now somewhere they cannot retrieve, carrying a lifetime risk of identity theft and medical fraud that no credit-monitoring offer erases. Health data does not expire the way a credit card does; you cannot reissue a diagnosis.

Keep this ledger for Wednesday's governance discussion. When a company decides how much to spend on security, much of the downside of underspending is exported, to patients, to small providers, to the public. Economists call that an externality, and it is the core reason security cannot be left entirely to each firm's private cost-benefit math. The company weighs its $3.1 billion; it does not weigh the closed clinic or the stolen medical history, because those never appear on its books. Part of the job of governance, and of regulation, is to represent the people who are not in the room when the security budget is set.

What business people actually do in security

Students often assume cybersecurity is a coding job they are unqualified for. Some of it is. But the field is short hundreds of thousands of workers in the US alone, and a large fraction of the open roles are business roles. Most of what determines whether an organization gets breached is decisions, not code. A few of the jobs a business graduate actually does:

Governance, risk, and compliance (GRC). Deciding which risks the organization will accept, reduce, or insure against, and proving to regulators and auditors that required controls exist. This is spreadsheets, policy, and judgment, not exploits, and it is one of the largest and best-paid corners of the field.

Third-party and vendor risk management. Change Healthcare was a vendor to the entire healthcare system; the Target breach a decade earlier came through an HVAC contractor. Someone has to assess whether the companies you depend on are secure, and that someone reads contracts and audit reports, not memory dumps.

Security awareness and training. Because phishing starts most breaches, the people who design how a workforce is trained and tested are doing frontline security work with no code at all.

Incident response coordination and business continuity. When an incident hits, someone has to run the response as a business operation: who is told, who decides, when regulators and customers are notified, how the company keeps functioning. That coordinator is often a manager, not an engineer.

The CISO. The Chief Information Security Officer sits on or near the executive team and answers to the board. The job is overwhelmingly about communicating risk in business terms, setting priorities against a budget, and building a culture, which is why a growing number of CISOs come from management backgrounds. Andrew Witty, the executive who testified to Congress about this breach, is not an engineer; he was the chief executive, and the breach was his problem.

The takeaway for this course: the most important security decisions at Change Healthcare, enforce MFA everywhere, segment the network, keep an acquired company's defenses current, were management decisions that went unmade. You do not need to write malware to prevent the next one. You need to understand the business well enough to insist on the boring controls before the crisis, which is precisely the vantage point an MIS degree provides.

Key takeaways
  • A breach is an availability problem before it is a confidentiality problem. The first question is not what was stolen but what stopped.
  • Attacks are sequences, and a sequence can be broken at any point. Nearly every breach is a failure to apply a known, boring control.
  • One portal without multi-factor authentication was enough. Nine days inside, six terabytes out, and a third of American medicine's cash flow frozen.
  • "We depend on one vendor for something we cannot operate without" is a risk every board is now re-examining, and most of them could not have named the vendor before.
  • The company weighs its own cost. The patients, the small practices, and the 193 million people are not in the room, which is why security cannot be left to private cost-benefit math alone.

We will separate the operational question (can we recover without paying?) from the data question (will paying stop the leak?), because they have different answers.

Where this landsPaying is two decisions wearing one label. On operations, good tested backups can make payment unnecessary, which is why prevention and backups are the real answer and the decision is really made months earlier. On the stolen data, the Change Healthcare exit scam is the whole lesson: paying buys a criminal's promise, and the incentives among criminals do not protect you. The point students should leave with is that "do we pay?" is a bad position to be in, and the work that matters, MFA, segmentation, backups, continuity planning, happens long before the screen locks.

0:00OpenAsk what happens at a hospital on the morning it cannot bill a single insurance claim. Let the room work out the cash-flow chain before you name it. Most students have never pictured a hospital as a business with a payroll, and that realization is the chapter.
0:06LectureThe CIA triad, and the point that older breaches were confidentiality failures while ransomware is primarily an availability attack. Make them name which property broke in an incident from the news before you move on.
0:16LectureRansomware as an industry rather than a prank: the service model, double extortion, and the practical argument against paying, which is that you are trusting criminals to keep a promise. The exit scam in section 04 makes this concrete later.
0:24LectureThe kill chain on the board, stage by stage, pausing at each to ask what would have stopped it. Then least privilege and segmentation, and MFA as the single highest-value control most organizations can deploy. Land the good news: nearly every breach is a failure to apply known controls.
0:38Cases 1–3The 1988 worm, Maersk, MOVEit, about two and a half minutes each. Name where each one came in and stop. The Ghana detail will get a reaction; use it, then ask what should have made the luck unnecessary.
0:47LectureHand over to Chapter 11: security is a set of decisions rather than a set of tools, with Equifax as the case where the tools existed and the decisions failed. Concentration risk is the bridge from this chapter.
1:02Think-pair-sharePay the ransom? See the activity in section 06. Protect the silent minutes; the first instinct in the room is unanimous and the case complicates it.
1:16ClosePreview the lab: they play the security lead at Cascadia and make these calls themselves. Assign section 04 in both chapters.
Case session plan · 80 minutes · Change Healthcare, whenever you teach it
0:00OpenTwo numbers and nothing else: one in three US patient records, and one password. Ask what could possibly connect them. Let the room guess before you walk the case.
0:08Walk the caseThe portal without MFA, nine days of undetected movement, six terabytes out, then the ransomware. Keep naming the stage of the kill chain as each piece lands, and keep asking what a single control would have cost at that point.
0:22The damageOrder it deliberately: the claims stopped first, the data theft was found later. Walk the cash-flow chain out to small practices missing payroll, then to patients waiting on prior authorizations. The sentence to repeat is that the business disruption was the disaster and the breach was the aftermath.
0:36The ransomThe $22 million payment, the exit scam, the second demand. This is where the room's Monday instinct meets the evidence, so let them sit in it rather than resolving it for them.
0:46Concentration riskNobody chose to route a third of American healthcare through one company; it accumulated. Ask the room to name a single point their own employer or school depends on, and whether anyone has written it down.
0:54ThinkAll five discussion questions on screen. Silent individual work, one sentence on at least two.
1:00Pair and shareGroups settle on positions they can defend, then take the questions in front of the room. Circulate for a group that landed somewhere unusual on the ransom question and call on them first.
1:16Close and bridgeBack to the opening two numbers. Then the line to leave them with: the attackers did not need a new idea, they needed one door without a second lock. Bridge to Chapter 11, where a company had the tools and still failed.
