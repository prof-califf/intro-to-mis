A fashion trend appears on a runway or a street corner. At most clothing companies, a garment responding to it reaches stores six to nine months later: an eternity, and a guess. At Zara, it can hang on a rack in roughly two weeks. Same industry, same fabrics, same sewing machines. The difference is an information system, and it made Zara's parent company one of the most valuable retailers on earth.

01What competitive advantage actually is

Key conceptSustainable competitive advantage

An advantage is sustainable only when rivals cannot copy it at a price worth paying. Keep that test in hand for the whole chapter: at each step of the Zara case below, ask what a rival with unlimited money would still find hard.

Competitive advantage is the ability to create more value than rivals: by charging more for something customers prefer (differentiation), by delivering at lower cost (cost leadership), or by responding faster than the market can (speed, which is really both). The word that matters more than "advantage" is sustained. Anyone can win a quarter. The strategic question is what stops rivals from copying you by next year.

The standard test: an advantage endures only if the resource behind it is valuable, rare, and hard to imitate. Now apply the test to Zara. Sewing machines? Buyable. Point-of-sale software? Buyable. The specific combination: nearby flexible factories, designers embedded with planners, store managers trained and trusted as sensors, small-batch discipline, a logistics network tuned to twice-weekly rhythm: took decades to assemble and touches every part of the company. Competitors can see exactly what Zara does. H&M and Gap have studied it for twenty years. Copying it would require rebuilding themselves, which is precisely why the advantage persists.

The chapter's core idea

Technology you can buy cannot be an advantage, because your competitor's procurement department has the same catalog. Durable advantage lives in the system, the way data, software, networks, and people are woven together around a process rivals can't easily rewire themselves to run. You will test every remaining case in this course against that sentence.

Beyond running the business better, information systems change the competitive structure of an industry itself. Four mechanisms recur, and you should learn to name them on sight:

  • Switching costs, a system that accumulates your history, preferences, or data makes leaving expensive. Every playlist you build deepens the trench around your streaming account.
  • Network effects, a system that grows more valuable as more people use it. Each additional rider makes the ride-share map denser for everyone; latecomers face an emptier map.
  • Barriers to entry, when serving customers requires a system that took a decade and billions to build, the price of admission excludes newcomers.
  • Speed of learning, Zara's mechanism. If your loop from signal to shelf runs in two weeks and your rival's runs in six months, you run a dozen experiments in the time they run one. The advantage compounds, because faster learning produces better data produces faster learning.

Porter's Five Forces

The standard map of an industry's competitive pressure, and the framework you will be asked to apply in interviews and exams alike, is Michael Porter's five forces. An industry is squeezed by (1) rivalry among existing competitors, (2) the threat of new entrants, (3) the threat of substitutes, (4) the bargaining power of buyers, and (5) the bargaining power of suppliers. Profit drains toward whichever forces are strong; strategy is the work of weakening them in your favor, and information systems are one of the few tools that can move all five at once.

02Why most IT advantage evaporates

A note on being early. First-mover advantage is the edge that comes from doing something before rivals: learning curves, brand association, and customers locked in by switching costs. It is real and it is routinely oversold, because being first also means paying to educate the market and making the mistakes later entrants get to skip. Zara’s RFID rollout, completed in 2016, was not especially early; the advantage came from what the company attached the tags to. Being first matters far less than being first to build something others find expensive to copy.

Key conceptCommoditization

When a capability becomes purchasable by anyone, it stops differentiating anyone. This is commoditization, and it is the engine of Carr’s 2003 argument that IT no longer matters strategically. The RFID tags Zara uses are a commodity. The twice-weekly delivery rhythm, the store-manager reorder authority, and the Arteixo logistics complex are not, because they are organizational commitments compounded over decades.

History is littered with technology advantages that died. American Airlines' SABRE reservation system was such a weapon in the 1970s and 80s that regulators eventually forced changes to how it displayed rivals' flights. The first banks with ATMs won customers, until every bank had ATMs and the machines became a cost of doing business, not an edge. In 2003, Nicholas Carr scandalized the industry with an essay titled "IT Doesn't Matter," arguing that as technology becomes cheap and ubiquitous it becomes like electricity: essential, and strategically irrelevant, because everyone has it.

Carr was right about a category and wrong about a boundary. He was right that commodity technology, the buyable kind: confers no advantage and quickly becomes table stakes: the ante required to play at all, worth nothing extra once paid. Online ordering was an edge for Domino's in 2008; today a restaurant without it simply loses. But the boundary matters: what commoditizes is the components. What resists commoditization is the system, the data only you have accumulated, the loop only your organization runs, the people trained to act on the signal. Nobody can buy Zara's twenty years of what-sold-yesterday, or the organizational reflexes built around it.

The trap to watch for all quarter

When executives say "we're investing in technology to gain an advantage," ask which side of Carr's boundary the investment lands on. Buying what everyone can buy is defense: necessary, and worth zero edge. In Week 7 you will watch Nike spend $400 million on buyable software and lose $100 million, precisely because the un-buyable parts (clean data, fitted process, trained people) were treated as afterthoughts.

03Zara: the two-week dress

The loop

Here is the loop, and notice how ordinary each piece is. Every evening, Zara store managers report what sold, what stalled, and critically, what customers asked for and didn't find. Point-of-sale data flows to headquarters in Arteixo, Spain, alongside those human observations. Designers sitting next to production planners turn the signal into new designs within days. Factories, many nearby rather than across an ocean, produce in deliberately small batches. Twice-weekly deliveries put the result in stores, where scarcity is a feature: if you don't buy it now, it may never come back. Then the loop runs again. Thousands of new designs a year, each one a small, fast, cheap bet informed by last week's evidence.

Compare the traditional model it beat: forecast a season's demand many months out, commit to huge orders from distant factories, then discount whatever the forecast got wrong. The traditional model bets big on a guess. Zara's system bets small on data, thousands of times, and lets the customers vote.

Zara doesn't predict fashion better than its rivals. It built a system that makes prediction less necessary.

"Zara is fast" is a slogan. Here is how it actually works, because the specifics are what make the advantage hard to copy.

Every garment is a tracked object

Zara completed a rollout of RFID across its stores in 2016, with the chip embedded in the garment's security tag. A radio-frequency tag lets a handheld reader identify every item in a room in seconds, without anyone reading a label. The practical effect is that headquarters knows, close to live, what is on which shelf in which store on which continent, and a staff member can find out in a minute that a size is sold out on the floor but sitting in the back room. Inventory counts that used to take a full night now take a fraction of it.

Sit with what that enables, because it is the difference between the story and the system. The famous nightly report from store managers is not a manager guessing. It is a manager adding human observation, what customers asked for and could not find, on top of a precise machine record of what moved. Judgment where judgment is needed, counting where counting is needed.

One warehouse, twice a week, everywhere on earth

Inditex runs a centralized logistics operation in Arteixo, in Galicia, with the Zara hub covering millions of square feet and running close to around the clock, nearly every day of the year. Merchandise is routed through Spain regardless of where it is going. Shipments reach stores about twice a week. The company's stated target: any store in the world can have new inventory within roughly two days of ordering it. Much of the fast-turning production sits close by rather than an ocean away, and that closeness is what makes a two-week cycle arithmetically possible in the first place.

This is the expensive, slow part of the advantage, and it is why the strategy resists copying. Inditex committed on the order of two billion euros to logistics expansion for 2024 and 2025 alone, building new distribution centers and adding capacity. A competitor who wants Zara's speed does not need to buy software. They need to rebuild their supply chain, their factory relationships, and their warehouse footprint, then wait years.

The number that proves it works

The whole system exists to reduce one specific loss: clothes nobody buys. A typical fashion retailer finishes a season with somewhere in the mid-teens as a percentage of merchandise unsold, headed for markdown, outlet, or disposal. Operational analyses of Inditex report end-of-season unsold inventory far below that, in the low single digits. Treat any single figure with appropriate caution, since the company does not publish it as a headline financial disclosure, but the direction is not in dispute and the mechanism is obvious: if you commit only a fraction of a season in advance and manufacture the rest against what actually sold last week, you make far less of what nobody wanted.

Five forces, all leaning one way

Run the case through Porter's five forces. Rivalry: the two-week loop turns a commodity fight over price into a fight over speed that rivals structurally lose. New entrants: replicating the factory-logistics-data web is a decade-scale barrier. Substitutes: fashion's substitute is "keep last year's clothes", freshness every two weeks attacks exactly that. Buyer power: scarcity flips it; the customer who waits loses the dress. Supplier power: owning and co-locating much of production keeps it low. One system, five forces, all leaning Zara's way, that is what "strategic" technology means, as opposed to merely useful technology.

The cost of the advantage

Everything above is a story about an information system working. It would be dishonest to stop there, because the same capability that makes Zara efficient is the capability that makes fast fashion an environmental problem, and the connection is not incidental. It is the same machinery.

Consider what the two-week cycle actually does. A traditional retailer committed to a season months ahead, produced a large batch, and lived with the consequences. Zara commits to almost nothing, reads demand continuously, and produces in small batches on short notice. That is why it wastes less inventory than its competitors, and it is also why it can put far more distinct items into the world per year. The New York Times coined the term "fast fashion" to describe exactly this: Zara's ability to move a garment from design to store in about fifteen days.

Multiply that across an industry that copied the model. Textile production accounts for somewhere between eight and ten percent of global carbon emissions depending on whose accounting you accept, which is comparable to the total emissions of the European Union and more than international aviation and shipping combined. Roughly 85 percent of textiles end up in landfill each year. The average garment is now worn seven to ten times before disposal, down more than a third over fifteen years. The speed that removes waste from the supply chain moves it downstream, to the consumer and then to the ground.

The pattern shows up in Inditex's own reporting. In 2024 the company's emissions rose about ten percent while its raw material volume rose about five, and the energy required to move materials to collection centers rose fifteen percent. Efficiency per garment can improve while total impact grows, because the system is optimized for throughput.

The environmental story has a human counterpart. Fast fashion's cost structure depends on manufacturing in countries with lower wages and weaker enforcement, and audits repeatedly find gaps between stated policy and factory reality. Zara makes more of its fashion-sensitive product close to headquarters than most competitors do, which is part of why it is fast. But its full supply chain still reaches into the same regions as everyone else's, and it has faced documented labor concerns along with the rest of the industry. Inditex has announced sustainability targets, including sustainable or recycled cotton, linen, and polyester, and has been criticized for the gap between announcement and verified outcome.

The point for this course

An information system is not morally neutral, and it does not become so by working well. The RFID tags, the twice-weekly delivery rhythm, and the store-manager reorder authority are the reason Zara wastes less inventory and the reason the industry can produce more clothing than the planet can absorb. The same system, read two ways. When you evaluate a system in this course, "does it work?" is only the first question. The second is what it makes easier, and for whom, and at whose expense. You will meet this again in Week 4 with charts that are accurate and misleading, and again in Week 10 when a model learns from a biased past.

What to take away

Strip the case down to what you can reuse. Zara's hardware and software are ordinary and buyable, which is exactly why they are not the advantage. The advantage is the loop: data only Zara collects, moving over a network tuned to a twice-weekly heartbeat, into the hands of people trusted to act on it, through factories close enough to respond in days. Every piece has been visible for twenty years, and no rival has copied it, because copying it means rebuilding the company around it.

Carry the second question with you too. The same system that makes Zara the most efficient firm in fast fashion is the system that made fast fashion possible at industrial scale. When you evaluate any system for the rest of this course, "does it work?" is where the evaluation starts, not where it ends.

The seam is a job market

Every system in this course was assembled by people who could sit between the business and the technology and translate: business analysts, data analysts, consultants, product and project managers. That seam is chronically understaffed, which is why firms like Accenture, Deloitte, and Slalom hire MIS graduates in volume. What their interviews actually test is what this course practices: structure a messy business situation (the discussion questions), handle data without fear (the labs), explain a finding to someone senior in three sentences (every submission note), and use AI to multiply your work without outsourcing your judgment (Lab 1's policy). The course is the portfolio; the certificates are its receipts.

05Discussion questions

None of these get handed in. On Wednesday you run the discussion and I steer it, which means the class is only as good as what you walk in with. Read the chapter, pick the question you have the strongest opinion about, and be ready to say it first.

  5. Cascadia Outfitters is a member-owned co-op competing against giant online retailers with better prices and bigger catalogs. Using this chapter's concepts, what is the co-op's most defensible advantage, and which of the five components does it live in? Open judgment
