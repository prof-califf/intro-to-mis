Chapter 8 · Business Intelligence and Analytics

Business Intelligence: How Oakland Won by Measuring a Different Thing

In 2002 the Oakland Athletics lost their three best players, fielded one of the cheapest rosters in baseball, and won 103 games. They did it with data everybody already had, in a sport that had been recording it for a century. This chapter is about why choosing the measure is the decision that matters, and why the advantage did not last.

Baseball has more data per event than almost any business on earth, and it has been collecting it since the 1870s. In 2001 an entire industry was using that mountain of numbers to measure the wrong thing, and the number that would have told them so had been sitting in the same box score the whole time.

How this chapter runs

Sections 1 and 2 are the concepts: what business intelligence is, the four kinds of analytics, and the traps that catch people reading numbers. Section 3 puts them to work on three failures, one at each level of ambition. Section 4 is the full case, Oakland, and it follows the story past 2002 to what happened once everyone caught up. Section 5 asks what it is like to be the thing being measured.

1What business intelligence actually is

Chapters 6 and 7 were about getting facts into one place and keeping them consistent. This chapter is about what happens next, which is somebody asking the records a question. That sounds like the easy half. It is not, and the reason is that a question has to be asked in numbers, and choosing which number is a decision most organizations make once, by accident, and then live with for decades.

In five-component terms, everything in this chapter sits on top of the data component and is decided by the people one. The hardware and software of business intelligence are ordinary and purchasable, which Chapter 3 already told you means they cannot be the advantage. What cannot be bought is a person who asks whether the number everybody is staring at measures the thing everybody assumes it measures. That question is the entire chapter, and the case at the end is an organization that asked it while an industry with identical data did not.

Key conceptBusiness intelligence & analytics

Business intelligence (BI) is the practice of turning an organization's data into information that supports decisions: the systems, reports, and dashboards that tell a business what is happening. Analytics is the analytical work layered on top, and it comes in four kinds, in rising order of ambition. Descriptive: what happened? Diagnostic: why did it happen? Predictive: what will happen? Prescriptive: what should we do about it? A monthly sales report is descriptive. Drilling into why Bellingham's number moved is diagnostic. A forecast is predictive. A recommended reorder quantity is prescriptive.

You have been doing this for four weeks without the vocabulary. Lab 2's totals were descriptive analytics. Lab 5's mini project, where something was wrong at Cascadia and you had to find out what, was diagnostic analytics, and the forecasting section was your first predictive step. The ladder matters because each rung is worth more and costs more. Most organizations live on the bottom rung, drowning in descriptive reports. (Most of you will spend your first job on the bottom two rungs. That is not a demotion; it is where the work is.) The ones that climb are the ones that turn data into different decisions rather than prettier summaries.

Baseball in 2001 was a perfect specimen of a descriptive-analytics industry. It had more data per event than almost any business on earth, a box score for every game since the 1870s, and it used that mountain of data mostly to describe, not to question. The questioning, when it came, came from outside: a night-shift statistician named Bill James had spent two decades publishing analyses, under the name sabermetrics, showing that some of baseball's most cherished numbers barely predicted winning. The A's were simply the first team desperate enough to act on it.

The wrong number on the scoreboard

For a century, the headline measure of a hitter was batting average: hits divided by at-bats. It is intuitive, traditional, and printed on the back of every card. It also has a design flaw: it ignores walks entirely. A player who draws a walk has reached base, avoided an out, and advanced the inning, and batting average records that event as if it never happened.

Key conceptMetrics, proxies, and measurement validity

A metric is a number chosen to stand in for something you actually care about, and that makes almost every metric a proxy. The thing baseball cares about is winning; wins come from runs; runs come from not making outs. Measurement validity asks: does the proxy actually track the thing? Batting average tracks run-scoring loosely. On-base percentage (OBP), which counts every way of reaching base including walks, tracks it far more tightly. OBP is really measuring the avoidance of outs, and outs are the game's true scarce resource: you only get twenty-seven. The A's insight, distilled: the industry's headline metric had weak validity, a better one was sitting in the same box score, and nobody's prices reflected it.

Notice what kind of insight this is. It required no new technology, no new data, and no advanced math; OBP had been recorded for decades. It required someone to ask whether the number everyone stared at actually measured what they assumed it measured. That question, not the software, is the core skill of business intelligence, and it transfers directly: a store's revenue is a batting average. It tells you something happened. It does not tell you whether the underlying engine of the business, at Cascadia the membership relationship, is healthy or quietly failing.

The scoreboard was fine. The industry was reading the wrong line of it.

2Reading numbers honestly

Choosing the right measure is the first job. Reading it without fooling yourself is the second, and there are three traps that catch people who are perfectly competent at the arithmetic. Two of them are about what the numbers can and cannot tell you. The third is about what happens to a number once people know they are being judged by it.

Key conceptCorrelation, causation, and sample size

Two numbers are correlated when they move together; causation means one actually drives the other. Team OBP correlates strongly with runs scored across a 162-game season, and there is a causal mechanism behind the correlation: not making outs extends innings. That combination, correlation plus a mechanism, tested across a large sample, is what earned the A's their confidence. But the same logic warns you where the model stops working. A five-game playoff series is a small sample, small enough for luck to swamp skill. That is why the 103-win A's could lose their playoff series, and why Beane himself said his approach could not be counted on in October. The lesson is symmetric: enough data makes patterns trustworthy, and small samples make even true patterns unreliable.

Business runs on small samples more than it admits. One viral month, one bad quarter, one focus group of nine people. The Cascadia connection is direct: your Lab 5 forecast extrapolated from months of data, and the honest analyst's question, there and everywhere, is whether the sample behind a pattern is large enough to bet on. A twenty-game winning streak is thrilling and it is also, as the A's themselves would tell you, partly a run of luck sitting on top of a good team.

When the metric becomes the target

There is a trap waiting inside every successful metric, and it has a name: Goodhart's law, usually stated as when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure. The moment you pay people on a number, they optimize the number, not the thing it was a proxy for, and the gap between the two becomes the business's blind spot.

Call centers that grade agents on average handle time get shorter calls and angrier customers, because the fastest way to end a call is to not solve the problem. Schools graded on test scores drift toward teaching the test. A sales team paid on units shipped will stuff the channel in December and eat returns in January. In every case the metric was a reasonable proxy right up until it became the target, and then the measured number improved while the real thing decayed. Baseball is not exempt: a hitter paid for OBP alone can learn to take walks in situations where swinging would win the game.

This is why choosing metrics is a management decision, not a reporting chore. The professional habit is to pair every target with a counter-metric that catches the gaming: handle time with resolution rate, units shipped with returns. Keep this section in mind for the Monday activity, because your job there is to find one of these traps in the wild.

From the dugout to the dashboard

What did the A's revolution look like as an information system? Unremarkably modest: public statistics, DePodesta's laptop, and spreadsheet models. The lesson is not the technology; it is the architecture, which is the same one every BI-capable business runs today, at larger scale.

Key conceptThe BI stack: operational systems, warehouse, dashboard

Operational systems, the registers and databases from Chapters 6 and 7, are built to record transactions quickly, one at a time. Analysis asks the opposite kind of question: patterns across millions of rows and months of history. So organizations copy operational data into a data warehouse, a database organized for analysis rather than transactions, and put dashboards on top: visual displays of the metrics that matter, updated continuously. The separation exists so that a heavy analytical query never slows down the register, and so that analysis runs on a cleaned, consistent copy, which after Lab 7 you know is not the copy the registers write.

The last layer is self-service BI, tools like Tableau that let the analyst, rather than a programmer, explore the warehouse and build the dashboard. That is this week's lab, and it closes the loop on the course's longest-running thread. At Cascadia, revenue is the batting average: Bellingham's looked fine on every standard report. The member attach rate is the on-base percentage: the truer measure of the underlying engine, and the number that finally exposed the problem. You found it in a pivot table in Lab 5; this week you will make it impossible for a manager to miss, which is what a dashboard is for.

The one-sentence version

BI is not having data. Everyone has data. BI is choosing the measure that tracks what you actually care about, and putting it where decisions get made.

3Three failures, three rungs

Before the case, three short ones, arranged up the ladder from section 1. The first is a descriptive failure, the second predictive, the third prescriptive. Read them in order and watch the cost of being wrong grow. A bad description produces a bad slide. A bad prediction produces a bad forecast. A bad prescription produces a bad forecast that has already spent your money.

Mini-case 1 · 1986: the chart that did not show the thing it contained

The night before the Challenger launch, engineers at the contractor that built the shuttle's rocket boosters argued against flying. It was going to be unusually cold in Florida, and they believed the rubber seals between booster sections would be less able to seal at low temperature. They had data. Seals had been damaged on previous flights, and the damage had been recorded.

What they did not have was a picture that made the relationship visible. The charts taken into that teleconference presented the damaged flights, and did not plot damage against temperature across all the flights, including the ones with no damage at all. Read that way, the case looked like scattered incidents rather than a trend. The launch went ahead the next morning in near-freezing conditions and the shuttle was destroyed, killing all seven people aboard.

Later analysis of those materials became a standard teaching example in how to display quantitative information, because the argument the engineers were making was in their data and was not in their charts. This is descriptive analytics failing at its only job.

The rung: descriptive. The component: software, meaning the tool that displayed it. Nobody lacked the numbers, and nobody miscalculated. The presentation decided what a room full of decision-makers could see, which is worth remembering in a week when you are choosing what to put on a dashboard.

Check yourselfThe damaged flights were shown and the undamaged ones were left out. Explain why leaving out the cases where nothing happened destroys the ability to see a relationship, and name one chart you have made where you did the same thing without noticing.

Mini-case 2 · 2013: the model that kept answering after the world moved

In 2008 Google launched Flu Trends, which estimated how much influenza was circulating in the United States from what people were searching for. It looked like a triumph. It produced estimates faster than the public health agency it was built to match, and it was held up everywhere as proof of what large data could do.

Then it drifted. Between August 2011 and September 2013 it reported flu levels that were too high in 100 weeks out of 108. In the 2012 to 2013 season it overshot by more than half, and at one point predicted roughly double the proportion of doctor visits the agency measured. Researchers who took the model apart found several causes, including that heavy news coverage of flu made healthy, worried people search in ways the model read as illness. Google revised it, then retired it in 2015.

Nothing broke. The model kept running and kept producing confident numbers, and the relationship between search behavior and illness had changed underneath it. A model is a claim about how the world works, and the world does not agree to hold still.

The rung: predictive. The component: data. The failure was not in the mathematics. It was that the inputs stopped meaning what they meant when the model was built, and no amount of computation notices that on its own.

Check yourselfGoogle's model was checked against the agency's actual figures, which is how anyone knew it had drifted. Name one forecast you have seen at work or in the news where no such check exists, and say what you would compare it to.

Mini-case 3 · 2021: the algorithm that was allowed to spend

A model looks at a house and names a price, and a company wires the money, thousands of times, on the model's word. Zillow is a property website. In 2018 it started buying houses itself, using its own price estimates to decide what to pay, renovating them and selling them on. The estimate was no longer advice for a reader. It was an instruction to spend money.

The model was not wildly wrong. It was wrong by a few percentage points, in a business where a few percentage points is the entire margin, and it was wrong in the same direction across thousands of houses at once. In November 2021 Zillow shut the business down, announced writedowns of up to $569 million, and cut about a quarter of its staff.

Compare that with the other two. A bad chart misleads a meeting. A bad forecast misleads a plan. A bad prescription buys seven thousand houses. The difference is not accuracy. It is how much authority somebody granted the output before anyone checked it.

The rung: prescriptive. The component: people. The decision that cost the money was not made by the model. It was made by the executives who decided how far the model's answers could travel without a human in between. That is the same decision Chapter 4 asked you to make about handing steps to an agent.

Check yourselfZillow's estimate had been public for years before it was used to buy anything. Explain what changed about the same number when it stopped being displayed and started being acted on, and name the specific control you would have put between the model and the cheque.

What the three have in common

Three failures, three rungs, and not one of them was caused by bad arithmetic. The Challenger data was correct. Google's model was competently built. Zillow's estimate was close. In every case the numbers were fine and something around them was not: how they were displayed, whether anyone checked them against reality, and how much authority they were given.

That is the argument of this chapter in advance. Business intelligence is not a calculation problem. It is a series of judgments about what to measure, how to show it, what to compare it against, and how far to let it act on its own. The case that follows is what it looks like when an organization gets the first of those judgments right while an entire industry gets it wrong.

4Oakland: the team that measured differently

After the 2001 season, the Oakland Athletics lost their best hitter, Jason Giambi, to the New York Yankees on a seven-year, $120 million contract, and lost outfielder Johnny Damon and closer Jason Isringhausen the same winter. Oakland could not outbid anyone: its payroll of roughly $41 million was the third lowest in Major League Baseball, against the Yankees' roughly $125 million. By every conventional measure, the 2002 A's were supposed to collapse.

General manager Billy Beane and his assistant Paul DePodesta could not buy the players everyone agreed were good, so they asked a more basic question: is everyone right about who is good? Baseball had a century of meticulous statistics and a settled consensus about which ones mattered. Beane and DePodesta treated that consensus as a hypothesis rather than a fact, tested it against the data, and found it wrong in an exploitable way. Then they built a roster out of the players the market had mispriced: a catcher with a damaged arm converted to first base, a sidearm reliever nobody wanted, and hitters who looked wrong and walked constantly.

The result was 103 wins, a division title, and a twenty-game winning streak that set an American League record at the time, capped by a walk-off home run from Scott Hatteberg, the converted catcher. The season became Michael Lewis's book Moneyball and later the film. But strip away the baseball and what remains is the cleanest business intelligence case ever documented. An organization gained a competitive advantage not by having more data than its rivals, because everyone had the same data, but by measuring the right thing while everyone else measured the wrong one.

Buying what the market mispriced

A mismeasured quality becomes a mispriced asset. Because the market paid for batting average, home runs, and how an athlete looked in uniform, players who merely got on base relentlessly came cheap. Hatteberg, coming off a career-threatening injury and hitting for modest averages, was exactly that. His on-base skills made him a bargain, and in 2002 he posted a .374 OBP and hit the home run that won game twenty of the streak. Reliever Chad Bradford threw underhand, looked unorthodox, got outs, and cost almost nothing. The roster was an arbitrage portfolio built on a market inefficiency: a price that had drifted away from the value it was supposed to track.

Connect this to Chapter 3. Porter would say the A's found a competitive advantage grounded in information. Everyone had the same raw data, but Oakland converted it into a truer model of value than its rivals used, which let a $41 million payroll buy roughly as much real production as teams spending three times more. And, exactly as Chapter 3 predicts, the advantage decayed as the information spread. Boston hired sabermetric thinkers and won the 2004 World Series; within a decade every front office employed analysts; and once everyone measured correctly, the edge returned to the teams with the most money. An information advantage is real, and it is rented, not owned.

What happened after everybody caught up

Chapter 3 predicted the ending of this story before you read it. An advantage built on information lasts exactly as long as the information stays scarce, and this one did not stay scarce, because Michael Lewis published a book explaining it.

Boston hired the same kind of thinking and won the 2004 World Series, ending an eighty-six-year drought. Within about a decade every club in the league employed analysts, and the analytics departments that were novelties in 2002 became standard staffing. Once every team measured correctly, measuring correctly stopped being an edge, and the advantage flowed back to the clubs with the most money, which now spent it efficiently. Oakland, for its part, never won a World Series under Beane, and by the 2020s was among the lowest-payroll teams in the sport and left the city entirely.

Then something happened that no chapter in this book has shown you yet: the analysis worked so well that the industry changed the rules to stop it.

Teams had used the data to reposition fielders, moving three infielders to one side of the diamond against hitters whose spray charts said they almost always hit there. It worked. It also removed base hits that fans had paid to see, and the sport's offensive numbers fell. Before the 2023 season Major League Baseball banned the practice, requiring two infielders on each side of second base. Batting averages on balls in play rose that season.

Stay with that for a moment, because it generalizes further than baseball. An analytics program succeeded, its conclusions were adopted by everyone, the aggregate result was a worse product for the customer, and the governing body outlawed the conclusion. You will meet the same shape in Week 8 with credit scoring and in Week 10 with recommendation systems. The question is what happens when optimization works, everyone does it, and the thing being optimized was not the thing anybody actually wanted.

5The other side: being measured

The Moneyball story has winners it celebrates and costs it mentions quietly. The A's model worked partly by overruling scouts, professionals with decades of pattern-recognition that resisted quantification, and the analytics wave that followed thinned their ranks across the sport. Players experienced the shift too: to a model you are a row of numbers, and the qualities the numbers do not capture, leadership, resilience, how you play hurt, stop being priced. Some of that was the point; prejudice about how an athlete looked was exactly the noise OBP filtered out. But a measurement regime always sees some things by going blind to others, and the people inside it feel the blindness.

Two honest footnotes belong in the case. The 2002 A's, for all 103 wins, lost in the first playoff round, and Beane's Oakland teams never won a World Series: analytics bought an edge, not a championship, and edges decay. And once every team adopted the same methods, the advantage flowed back to the largest payrolls, now spending efficiently. Measuring right is necessary. It is not, on its own, sufficient, and it is never permanent. You will see the same pattern next week when every retailer learns to mine its data and the question becomes who has more of it.

Key takeaways
  • Business intelligence is not a calculation problem. It is a series of judgments about what to measure, how to show it, what to check it against, and how far to let it act alone.
  • Every metric is a proxy. The first question is never what the number says; it is whether the number tracks the thing you actually care about.
  • When a measure becomes a target, it stops being a good measure. Pair every target with a counter-metric that catches the gaming.
  • The A's did not have more data than the Yankees. They had a truer model of value, and the market inefficiency it revealed lasted only until everyone else adopted it.
  • A dashboard is a decision about what people will look at. The people who choose the metric, and how far its answers may travel without a human, are making the management decision.

6Vocabulary

Business intelligence (BI)
Systems and practices that turn organizational data into decision-ready information.
Descriptive / diagnostic / predictive / prescriptive
The four kinds of analytics: what happened, why, what will, what to do.
Metric / KPI
A number chosen to stand for something the business cares about; a key performance indicator is a metric elevated to a target.
Proxy
What every metric secretly is: a stand-in for the real thing, valid only as far as it tracks it.
Measurement validity
Whether a metric actually measures what it claims to.
Sabermetrics
The empirical analysis of baseball; the movement that found the industry's measurement error.
On-base percentage
Share of plate appearances reaching base; the undervalued, more valid measure.
Market inefficiency
A gap between price and value, here created by an industry-wide measurement error.
Correlation vs. causation
Moving together vs. one driving the other; trust correlations with a mechanism and a large sample.
Small-sample problem
Patterns in little data mislead; a five-game series is a coin flip.
Goodhart's law
When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure.
Data warehouse
A database organized for analysis, fed by copies of operational data.
Dashboard / self-service BI
Visual metric displays, and the tools (like Tableau) that let analysts build them directly.
