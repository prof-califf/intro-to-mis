Chapter 10 · IT Governance

IT Governance: When Everyone Was Responsible and No One Was

In 2017, Equifax lost the personal data of nearly 150 million people. There was a patch that would have prevented it. The patch was known, available, and even ordered internally. It was simply never applied to the right server, because no one owned the job. This chapter is about governance: the structures that decide who is accountable, before the crisis makes it obvious that no one was.

01A patch nobody applied

In March 2017, a serious vulnerability was disclosed in Apache Struts, a piece of open-source software used inside countless corporate web applications. A fix was published the same week. Equifax, one of the three US credit bureaus, was told internally to apply it. The instruction went out. And then, on one internet-facing system that happened to run the vulnerable software, it did not get done. No alarm connected the order to the omission.

Beginning in May, attackers used that exact vulnerability to get in, and they stayed for about 76 days, extracting the names, Social Security numbers, birth dates, and addresses of roughly 147 million people, close to half the US population. A device meant to detect the exfiltration had been inactive for months because an expired security certificate had never been renewed. Two boring omissions, a missed patch and a lapsed certificate, and a firm whose entire business was holding sensitive data handed nearly half a country's identities to strangers.

Why this is the governance chapter

Nothing about Equifax required advanced attackers, and this is the point. Every technical safeguard that would have stopped it already existed and was already understood. What failed was governance: the structure that assigns who must do what, who checks that it was done, and who answers when it was not. Chapter 9 was about controls. This chapter is about who is accountable for the controls, which turns out to be the harder problem.

02What governance is

Key conceptIT governance

IT governance is the set of structures, policies, and decision rights that determine how an organization directs and controls its technology: who is allowed to decide what, who must do what, who verifies it was done, and who is answerable when it is not. Governance is not software you install; it is the answer to the question "who is in charge of this, specifically?" for every part of the technology that matters. When that answer is vague, work falls through the gaps between people who each assumed someone else had it, which is a fair one-sentence summary of the Equifax breach.

Governance sounds bureaucratic until you watch its absence. The Struts patch failed not because anyone refused to do it but because the task lived in the space between teams: the people who knew about the vulnerability, the people who owned the server, and the people responsible for verifying compliance were not connected by any structure that forced the loop to close. Good governance is mostly the unglamorous work of making sure every critical task has exactly one owner and one check.

03Responsibility versus accountability

Key conceptResponsibility and accountability

These words are used loosely in conversation and precisely in governance. Responsibility is the obligation to do the work: the engineer who applies the patch. Accountability is the obligation to answer for the outcome, and it cannot be shared or delegated away: one named person is accountable that the patch got applied, whether or not they touched a keyboard. The classic governance failure is work where responsibility is spread across many people and accountability is assigned to none, so when it fails, everyone can truthfully say it was not their job. "Everyone was responsible" is how you get an outcome nobody owned.

Equifax is the textbook illustration. Afterward, no single person could be cleanly blamed for the missed patch, which sounds like exoneration and is actually the diagnosis. A system in which no one can be held accountable for a critical task is a system that has been designed, by accident, to fail quietly. The company's CEO, CIO, and CSO all departed, because accountability, when it has not been assigned in advance to someone specific, rolls upward to whoever sits at the top, which is exactly why executives should want it assigned in advance.

04The frameworks

Organizations do not invent governance from scratch. They adopt frameworks: shared checklists and vocabularies that encode what mature IT management looks like, so that "are we secure?" becomes a set of answerable questions rather than a feeling.

Key conceptGovernance frameworks

A framework is a structured, widely accepted set of practices an organization can adopt and be measured against. In security and IT governance the common ones include the NIST Cybersecurity Framework (organized around five functions: identify, protect, detect, respond, recover) and COBIT (aimed at aligning IT with business goals). Their value is not magic; it is that they turn vague intentions into specific, auditable commitments, and they let an outsider, an auditor, a regulator, a board, check whether the commitments are being met. Frameworks are how an organization proves its governance exists rather than merely claiming it does.

Map Equifax onto the NIST functions and the failure is easy to locate: identify (they knew about the vulnerability), protect (they failed to patch), detect (the monitoring certificate had expired), respond (they were slow and their public response was widely criticized). A framework would not have applied the patch for them. But a framework, honestly audited, forces the question "show me that every internet-facing system got this patch," and that question is precisely the one no one at Equifax was structurally required to answer.

05Compliance is not security

Key conceptCompliance versus security

Compliance means meeting the specific requirements of a standard or law. Security means actually being hard to breach. They overlap but are not the same, and the gap between them is where a lot of breaches live. An organization can pass its audits, hold its certifications, and check every box while a single unpatched server sits exposed, because compliance measures whether documented controls exist, not whether every real system is actually covered every day. Treating a passed audit as proof of safety is one of the most common and expensive mistakes in the field. Compliance is a floor, not a finish line.

This is the trap that connects both cases in this pair. Target, a decade before Change Healthcare, was certified compliant with the payment-card security standard when it was breached. Equifax operated in a regulated industry with real requirements. Compliance regimes are valuable, they raise the floor for everyone, but a compliance mindset can actively harm security if it lets an organization mistake the paperwork for the protection. The job of good governance is to close that gap, not to celebrate the certificate.

06The product was people

There is a fact about Equifax that makes its breach different in kind from a retailer losing card numbers, and it deserves to be stated plainly. The 147 million people whose data was exposed were never Equifax's customers. They never signed up, never agreed to terms, never chose to do business with the company at all. Equifax's customers are lenders. The people in its database are its product: their financial histories, collected without their direct involvement, packaged and sold to others.

This inverts the usual accountability relationship and it is the deepest governance question in the chapter. A normal company that loses customer data faces those customers in the market: they can leave. The people harmed by the Equifax breach had no such lever, because they were never in a relationship they could exit. You cannot boycott a company you were never a customer of. When the people bearing the risk are not the people the company answers to, the market cannot govern the outcome by itself, and governance has to come from somewhere else: regulation, law, and the assignment of accountability by force rather than by choice.

07The other side: data about people who never agreed

Chapter 9 argued that breach costs are an externality, exported to people outside the company's books. Equifax pushes that argument to its limit, because the people bearing the risk never entered into anything at all.

A Social Security number cannot be reissued. The 147 million people in the Equifax breach carry a lifetime exposure to identity theft that no settlement retires, and the roughly $700 million Equifax paid in 2019, split among restitution, credit monitoring, and penalties, works out to a few dollars of face value per person for a permanent risk. The company continues to operate and remains one of three firms that decide whether Americans can get a mortgage. The breach did not end the business, because the business does not depend on the goodwill of the people it holds data about.

Hold this against the second question of the course. "Does the system work?" Equifax's data system worked extremely well, at collecting, matching, and selling. The question this course insists on adding, "for whom, and at whose expense?", is the entire content of the Equifax story, and it is why the answer to a governance failure this large is not only a better patch process but a public argument about who should be allowed to hold what data about whom, and under what obligations. That argument is where the next unit, on regulating technology, begins.

10Discussion questions

  1. The Struts patch was ordered but never applied to the right server. Redesign the process so this specific failure cannot happen: who owns the task, who verifies it, and how does the loop close? Apply
  2. Distinguish responsibility from accountability using an example from your own life or work. Why can accountability not be shared, even when responsibility is? Explain
  3. "Everyone was responsible" produced an outcome nobody owned. Where else, in organizations you know, does diffuse responsibility hide the absence of accountability? Analyze
  4. Target was PCI-compliant and Equifax was regulated when each was breached. Explain to a skeptical executive why passing the audit did not make them secure. Evaluate
  5. The 147 million people harmed were Equifax's product, not its customers, and could not leave. What does that fact do to the argument that market pressure will discipline companies into security? Judgment
  6. Apply the NIST functions (identify, protect, detect, respond, recover) to the Equifax timeline. At which function did it first fail, and would fixing only that one have prevented the breach? Systems
  7. A settlement of about $700 million works out to a few dollars per exposed person for a permanent risk. Is that justice, deterrence, both, or neither? What would a better remedy look like? Ethics
  8. Connect to Change Healthcare: both breaches trace to a single unglamorous omission (a missing patch, a missing MFA setting). What governance structure would catch that class of omission before an attacker does? Connect
