Every December, a music app produces a slideshow about you, your top artist, your minutes, your “listening personality”, and within hours it is everywhere, posted voluntarily by hundreds of millions of people. Wrapped is among the most successful marketing campaigns in the world, it costs Spotify almost nothing at the margin, and it is not really a marketing artifact at all. It is an information system doing exactly what information systems do.

01Wrapped as a system

Strip the confetti off and look at what has to be true for Wrapped to exist. Every play, skip, and replay you produced all year was captured, timestamped, attached to your account, stored. That raw data was processed: aggregated, ranked, compared against everyone else’s. The results were packaged into a form a human wants to look at. And the package was delivered at the exact moment of the year you’d most want to share it, turning you, unpaid, into Spotify’s distribution channel during the season people choose next year’s subscriptions.

Capture, process, package, deliver, and a business result at the end. That pipeline, not the app, not the servers, is the information system. The technology is just what the pipeline is currently made of.

02So what is MIS?

Key conceptManagement information system

A management information system is the combination of hardware, software, data, networks, and people that an organization uses to collect information and act on it. The definition’s point is the word combination: none of the five is a system by itself, and a failure in any one is a failure of the whole. This course is training in seeing all five at once, in systems that mostly show you only their surface.

Management Information Systems is the discipline that studies how organizations use data, technology, processes, and people to make decisions and create value. Notice the list has five elements and only one of them is technology. That ratio is the course.

This is why MIS lives in a business school and not only in a computer science department. The computer scientist asks whether the system works. We ask whether the system is worth building, whether the organization around it will actually use it, and whether the decisions it feeds are better than the decisions it replaced. Most famous technology failures, and you will meet several this quarter, were systems that worked, inside organizations that didn’t.

03The thread of the course: the five components of MIS

Key conceptThe weakest component rule

A system is only as good as its weakest component. Spotify’s models, data pipelines, and delivery network all worked in 2025, and a timezone assumption that nobody tested made some of the output wrong anyway. The rule’s practical form is a habit of investigation: when a system fails, resist the first explanation and walk all five components before assigning blame. You will run that walk on a real failure in every chapter of this book.

Every information system, Wrapped, a hospital records system, the register at your coffee shop, is built from the same five components. Every case in this book gets mapped onto them. Learn to see the five and you can decompose any system you ever meet, including the ones you will be hired to fix.

The Five Components of MIS
Hardware

The physical machines: phones, laptops, servers, sensors, registers.

Software

The instructions the hardware runs, from operating systems to Wrapped's pipeline.

Data

The raw facts captured about the world: plays, sales, clicks, temperatures.

Networks

The connections that move data between machines, and between organizations.

People

Everyone who builds, operates, interprets, ignores, or games the system.

One rule governs the whole course: a system is only as strong as its weakest component, no matter how strong the others are.

Wrapped has all five working: your phone and Spotify’s data centers (hardware), the pipeline that aggregates and packages a year of listening (software), every timestamped play (data), the internet carrying events in and finished stories out (networks), and millions of listeners who volunteer as the distribution channel (people). Next week you meet a company running the same five on coffee. By Week 3 you meet companies where a component is broken, and you practice locating which one.

04Inside the 2025 Archive

Everything above is the shape of Wrapped from outside. In March 2026, Spotify's engineering team published an account of how they actually built the 2025 edition, and it is worth walking through carefully, because it turns the five components from a definition you memorize into five sets of decisions you can see people making.

The 2025 feature was called Wrapped Archive. Rather than just handing you your top artist, it picked up to five remarkable days out of your listening year and wrote you a short story about each one. The day you discovered the artist that changed everything. The day your taste took a hard left turn. Here is what had to be true for that to reach you.

Data: finding five days in a year of listening

Start with the raw material, because everything downstream depends on it. Spotify had every play event from every listener, timestamped. The problem was not collecting data. The problem was that a year of listening for hundreds of millions of people is far too much to hand to anything and say "find what matters."

So the team wrote rules, what engineers call heuristics, that define what "remarkable" means in terms a computer can check. Biggest listening day is just the highest total minutes. Biggest discovery day is the day you heard the most first-time artists. Most unusual day is the day you strayed furthest from your normal taste. Then they ranked the candidates and kept the best few per person. Notice what happened there: a fuzzy human idea ("what was memorable about your year?") got translated into arithmetic. That translation is most of what data work actually is, and you will do a smaller version of it in every lab this quarter.

One detail from their account is worth noting. When they sent each day to the model that wrote the story, they also sent a pre-computed block of statistics, because, in their words, language models are bad at math. The system was designed around a known weakness of one of its own components. Good engineering is mostly that.

Software: teaching a model to write 1.4 billion stories

Around 350 million eligible listeners, up to five stories each, comes to roughly 1.4 billion reports. Every one had to be written before launch day.

The first version of the writing instructions took more than three months of daily iteration. The team split the instructions in two: a standing set of rules about tone and safety, and a per-story packet containing that day's listening log, the stats block, the listener's country, and the stories already written for that person so the next one would not repeat them. One of their findings runs against intuition: the more instructions they added, the less creative the output became.

Then economics intervened. The best available model wrote wonderful stories and would have been financially impossible to run 1.4 billion times. So they used the expensive model to produce a carefully reviewed set of excellent examples, and used those examples to train a smaller, cheaper, faster model to write the same way. That tradeoff, quality against cost, is a business decision rather than a purely technical one, and it is exactly the kind of judgment MIS graduates are asked to make.

Hardware: what "one second idle, the next second global" requires

Wrapped does not roll out gradually. It arrives everywhere at once, which makes the preparation behind it worth examining closely, because you will meet the opposite outcome in Week 4.

Automatic scaling, where a system adds capacity in response to load, is standard practice and would have failed here. It reacts, and Wrapped does not ramp, it spikes. So the team scaled up compute and database capacity hours before launch, coordinated capacity with their model provider ahead of time, and then ran fake traffic through every region on purpose, timed to finish just before real listeners arrived. The point of the fake traffic was to warm everything up. As they put it, when real traffic arrived, nothing was cold.

Hold onto this comparison

Wrapped and Healthcare.gov are the same launch shape: no gradual rollout, everyone at once, one high-stakes moment. One team pre-scaled capacity, coordinated with suppliers, and rehearsed with synthetic load across every region. The other launched on a date set by law with no idea how many people would show up. In Week 4 you will read what that second choice cost. The lesson is not that big-bang launches are reckless. It is that they are survivable only when someone does this work first.

Networks: moving the work, and the safe way to store it

Once a listener's remarkable days were computed, they went onto a message queue, a line that work waits in so the next stage can pick it up when ready. The pieces of the system do not have to run at the same speed or at the same time, which is what let generation run flat out for four days straight.

Storage held a second lesson. Each listener's five stories were written at the same moment by different processes. Written naively, two of them updating the same "here is what's done" list would overwrite each other, and stories would silently vanish. Instead the team gave every day its own slot, labeled by date, so simultaneous writes touch different places and never collide. Their conclusion is the sentence to remember: the most elegant solution to that problem was not clever code, it was thoughtful data modeling. When you get to databases in Week 7, this is why structure comes before queries.

People: the timezone bug

The final component is the one that determined whether any of this worked, and it is the reason this case belongs at the front of an MIS course.

At this scale, a failure rate of one tenth of one percent still means millions of broken stories, and nobody can read a billion anything. So the team had larger models grade a sample of about 165,000 stories on accuracy, safety, tone, and formatting, with humans reviewing on top.

The grading caught something. Some "biggest discovery day" stories were confidently celebrating the wrong number of new artists. The rule for picking the day was fine. The writing was fine. Buried upstream in the data pipeline was a timezone bug that occasionally surfaced the wrong day, and the model, faithfully and beautifully, wrote a compelling story about a day that was not the right one.

The data was wrong, so the system produced fluent, confident, well-written nonsense.

That sentence summarizes the course. Four components performed perfectly. Hardware served, software wrote, networks delivered, and the data was quietly wrong, so the output was wrong. No amount of excellence in the other four could rescue it. And notice how it got caught: not by the technology, but by people who assumed their system would fail somewhere and built the checking, the logging, and the ability to trace a bad story back to its source. Then they fixed the pipeline, deleted the affected stories in bulk, and regenerated them.

That is the weakest component rule with a real company's name on it. It is also your job description. Somebody has to be the person who asks whether the beautiful output is actually true.

05How this course works

Mondays introduce concepts. Wednesdays apply them to a real company at a specific moment, with your groups presenting assigned discussion questions. About AI in this course

You will use generative AI throughout, deliberately, and under a policy you learn in Lab 1 this week. The short version: AI as tutor is always fine; AI as ghostwriter needs disclosure; AI as substitute for your own thinking, or during exams, is never fine.

06Vocabulary

Information system
The combination of data, technology, process, and people that produces information for decisions.
MIS
The business discipline studying how organizations use information systems to create value.
Data vs. information
Data is captured facts; information is data processed into something that changes a decision.
Five components of MIS
Hardware, software, data, networks, and people, this book’s analytical thread.
Efficiency
Doing the thing with less.
Effectiveness
Doing the right thing at all; the harder test.
Systems thinking
Seeing the whole system rather than its parts in isolation.
Weakest component rule
System value is capped by the weakest of the five components, not the strongest.
Heuristic
A rule that turns a fuzzy human idea into something a computer can check.
Pre-scaling
Adding capacity before a known spike, rather than reacting to load as it arrives.
Message queue
A line work waits in, letting parts of a system run at different speeds.
By-product data
Data generated as a side effect of ordinary activity, often more valuable than the activity itself.

Wednesday activity · The timezone bug20 minutes

Spotify’s 2025 Wrapped shipped to hundreds of millions of listeners. One bug meant a set of users received summaries built from the wrong day boundaries: fluent, confident, personalized, and wrong. Nothing crashed. The reports looked perfect.

Which of the five components failed here: hardware, software, data, networks, or people? Commit to one before you discuss.

Think 3 min

Write your one component and a single sentence of reasoning. No hedging, pick one.

Pair 5 min

Compare with the person next to you. If you chose differently, try to talk each other out of it. If you chose the same, find the strongest case for a different one.

Share 12 min

We will take a show of hands for each component, then hear the best argument for two of them.

Where this landsExpect the room to split, mostly between software and data, and that split is the point. The defensible answer is people: the code did what it was told, the data was intact, and what was missing was a check that anyone could have run. Failures rarely announce which component broke, which is why the weakest component rule is a habit of investigation rather than a fact to memorize.
AI and these questions

Green: after drafting your own answer, ask an AI to argue against it and press on your weakest claim, sparring partner mode, before you present. Red: submitting or reading out an AI-written answer. These discussions grade your reasoning, and everyone in the room can tell the difference.

07Discussion questions

  1. Map Wrapped onto all five components in your own words. Warm-up
  2. Name an app you used today and identify one piece of by-product data it captured that you did not deliberately give it. What decision might that data inform?
  3. Wrapped users do Spotify’s marketing for free, happily. Is that a fair exchange? What exactly does each side give and get?
  4. Give an example, real or plausible, of a system with excellent hardware and software but a broken People component. What does the failure look like from outside? Open judgment
  5. The timezone bug produced stories that were fluent, confident, and wrong. Where else do you accept a well-presented answer without checking whether the data underneath it is correct? (Spotify's account describes how they caught it.)
  6. Spotify replaced their best model with a cheaper one that was nearly as good, because 1.4 billion reports had to be affordable. Explain that tradeoff to someone who does not work in technology, and say where you would have drawn the line. Hard
  7. Wrapped Archive used a year of listening history to write stories about people's lives. Spotify did not ask permission for that specific use. Is the exchange fair, and would your answer change if the stories were about something more sensitive than music? Open judgment
