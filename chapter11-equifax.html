Chapter 11 · IT Governance

IT Governance: When Everyone Was Responsible and No One Was

In 2017 Equifax lost the personal data of nearly 150 million people. A fix existed, was known, was available, and had even been ordered internally. It was never applied to the one server that mattered, because no single person owned the job. This chapter is about the structures that decide who answers, before a crisis reveals that nobody did.

Chapter 10 was about controls: the locks, the segmentation, the second step at the door. This chapter is about who is accountable for the controls. That turns out to be the harder problem, and the one that actually decides whether the controls exist on the day you need them.

How this chapter runs

Sections 01 and 02 are the concepts: what governance is, the difference between responsibility and accountability, and why passing an audit is not the same as being safe. Section 03 puts them to work on three failures where the technology behaved exactly as built. Section 04 is the full case, Equifax. Section 05 asks the question the case forces, which is what an organization owes to people who are its product rather than its customers.

Learning objectives

By the end of this chapter you should be able to:

  1. Define IT governance and explain what it decides: who may decide, who must do, who verifies, and who answers.
  2. Distinguish responsibility from accountability, and explain why accountability cannot be delegated.
  3. Explain what frameworks like NIST and COBIT provide, and the difference between compliance and security.
  4. Read a failure for the governance gap: the work that was everyone's responsibility and nobody's accountability.
  5. Explain why Equifax is different in kind from a retailer losing card numbers, and what that implies about who governs.

01What governance is

Chapter 10 ended on a hopeful note: nearly every breach is a failure to apply known controls, which means most security is a management problem. This chapter is about the management problem, and it is less comfortable than it sounds.

Governance is what decides whether the five components are looked after at all. Somebody has to own each piece of hardware, or a server sits on the internet with nobody's name against it. Somebody has to own whether software is patched and whether the monitoring is still alive. Somebody has to own what data the organization holds and what it owes the people in it. Somebody has to own who may reach what across the networks. And all of those owners are people, which is why governance is not a technology at all.

Here is the version of the weakest component rule that this chapter adds. A control with no named owner is not a control. It is an intention, and intentions do not fail loudly. Everything below is about making sure that when something is nobody's job, you find out before an attacker does.

Key conceptIT governance

IT governance is the set of structures, policies, and decision rights that determine how an organization directs and controls its technology. It answers four questions: who is allowed to decide what, who must do what, who verifies it was done, and who is answerable when it is not. Governance is not software you install; it is the answer to the question "who is in charge of this, specifically?" for every part of the technology that matters. When that answer is vague, work falls through the gaps between people who each assumed someone else had it, which is a fair one-sentence summary of the Equifax breach.

Governance sounds bureaucratic until you watch its absence. The Struts patch failed not because anyone refused to do it but because the task lived in the space between teams. The people who knew about the vulnerability, the people who owned the server, and the people responsible for verifying compliance had no structure connecting them that forced the loop to close. Good governance is mostly the unglamorous work of making sure every critical task has exactly one owner and one check.

Responsibility versus accountability

Key conceptResponsibility and accountability

These words are used loosely in conversation and precisely in governance. Responsibility is the obligation to do the work: the engineer who applies the patch. Accountability is the obligation to answer for the outcome, and nobody can share it or delegate it away. One named person is accountable that the patch got applied, whether or not they touched a keyboard. (The practical test is simple: whose name is on the email when it goes wrong?) The classic governance failure is work that many people are responsible for and nobody is accountable for. When it fails, everyone can truthfully say it was not their job. "Everyone was responsible" is how you get an outcome nobody owned.

Equifax is the textbook illustration. Afterward, no single person could be cleanly blamed for the missed patch, which sounds like exoneration and is actually the diagnosis. A system in which no one can be held accountable for a critical task is a system that has been designed, by accident, to fail quietly. The company's CEO, CIO, and CSO all departed. Accountability that nobody assigned in advance rolls upward to whoever sits at the top, which is exactly why executives should want it assigned in advance.

02Frameworks, compliance, and their limits

Organizations do not invent governance from scratch, and they are frequently required to prove it exists. Both of those facts produce useful machinery and a characteristic trap, and you should be able to see the difference from across a room.

Organizations do not invent governance from scratch. They adopt frameworks: shared checklists and vocabularies that encode what mature IT management looks like, so that "are we secure?" becomes a set of answerable questions rather than a feeling.

Key conceptGovernance frameworks

A framework is a structured, widely accepted set of practices an organization can adopt and be measured against. In security and IT governance the common ones include the NIST Cybersecurity Framework (organized around five functions: identify, protect, detect, respond, recover) and COBIT (aimed at aligning IT with business goals). Their value is not magic. They turn vague intentions into specific, auditable commitments, and they let an outsider, an auditor, a regulator, a board, check whether the organization is keeping them. Frameworks are how an organization proves its governance exists rather than merely claiming it does.

Map Equifax onto the NIST functions and the failure is easy to locate: identify (they knew about the vulnerability), protect (they failed to patch), detect (the monitoring certificate had expired), respond (they were slow and their public response was widely criticized). A framework would not have applied the patch for them. But a framework, audited in good faith, forces the question "show me that every internet-facing system got this patch," and that question is precisely the one no one at Equifax was structurally required to answer.

Compliance is not security

Key conceptCompliance versus security

Compliance means meeting the specific requirements of a standard or law. Security means actually being hard to breach. They overlap but are not the same, and the gap between them is where a lot of breaches live. An organization can pass its audits, hold its certifications, and check every box while a single unpatched server sits exposed. Compliance measures whether documented controls exist, not whether every real system is actually covered every day. Treating a passed audit as proof of safety is one of the most common and expensive mistakes in the field. Compliance is a floor, not a finish line.

This is the trap that connects both cases in this pair. Target, a decade before Change Healthcare, was certified compliant with the payment-card security standard when it was breached. Equifax operated in a regulated industry with real requirements. Compliance regimes are valuable, they raise the floor for everyone, but a compliance mindset can actively harm security if it lets an organization mistake the paperwork for the protection. The job of good governance is to close that gap, not to celebrate the certificate.

Try it · Responsible or accountable?

Five people in a company that just had a patch fail. Say which word applies to each one before you check.

Responsible means they do the work. Accountable means they answer for whether it got done, whether or not they touched a keyboard. Several people can be responsible; only one can be accountable.

03Three failures, three broken links

Section 02 defined governance as the answer to four questions: who decides, who does the work, who checks it was done, and who answers when it was not. Each of the three cases below broke a different one of those links, and in each the technology was working exactly as built.

Mini-case 1 · 2003: the alarm that failed quietly

On the afternoon of 14 August 2003, a high-voltage line in northern Ohio sagged in the heat into overgrown trees and switched off. That is routine. Grids handle it constantly, and operators redistribute the load.

The fault should have raised an alarm in FirstEnergy's control room. A software fault in the alarm system meant it did not, and, more importantly, the alarm system gave no indication that it had stopped working. Operators sat looking at a screen that appeared normal. Over the next ninety minutes three more lines sagged into trees and dropped out, each one loading the remaining lines further. The operators had no alerts and no way of knowing their alerts had gone. By early evening the cascade had taken out much of the north-eastern grid: about 55 million people across eight states and Ontario lost power, some for days.

The investigation afterwards found the trees, the inadequate planning and the training gaps. But the governance finding is the one that changed the industry: before 2003, the reliability standards utilities were supposed to follow were voluntary. Afterwards Congress made them mandatory and legally enforceable, because it had become clear that a rule nobody has to follow and nobody verifies is not a rule.

The link that broke: nobody checked. The component: software. Note the specific shape, because it is the same as the expired certificate in section 04. The failure was not that a monitor broke. It was that a broken monitor looked exactly like a working one, and no person or process owned the question of whether it was still working.

Check yourselfName a check in your own life or work that would look identical whether it was running or not: a backup, an alert, a report you assume somebody reads. Then say how you would find out, today, whether it is actually working.

Mini-case 2 · 2020: the grades nobody would own

When examinations were cancelled in England in 2020, the regulator had to award A-level grades without exams. It built a model. Teachers submitted predicted grades and rankings, and the model adjusted them using each school's historical results, on the reasoning that teacher predictions alone would inflate grades nationally.

Read what that adjustment actually does. The model marked down a bright student in a school that had historically done badly, because of results achieved by people they had never met, some of whom had left years earlier. Roughly four in ten grades came out lower than the teacher had submitted, and the pattern fell hardest on large state schools while small classes, which the model treated differently, were largely protected. Students protested outside the department with placards, and within days the whole thing was withdrawn and teacher assessments used instead.

The interesting question is not whether the model was well built. It is who decided it should be used at all to make a decision of that consequence about individuals, and who could have stopped it. Ministers pointed at the regulator, and the regulator pointed at its instructions. The phrase that entered the language that summer was people blaming "the algorithm", which is precisely the sentence section 01 tells you is never an explanation.

The link that broke: nobody decided, so nobody could stop it. The component: data. The model's inputs were a school's past performance, which is a fact about a building rather than about the student being graded. Chapter 8 would call that a validity failure. This chapter calls it a governance failure, because somebody should have had both the authority and the obligation to ask whether that input was acceptable before a single grade was issued.

Check yourselfThe model was trying to solve a real problem: teacher predictions would have inflated grades. Propose an approach that addresses that problem without judging individuals by their school's history, and then name what your approach costs.

Mini-case 3 · 2021 to 2025: the convictions that had to be undone

Start at the end, because the end is happening now. From 2021 British courts began quashing the criminal convictions of subpostmasters, a statutory public inquiry was set up, and in 2025 that inquiry reported on the human damage. To understand why, go back to 1999, when the Post Office rolled out an accounting system called Horizon across thousands of branches. Each branch was run by a subpostmaster who was contractually liable for any shortfall in the accounts. The system reported shortfalls that were not real.

What happened next is the reason this case is in a governance chapter rather than a software one. The Post Office maintained that the system was reliable, and used that position to pursue the people the system was failing. More than seven hundred subpostmasters were prosecuted, many for theft or false accounting. People lost their businesses, their homes and their reputations; some went to prison; some died before the record was corrected. The organization had evidence of faults in the system and continued regardless.

A television drama in 2024 turned it into a national political issue, decades after the first prosecutions and years after the faults were documented. The inquiry's 2025 report set out the human damage, including that a number of people are believed to have taken their own lives. Compensation schemes are still running as you read this, and the rest of the inquiry's report, the part that says who was responsible, had still not been published by September 2026, a year after the first volume. That is worth noticing: the failure began before most of you were born and is not finished.

The link that broke: who answers. The component: people. Accountability did exist here, and that is what makes it the most disturbing case in the book. It was assigned, formally and contractually, to the subpostmasters. When the system was wrong, the structure was already built to conclude that the people must be.

Check yourselfSection 03 says accountability cannot be delegated away. The Post Office had delegated it, in writing, to the least powerful people in the chain. Say what test you would apply to an accountability arrangement to tell the difference between assigning it properly and offloading it.

What the three have in common

An alarm, a grading model, and a branch accounting system. In none of the three was the failure a mystery once anybody looked, and in all three the organization had a governance structure on paper.

What they share is that the structure did not answer one of the four questions. Nobody owned whether the alarm was alive. Nobody owned whether the model should be used. And in the third, the question of who answers had been settled in advance, in the organization's favor, against the people with the least power to argue.

Keep the third one especially close for the case that follows. Equifax is usually taught as a story about a missed patch, and it is. But it is also a story about a company holding data on a hundred and forty-seven million people who were never its customers and never agreed to anything. The question of who answers to whom had been settled long before anyone forgot to patch a server.

04Equifax: a patch nobody applied

In March 2017, a serious vulnerability was disclosed in Apache Struts, a piece of open-source software used inside countless corporate web applications. A fix was published the same week. Equifax, one of the three US credit bureaus, was told internally to apply it. The instruction went out through the company's patch management process, the routine that is supposed to track every known fix until it has landed. And then, on one internet-facing system that happened to run the vulnerable software, it did not get done. No alarm connected the order to the omission.

Beginning in May, attackers used that exact vulnerability to get in, and they stayed for about 76 days. They extracted the names, Social Security numbers, birth dates, and addresses of roughly 147 million people, close to half the US population. A device meant to detect the exfiltration had been inactive for months because an expired security certificate had never been renewed. Two boring omissions, a missed patch and a lapsed certificate, and a firm whose entire business was holding sensitive data handed nearly half a country's identities to strangers.

Why this is the governance chapter

Nothing about Equifax required advanced attackers, and this is the point. Every technical safeguard that would have stopped it already existed and was already understood. What failed was governance: the structure that assigns who must do what, who checks that it was done, and who answers when it was not. Chapter 10 was about controls. This chapter is about who is accountable for the controls, which turns out to be the harder problem.

05Data about people who never agreed

There is a fact about Equifax that makes its breach different in kind from a retailer losing card numbers, and it deserves to be stated plainly. The 147 million people whose data was exposed were never Equifax's customers. They never signed up, never agreed to terms, never chose to do business with the company at all. Equifax's customers are lenders. The people in its database are its product: their financial histories, collected without their direct involvement, packaged and sold to others.

This inverts the usual accountability relationship and it is the deepest governance question in the chapter. A normal company that loses customer data faces those customers in the market: they can leave. The people harmed by the Equifax breach had no such lever, because they were never in a relationship they could exit. You cannot boycott a company you were never a customer of. When the people bearing the risk are not the people the company answers to, the market cannot govern the outcome by itself. Governance has to come from somewhere else: regulation, law, and accountability assigned by force rather than by choice.

The other side: data about people who never agreed

Chapter 10 argued that breach costs are an externality, exported to people outside the company's books. Equifax pushes that argument to its limit, because the people bearing the risk never entered into anything at all.

A Social Security number cannot be reissued. The 147 million people in the Equifax breach carry a lifetime exposure to identity theft that no settlement retires. The roughly $700 million Equifax paid in 2019, split among restitution, credit monitoring, and penalties, works out to a few dollars of face value per person for a permanent risk. The company continues to operate and remains one of three firms that decide whether Americans can get a mortgage. The breach did not end the business, because the business does not depend on the goodwill of the people it holds data about.

Hold this against the second question of the course. "Does the system work?" Equifax's data system worked extremely well, at collecting, matching, and selling. The question this course insists on adding, "for whom, and at whose expense?", is the entire content of the Equifax story. It is why the answer to a governance failure this large is not only a better patch process. It is a public argument about who should be allowed to hold what data about whom, and under what obligations. That argument is where the next unit, on regulating technology, begins.

Key takeaways
  • Governance is what decides whether the five components are looked after at all. It sounds bureaucratic until you watch its absence.
  • Responsibility is the obligation to do the work. Accountability is the obligation to answer for the outcome, and it belongs to one named person or it belongs to nobody.
  • A framework turns vague intentions into auditable commitments. Compliance measures whether documented controls exist, not whether every real system is covered every day.
  • Equifax's patch failed in the space between teams. A fix existed, was known, was available, and had been ordered. Nobody was accountable that it landed.
  • When the people bearing the risk are not the people the company answers to, the market cannot govern the outcome. Governance has to come from somewhere else.

06Vocabulary

IT governance
The structures and decision rights that determine who directs, does, checks, and answers for technology.
Responsibility
The obligation to do the work.
Accountability
The obligation to answer for the outcome; cannot be shared away.
Patch management
The process ensuring known fixes actually reach every affected system.
Vulnerability
A known weakness attackers can exploit; the Struts flaw was one.
Framework
A structured, auditable set of accepted practices (e.g., NIST CSF, COBIT).
Compliance
Meeting a standard's requirements; a floor, not proof of security.
Audit
An independent check that controls exist and are followed.
Data stewardship
The duty of care owed by whoever holds data, especially about people who did not consent.
Externality
A cost borne by people outside the decision; central to both security chapters.

Name one recurring task at a place you have worked, studied or volunteered where you do not know who is accountable. Then work out, from the outside, how you would find out.

Think 3 min

Write the task in one line. Then write the four answers from section 01, and leave blanks where you do not know.

Pair 4 min

Swap. Your partner's job is to try to fill in your blanks by asking questions, and to notice which blank you cannot fill even with help.

Share 5 min

We collect a few on the board, sorted by which of the four questions was blank.

Where this landsTwo things come out reliably. The blank is almost always "who checks", because doing the work is visible and verifying it is not, which is exactly the shape of both the missed patch and the expired certificate. And students discover that finding the answer from outside is hard, which is the honest version of why these failures persist: the information needed to spot an ownership gap is itself ungoverned. Push on the second half of the question, because "ask my manager" is not an answer if the manager assumes somebody else has it. Carry the best example into the case and ask what it would look like if it failed for seventy-six days without anybody noticing.
