Chapter 11 · Software Design and Selection

Software: The Pizza You Could Not Order

Guillermo Robles is blind and uses software that reads a screen aloud. At least twice he tried to order a pizza from Domino's, on the website and in the app, and could not, because neither had been built to work with a screen reader. He sued, and the case reached the door of the Supreme Court. This chapter is about the two software decisions every business makes: how it is designed and how it is chosen. It is also about why the interface is the part of the system that decides who gets to be a customer.

Learning objectives

  2. Explain usability and accessibility, and why the interface decides who gets to be a customer.
  3. Apply the build, buy, or subscribe decision using the differentiator test and total cost of ownership.

1Software, and the interface that decides who gets in

Every chapter so far has treated software as one of five components, the one that holds the rules. This chapter looks at it from the only angle a customer ever sees: the surface where a person meets the system. That surface is where the software component touches the people component, and it is where most of a system's value is either delivered or lost.

Two decisions follow from that, and both belong to managers rather than programmers. The first is how software is designed, meaning what the interface lets a person do and who it lets do it. The second is how software is chosen, because most organizations buy far more of it than they build. The case at the end of the chapter is a company that built its own front door, for a process it had already made the center of its business, and forgot to ask who could open it.

1.1 Kinds of software, and where it comes from

Let's start with the plain inventory: what kinds of software a business runs, and the three ways it can get them.

Key conceptKinds of software, and where software comes from

System software, the operating systems and utilities that run the machine, versus application software, the programs that do the business's actual work: a register, a payroll system, a pizza-ordering app. Applications arrive three ways. Custom software is built for you, fits exactly, and costs the most to create and maintain. Packaged (off-the-shelf) software is bought and installed, cheaper, and fits approximately, which is why Chapter 8 spent a whole case on the configure-versus-customize decision. Software as a service (SaaS) is rented: it runs on the vendor's computers, you reach it through a browser, and you pay by subscription. Most new business software today is SaaS, for reasons that come down to cost.

Domino's ordering system sits at the expensive end: custom software, built and owned, because Chapter 4 established that the ordering pipeline is the business, exactly the situation where custom is worth it. Which makes the case sharper, not softer: this was not a neglected vendor product the company barely controlled. It was the software Domino's chose to build itself, and the gap in it was a gap in the company's own design decisions.

1.2 The interface decides who gets in

For the first decades of computing, the interface was a command line: a blinking cursor awaiting typed instructions in a syntax you had to already know. The population of people who could use a computer was, in effect, the population willing to memorize it. The graphical interface, windows, menus, a mouse, things you could recognize instead of recall, did not make computers more powerful. It made them usable by more people, and the personal-computing industry is the direct result. Hold that historical fact as a principle: every interface decision is a decision about who is in your market.

Key conceptUsability

Usability is not a matter of taste; it is a set of measurable properties. Learnability: how quickly can a new user do the core task? Efficiency: how fast is the task once learned? Error tolerance: how easily do users make mistakes, and how gracefully can they recover? You can put numbers on each, by watching real users attempt real tasks, which is what Lab 9 will have you do. The business stakes compound quietly. A confusing checkout leaks customers one abandoned cart at a time. Inside the company, hard-to-use enterprise software taxes every employee every day, a cost that never appears on the invoice of the system that causes it. The tax is measurable. One of this book's authors studied nurses using electronic medical records and found that the same system produced two different kinds of stress: the good kind, where a demanding tool feels worth mastering because it is useful and somebody helped, and the bad kind, where it feels imposed and pointless, which predicts wanting to leave the job. Usability is not a nicety. It is the difference between those two.

1.3 Accessibility is not a feature request

Usability asks whether a typical user can do the task. Accessibility asks the harder version: whether every user can, including the ones the builders never pictured.

Key conceptAccessibility and WCAG

Accessibility is usability's widest circle: designing software that works for people with disabilities, including blind and low-vision users on screen readers, deaf users needing captions, and people navigating by keyboard or voice because they cannot use a mouse. The World Wide Web Consortium's Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG) are the de facto standard, organized around four properties: content must be perceivable, operable, understandable, and robust. In practice much of it is unglamorous discipline: labeling images and buttons so a screen reader has something to say, making every function reachable by keyboard, maintaining contrast a low-vision user can read.

Two facts turn accessibility from charity into strategy. First, scale: the World Health Organization estimates about one in six people worldwide live with a significant disability, a market no retailer would knowingly lock out. Second, the curb-cut effect: features built for disability keep turning out to serve everyone, the way sidewalk curb cuts built for wheelchairs now serve strollers, suitcases, and delivery carts. Captions serve the noisy gym; voice interfaces serve drivers; high contrast serves your phone in sunlight. Accessible design is, reliably, just better design under constraints, and building it in from the start costs a fraction of retrofitting it under a court deadline, which brings us back to the pizza.

2How businesses choose software

Design is one software decision a business makes. Selection is the other, and by far the more common one, because most companies buy far more software than they build. Done badly it produces the systems everyone hates, and Chapter 8 showed you the nine-figure version. The professional process is unglamorous and learnable, and it ends with a question that decides how much of your company's technology you should own at all.

2.1 The selection process

Learnable is the word to notice. Nobody is born knowing how to buy software, and most managers never get taught. Here is the process:

Key conceptSoftware selection and total cost of ownership

Start with requirements, written before any vendor demo, split into must-have and nice-to-have, because a good demo will otherwise write your requirements for you. Shortlist, then run scripted demos: every vendor performs your tasks on your sample data, not their rehearsed happy path. Compute total cost of ownership (TCO): the license or subscription is often the minority of the real cost once implementation, integration, training, support, and the eventual cost of leaving are added. Check references from companies your size, then pilot with real users before committing. And after this chapter, one line belongs in every requirements document: conformance with WCAG, verified in the demo with a screen reader running, because section 4 establishes what that line is worth.

2.2 Build, buy, or subscribe

The strategic part of selection is the oldest question in corporate IT: build it, buy it, or rent it? The working rule falls straight out of Chapter 3. If the capability is a commodity, something every business does the same way, payroll, email, accounting, buy or subscribe, because custom-building it buys you no advantage and all the maintenance. If the capability is your differentiator, the thing customers choose you for, owning it can be worth the enormous cost. That is why Domino's built its ordering pipeline, and why Chapter 4's whole story exists. The judgment call is knowing which is which, and the honest test is Porter's: would doing this better than rivals actually change what customers do?

The rule scales from a co-op's point-of-sale choice to a $100 billion streaming service without changing shape, and it comes up again in the last chapter of this book.

Quick quiz · Build, buy, or subscribe?

Five software needs at the Cascadia co-op. Decide what it should do about each one before you check.

Ask two questions. Is this the thing customers choose us for? If yes, owning it can be worth the cost. If no, is it standard enough that a rented version exists? Subscribe if it is; buy a license if you need it on your own machines.

3Two interfaces that decided the outcome

Before the main case, here are two short ones. In each one, the system underneath worked exactly as built, and the interface decided the outcome anyway. That is the claim section 1 makes in the abstract; these are what it looks like when it costs something.

3.1 Mini-case 1 · 2000: the ballot that was hard to read

In the 2000 United States presidential election, Palm Beach County in Florida used a ballot that listed candidates on two facing pages with a single column of punch holes running down the middle. To vote for the second candidate on the left page, you punched the third hole, because the second hole belonged to the first candidate on the right page. It was designed that way to fit ten candidates in large type, by an election official trying to help older voters see the names.

The result was thousands of votes for a minor candidate whose name sat opposite the second hole, far more than he received in comparable counties. Many thousands more ballots were punched twice and discarded. The presidential election was decided in Florida by a margin of 537 votes.

In Palm Beach County, the voting machines counted correctly. The tabulation was accurate. The interface, a piece of card and a row of holes, determined what people were able to express. No test of the counting system would ever have caught that.

What decided the outcome: the layout. The component: hardware. This is the purest version of section 1's claim that every interface decision is a decision about who is in your market, and here the market was an electorate.

Check yourselfThe official who designed the ballot did it to make the names larger for older voters. Say what she should have done before printing it, using the vocabulary in section 1, and say why that step is so routinely skipped.
Show the answer

Usability, and specifically the property called error prevention. Larger type made each name easier to read, which is one usability goal, and the layout that larger type forced, names on two facing pages with one column of punch holes between them, made it easy to punch the wrong hole, which defeats the other. The ballot passed the test the designer ran (can you read it?) and failed the test she did not run (can you vote for who you meant to?). That is the general lesson: a design decision made to help one property can quietly break another, and the only way to know is to watch real people use the thing before it counts.

3.2 Mini-case 2 · 2024: the app that made the speakers stop working

One morning in May 2024, people who owned expensive speakers found that their alarms had not gone off, their sleep timers were gone, and in some cases the speakers would not respond at all. Nothing in the hardware had changed. Sonos makes wireless speakers that are controlled entirely through its app, and that month it released a rebuilt version of that app, timed to accompany the launch of its first headphones. The new app was missing features customers used every day: sleep timers, editing a queue, searching a local music library. Alarms did not fire. Some systems could not be controlled at all. And support for screen readers, which the previous app had, went backwards.

The Sonos hardware was unchanged and working. The speakers on people's shelves were exactly as good as the week before. What changed was the only door customers had into them, and the company's first response was to defend the redesign as a better experience.

Eight months later the chief executive resigned. By then the company had said fixing the app would cost between $20 million and $30 million. It had cut about six percent of its staff, reported a quarter in which revenue fell by around sixteen percent, and appointed an ombudsperson to publish reports on quality. It also said it could not go back to the old app, because the new one had changed the architecture underneath.

What decided the outcome: a launch date. The component: people. The app shipped when the headphones were ready rather than when the app was, and that was a decision made by named executives. Notice, too, that accessibility was the first thing to fall off, which is exactly the pattern section 1 describes and the case that follows turned into a lawsuit.

Check yourselfSonos had a testing process and shipped anyway. Using section 2's selection vocabulary, say what a scripted demo with real customers doing real tasks would have shown, and say why a company that knew that would still choose the launch date.
Show the answer

Testing tells you whether the software does what the specification says. It cannot tell you whether the specification left something out, and Sonos's left out the users who could not see the screen. The app passed its tests because screen-reader support was not in them; it had been treated as a feature rather than a requirement, so removing it broke no test. The gap is in section 2's selection and design step, before testing begins: the requirement was never written down as one, so nobody was responsible for it, and the process that was supposed to catch failures had nothing to catch. A test suite is only as complete as the list of people the product is for.

3.3 What the two have in common

A ballot and a mobile app. In both, the system underneath was correct: the votes were counted accurately, the speakers played. And in both, the interface decided what people could actually do, which is the only thing they experience.

Hold the second one especially close for the main case, because it is the same failure at a different scale. Sonos removed screen-reader support and lost a chief executive. Domino's never added it and ended up at the door of the Supreme Court. The interface is where a business meets a person, and it is the part of the system a court can see.

4Main case · Robles v. Domino's: the ADA meets the web

Let's begin with a pizza. Guillermo Robles tried to order one. He is blind, he uses software that reads a screen aloud, and Domino's website and app would not let him finish. Domino's, the company from Chapter 4, had rebuilt itself as "a tech company that happens to sell pizza," and by the mid-2010s a large share of its orders arrived through its website and app. That is the front door Guillermo Robles walked up to. His screen reader converts on-screen text and controls into speech, and it works only when software is built with the underlying labels and structure the reader needs. Domino's site and app were not. On at least two occasions he could not complete an order or claim an online-only discount, which meant the company's digital front door, its proudest system, was closed to him. In September 2016 he sued under the Americans with Disabilities Act.

Domino's defense is worth stating fairly, because your discussion questions will ask you to weigh it. The company made three arguments. The ADA, a 1990 law about physical places of public accommodation, does not reach websites and apps at all. No federal regulations existed telling businesses what an accessible website even is. And Mr. Robles was not shut out of Domino's, because he could always order by phone. Keep those three arguments in mind; a federal appeals court is going to answer each one, and the answers now shape how every business with a website gets advised.

4.1 What the software actually did to him

Start with the mechanics, because the whole case turns on them and they are not complicated. Mr. Robles used JAWS, the most widely used screen reader on Windows, and VoiceOver, the screen reader built into the iPhone. Both work the same way. They do not look at the picture on the screen the way you do. They read the labels and structure underneath it, and then speak them.

So a button works if somebody wrote a label on it. An image means something if somebody wrote alt text for it. A form field can be filled in if somebody connected the label to the box. When those labels are missing, the screen reader has nothing to say. It does not announce "this site is broken." It announces "button," or nothing, over and over, while the user tries to guess what the page contains.

That is what Domino's site and app did. The website's images lacked alt text. The app had buttons with no labels, which is a failure against Apple's own published guidance for iOS developers. Mr. Robles tried more than once, and he could not build his pizza and finish the order. He also could not claim a discount that existed only online, which matters legally as well as practically: the company was offering something to its digital customers that it was not offering to him.

Keep hold of what the fix would have been. Nobody was asking Domino's to build a separate site for blind customers, or to invent anything. The remedy is to label the controls and describe the images in the software the company already had. That is the same work Chapter 12 will call an accessibility requirement, and it is cheapest when it is part of how the team builds, rather than a project somebody funds later under a court order.

4.2 Three defenses, and what happened to each

Domino's made three arguments, and each one is a version of something you will hear in a real meeting. Take them one at a time.

"The law does not cover this." The ADA is a 1990 statute about places of public accommodation, and Domino's argued that a website is not a place. The Ninth Circuit answered with what lawyers call a nexus: the website and app connect customers to the goods and services of physical restaurants, of which Domino's had thousands, so Title III reaches them. Note the reasoning, because it generalizes past this case. Courts read a statute by what it was for, not by the technology that existed when it was written.

"Nobody told us what the rules are." This was the strongest argument, and the district court accepted it at first. There were no federal regulations defining an accessible commercial website, so, Domino's said, holding the company to an unwritten standard would violate due process. The appeals court rejected it, and the reasoning is worth keeping. The obligation in the statute is to communicate effectively with disabled customers. That obligation is the notice. A court can then order compliance with WCAG as the remedy for breaking it, without WCAG ever having been turned into a federal rule.

"He could have phoned us." Domino's argued that a telephone line meant Mr. Robles was not excluded. On the evidence in front of it, the district court did not accept that the phone was an adequate substitute. Think about what the argument actually asks. Everyone else may order at two in the morning, in thirty seconds, with the online-only price. You may call, wait, and pay more. An alternative channel is only equivalent if it delivers the same thing on the same terms, and the company's own pricing had already conceded that it did not.

The pattern across all three is the one to carry into your career. Each defense was a reason the company should not have to do the work, and none of them was a claim that the work was hard.

4.3 The ruling, and what it now binds

The district court initially sided with Domino's on procedure, reasoning that without federal web-accessibility regulations, courts should wait for the Department of Justice to define the rules. In January 2019 the Ninth Circuit Court of Appeals reversed, and its two holdings are the ones that matter. First: Title III of the ADA applies to the websites and apps of a business where they connect customers to the goods and services of its physical locations. Domino's, with thousands of stores, plainly had that connection, which the court calls a nexus. The court put it directly: the website and app facilitate access to the goods and services of a place of public accommodation. Second: the absence of formal regulations is not a due-process escape hatch; the statute's obligation to communicate effectively with disabled customers was itself fair notice. In October 2019 the Supreme Court declined to hear Domino's appeal, leaving the ruling standing, and the case later ended in settlement.

Notice the shape of the legal lesson, because you have seen it before. Regulators have taken the same position on AI hiring tools, which Chapter 12 returns to: existing discrimination law applies no matter how novel the technology. Here, a 1990 statute written before the web reached the web anyway, because courts read laws by their purpose, not by the technology of their era. "There are no rules for this yet" is one of the most expensive sentences in business, and the aftermath proved it. With the precedent standing, web-accessibility suits, already climbing, now number in the thousands per year. The practical standard companies are held to is the WCAG guidance that was freely available the whole time. The cheapest moment to meet it was before the lawsuit.

The interface is a legal surface. Domino's learned it from a pizza order; every business with a website inherited the lesson.

4.4 Six years, and what the record actually shows

Follow the calendar. Mr. Robles filed in September 2016. The district court dismissed in 2017. The Ninth Circuit reversed in January 2019. The Supreme Court declined to take the appeal in October 2019. In June 2021, back at the district court, the judge granted summary judgment for Mr. Robles on the website, ordered Domino's to bring it into line with WCAG, and awarded him $4,000 in statutory damages under California's civil rights act. The parties settled in June 2022, on undisclosed terms. Six years of litigation, over an order for a pizza.

Now a caution about a number you will meet the moment you search for this case. Many articles state that fixing the website would have cost Domino's about $38,000, usually next to an estimate of millions spent on lawyers. That comparison is irresistible and you should not use it, because the figure does not appear in the court record. It seems to have entered circulation through a single 2019 social media post and has been repeated ever since without a source. Domino's litigation spending, its remediation cost and the settlement amount are all undisclosed. The only dollar figure the public record establishes is the $4,000.

That is worth a moment in a course about information. The argument for accessibility does not need an invented number, and using one hands the other side an easy way to dismiss the whole point. What the record does establish is strong enough: a company spent six years and an undisclosed amount of legal effort defending its right not to label its buttons, and was ordered to label them anyway.

4.5 Since the ruling

The Ninth Circuit's decision stood, and the practical standard businesses are held to is the WCAG guidance that was freely available the whole time. Web-accessibility suits in the United States have run to several thousand a year since, many of them against small businesses that never imagined their website was a legal surface.

The law has moved too, though unevenly. In 2024 the United States Department of Justice issued a rule requiring state and local government websites and apps to meet a specific version of WCAG by April 2026. In April 2026, days before that deadline, it pushed the dates back a year, to 2027 and 2028, and said it might revise the rule itself. The National Federation of the Blind sued over the delay. The standard did not change and the private lawsuits did not pause: more than three thousand federal suits were filed against businesses in 2025 alone. In 2025 the European Union's accessibility act came into force, extending requirements to private-sector products and services including websites, apps and self-service terminals. And the Sonos case in section 3 shows the market's version of the same lesson. A company that went backwards on accessibility in 2024 lost customers, revenue and a chief executive without any lawsuit at all.

The direction is not in doubt, even where the deadlines slip. What was arguable in 2016 is settled in 2026: the interface is a legal surface, and the cheapest moment to meet the standard is before anybody has to ask.

4.7 What a business technology manager takes from this chapter

Strip the Robles case down to what you can reuse in any company. Four decisions came up in it, and each one is a decision you will be in the room for.

The interface is the system, as far as the customer and the court are concerned. Domino's counted votes correctly in the ballot case, played music correctly in the Sonos case, and took orders correctly on its website, and in every one the interface decided who could actually use it. When you review a system, review it from the outside, as the person who has to use it, because that is the only part they will ever see.

Write accessibility down as a requirement, not a feature. Sonos had a testing process and shipped an app that blind users could not operate, because screen-reader support was never a requirement, so no test covered it. A feature can be dropped in a sprint. A requirement has an owner and a test. Which one accessibility is at your company is a decision, and after Robles it is a legal one.

Decide build, buy, or subscribe by who is accountable for the result. Most companies buy far more software than they build, and the selection process is learnable. But whichever you choose, the company that puts the software in front of customers answers for what it does. Domino's did not write its screen-reader failure; it bought and configured its way into it, and the court did not care.

Ask who the system forgets. Every one of the cases in this chapter is about a user the designers never pictured: a voter who could not follow the layout, a blind customer, an employee stuck with the tools the company chose. That question costs nothing to ask before launch and a great deal to answer after, and it is the manager's question, because the developers are building what they were told.

5The downside: who software forgets

Robles is the visible case of a quiet, general pattern: software forgets the users its builders did not picture. Disabled users are the sharpest example, but the circle is wider. Older users meet interfaces tuned to reflexes trained by two decades of apps they never used. Low-income users meet sites built and tested on new phones over fast connections. Non-native speakers meet error messages written in idiom. None of this is malice; it is the default outcome of teams building for themselves, which is why deliberate practices, accessibility standards, usability testing with people unlike the builders, exist at all.

And one forgotten user lives inside the company itself: the employee. The person who selects enterprise software is almost never the person who will use it eight hours a day, and the gap shows. The warehouse worker fighting a fifteen-click process, the nurse clicking through screens designed by a billing department, pay daily for a selection decision they were never consulted on. Section 2's pilot step exists precisely to close that gap. The best single question to ask about any software purchase is the one this chapter has been circling: who was in the room when this was chosen, and who has to live with it?

Key takeaways
  • The interface is the part of the system that decides who gets to be a customer. Everything behind it can work and the front door can still be closed.
  • Accessibility is usability's widest circle, and the WCAG guidance was freely available the whole time Domino's argued there were no rules.
  • Build your differentiator; buy or subscribe to your commodities. Custom-building a commodity buys you no advantage and all the maintenance.
  • Write the requirements before the demo, or the demo will write them for you. Then pilot with real users doing real tasks.
  • "There are no rules for this yet" is one of the most expensive sentences in business. The law caught up, unevenly, and the private suits never paused.

Week 9

This chapter says usability is a set of measurable properties and accessibility is its widest circle. This week's lab hands you the instrument professionals use to measure them: the ten usability heuristics. You will learn all ten, go deep on three, find each one working or failing in a real interface, and write the short evaluation report a UX designer produces on the first day of a project.

Keep mini-case 3 beside you while you do it. Sonos had a testing process. The question is not whether you tested but who you tested with, doing what, and whether anybody had the authority to say the launch date moves.

9Sources and further reading
  • Robles v. Domino's Pizza LLC, 913 F.3d 898 (9th Cir. 2019), the Supreme Court's denial of certiorari in October 2019, and the district court's summary judgment of 23 June 2021, for the nexus holding, the fair-notice reasoning, the order to conform to WCAG, and the $4,000 in statutory damages under California's Unruh Act. The parties settled in June 2022 on undisclosed terms, and the claims about the mobile app were never decided on the merits.
  • A number to avoid. Many secondary accounts say remediation would have cost Domino's about $38,000. That figure is not in the filings reviewed for this chapter and appears to originate in a 2019 social media post. Domino's litigation spending, its remediation cost and the settlement terms are all undisclosed. Section 4.4 says so deliberately; if a student brings the figure to class, this is the source discussion to have.
  • Christopher B. Califf, Saonee Sarker and Suprateek Sarker, "The Bright and Dark Sides of Technostress: A Mixed-Methods Study Involving Healthcare IT," MIS Quarterly 44(2), 2020, pp. 809–856, for the two kinds of technology stress described in section 1.
  • Robles v. Domino's Pizza LLC, Ninth Circuit Court of Appeals, January 2019, and the Supreme Court's denial of certiorari, October 2019, for the two holdings in section 4.
  • Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG), World Wide Web Consortium, for the four principles in section 1. The guidelines themselves are readable and worth an hour.
  • Contemporary reporting and subsequent analysis of the Palm Beach County ballot in the 2000 election, for mini-case 1. The precise number of misdirected votes is contested; the design and the margin are not.
  • Sonos investor communications and reporting from May 2024 to January 2025, for the app release, the missing features, the remediation cost, the layoffs, the revenue decline and the chief executive's departure, for mini-case 3.
  • US Department of Justice, final rule on web and mobile accessibility under Title II of the ADA (April 2024), and the European Accessibility Act (in force June 2025), for the "since the ruling" material. Both are recent; check current deadlines before teaching them.
  • World Health Organization estimates on the global prevalence of significant disability, for the scale figure in section 1.
  • Robles v. Domino's Pizza LLC, 913 F.3d 898 (9th Cir. 2019): the holdings that Title III of the ADA applies to a website and app with a nexus to physical locations, and that the absence of regulations raises no due-process bar.
  • Reporting on the Supreme Court's denial of certiorari, October 7, 2019 (ABA Journal and others): the ruling left standing, and the anticipated rise in web-accessibility litigation that followed.
  • W3C, Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG): the perceivable, operable, understandable, robust framework that functions as the practical compliance standard.
  • World Health Organization, World Report on Disability estimates: roughly one in six people worldwide live with significant disability, the market-scale figure in section 1.
  • Coverage of the case's 2022 settlement (accessibility-industry reporting): the quiet ending, after the expensive route.
  • US Department of Justice, interim final rule of 20 April 2026 extending the Title II web-accessibility compliance dates to 26 April 2027 (large public entities) and 26 April 2028 (smaller ones); the National Federation of the Blind's suit over the delay; and law-firm tallies of 2025 web-accessibility litigation (more than 3,100 federal Title III suits), for the "Since the ruling" passage.
