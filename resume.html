Turning the labs into a resume

Every lab in this course produces something a hiring manager would recognize as work. Most students never write it down that way. This page shows you how.

The formula

The data analyst Karina, who writes a newsletter on data careers and has reviewed hundreds of CVs for hiring, gives one piece of advice to students with no job experience yet: you have done things, you just have not written them down properly. A class project is experience. A spreadsheet you built to answer a question is experience. The mistake is burying it in one line that says "group project for MIS class." Her formula for writing up any project is four parts:

What you did → how much data → what you found → what it means for the business

"Used Excel to analyze sales data" is true and weak. "Analyzed 12 months of retail sales (about 2,900 transactions) in Excel to find why member revenue was flat; found that two of eight stores accounted for most of the decline and recommended a targeted re-enrollment campaign" is the same work, written so that somebody would want to ask you about it. The whole guide is in her video for fresh graduates.

Three more of her rules that apply to you now, before you have a job title:

  • Do not call yourself an "aspiring" anything. You are a junior analyst who is still in school.
  • Take "teamwork" and "time management" out of your skills section. Everybody claims them. Use the space for tools: Excel, SQL, Tableau, and the specific functions you can write without looking them up.
  • Put the work somewhere a stranger can see it. A GitHub page, a personal site, or a folder of PDFs you can link to. The lab certificates and the files you submitted are that work.

Step back from the tool

Before you write a single bullet, here is the advice Prof. Califf gives every quarter. Do not write about the tool. Excel, Tableau, and SQL are what you happened to use; in five years some of them will be replaced and a recruiter already assumes you can learn a tool. What lasts is what you did with the data: the question you were asked, the way you decided which numbers would answer it, the pattern you noticed that nobody had pointed at, and what you told somebody to do because of it. That is analysis, and analysis is the thing that gets you hired and keeps you hired after the tools change.

So the formula stays the same, but the emphasis moves. Lead with the business question and what you found. Name the tool last, in passing, the way you would mention which car you drove to get somewhere.

Below is each lab written that way, using what the lab actually asked and the data you actually worked with. Cascadia Outfitters is a fictional company, so say so: "a simulated regional retail co-op" is honest and nobody will hold it against you. Use these as a starting point, not as text to paste. Rewrite each one in your own words, replace the findings with your own numbers, and cut anything you did not actually do. Recruiters see hundreds of resumes from the same programs, and a bullet copied from a course page reads like one; a bullet in your own words with your real result reads like you.

Lab 1 · GenAI orientation

Worked for a full quarter under a written AI-use policy, deciding assignment by assignment where AI would help my thinking and where it would replace it, and documented every use. Why it is worth a line: employers are asking how you use AI, and most applicants have no answer that involves judgment.

Lab 2 · First questions from a client

Answered a general manager's first questions about a year of sales at a simulated retail co-op (about 2,900 transactions): how much, how often, and what share of purchases came from loyalty members. Learned to read a question from a real person and decide which number actually answers it. (Excel.)

Lab 3 · Sharper questions

Broke a year of sales down by store, by product category, and by member status to answer targeted questions from the client, and caught a case where a misspelled criterion returned a confident zero, which taught me to check every number against what I expected before sending it. (Excel.)

Lab 4 · Choosing the right picture

Decided, for four different questions about the same sales data, which kind of chart would answer each one honestly (a comparison, a trend, a distribution, a share), built them, and wrote a one-line finding for each. Learned that the wrong chart can invent a finding that is not there. (Excel.)

Lab 5 · The board's question

Investigated why a retail co-op's member revenue was flat when every headline chart said the business was healthy. Produced the six numbers the board asked for from 12 months of transactions, found [your finding, for example: the member-attach rate had fallen in two of eight stores], and wrote a one-page memo recommending [your recommendation]. (Excel pivot tables.)

Lab 6 · A dashboard people can ask questions of

Designed an interactive dashboard so that a manager could click a region or a store and see every chart update, choosing which questions the dashboard should answer and which it should leave out. Used AI as a coach for tool questions and made every design decision myself. (Tableau.)

Lab 7 · Going to where the data lives

Wrote SQL queries against a database of eight related tables (sales, members, products, stores, suppliers) to answer questions the exported spreadsheets could not, including joining sales to the members who made them to find [your finding]. Learned to think about how facts are related before asking for them. Note the difference from the other bullets: SQL is not a tool that will be replaced next year. It is the language every database has spoken for forty years, and being able to write it as a beginner is worth saying plainly.

Lab 8 · Deciding what to trust

Evaluated seven inbound messages to a company, some of them attacks, by checking each one's claims against company records instead of judging by tone, classified what each attack was after, and recommended the first security control a board with no budget should put in place. (No tools; judgment.)

Lab 9 · Seeing what an interface does to people

Evaluated a real app against the ten standard usability heuristics, documented where it helped and where it trapped its users, and wrote a short evaluation report of the kind a UX designer produces on the first day of a project. (Nielsen's heuristics.)

Lab 10 · Deciding what belongs where

Ran a card-sorting exercise to find out how people actually group a set of items, compared that with how the organization had grouped them, and built a category model that fit the users rather than the org chart. (Information architecture.)

The skills section

Write the analysis skills first, because those are the ones that last: turning a question from a manager into the numbers that answer it; checking a number against what you expected before trusting it; choosing a chart that tells the truth; finding the pattern a headline hides; writing a recommendation somebody can act on; deciding what to trust. Then SQL, on its own line, because it is a language rather than a product and a recruiter reads it differently: "SQL (beginner: SELECT, WHERE, GROUP BY, JOIN)" is an honest, specific claim. Then the tools, and here is the thing about tools: naming one tells a recruiter almost nothing, because everyone lists Excel. What you did with it is the claim. So not "Excel," but "Excel: summarized a year of transactions with pivot tables to answer a board's question, and built charts chosen to fit the question rather than the data." Not "Tableau," but "Tableau: designed an interactive dashboard a manager could filter by store and region." A recruiter who reads it in that order sees an analyst who can talk to a database and can make a tool answer a real question, which is the right way round.

The evidence

Each lab ends with a completion certificate. Keep the PDFs. Keep the workbooks, the dashboard file, and the memo from Lab 5. If you put them in one folder you can link to, you have a portfolio, and a portfolio is what turns a bullet on a page into something an interviewer can open.

A folder is fine. A website is better, and this is a good place to use AI the way Lab 1 taught you to. Open Claude or ChatGPT and ask something like: "I would like to build a simple portfolio website to showcase the work I have done in college at WWU. I have Excel workbooks, a Tableau dashboard, a one-page memo, and some PDFs. Walk me through the options and help me build it." It will help you pick a free host, lay out the pages, and write the code, and it will explain each step if you ask. Two rules. The bullets and the descriptions on the site are yours, in your own words, for the reason above. And every file you post should be one you made, with the company noted as simulated where it was. A working link at the top of a resume, pointing at real work, is worth more than any line on it.
