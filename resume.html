Turning the labs into a resume and a portfolio

Every lab in this course produces something a hiring manager would recognize as work. Most students never write it down that way, and fewer still show it to anyone. This page shows you how to do both.

The formula

The data analyst Karina Samsonova, who writes a newsletter on data careers and has reviewed hundreds of CVs for hiring, gives one piece of advice to students with no job experience yet: you have done things, you just have not written them down properly. A class project is experience. A spreadsheet you built to answer a question is experience. The mistake is burying it in one line that says "group project for MIS class." Her formula for writing up any project is four parts:

What you did → how much data → what you found → what it means for the business

"Used Excel to analyze sales data" is true and weak. "Analyzed 12 months of retail sales (about 2,900 transactions) in Excel to find why member revenue was flat; found that two of eight stores accounted for most of the decline and recommended a targeted re-enrollment campaign" is the same work, written so that somebody would want to ask you about it. The whole guide is in her video for fresh graduates.

Three more of her rules that apply to you now, before you have a job title:

  • Do not call yourself an "aspiring" anything. You are a junior analyst who is still in school.
  • Take "teamwork" and "time management" out of your skills section. Everybody claims them. Use the space for what you can actually do: the questions you can answer with data, the language you can write (SQL), and what you have built with the tools you know.
  • Put the work somewhere a stranger can see it. A GitHub page, a personal site, or a folder of PDFs you can link to. The lab certificates and the files you submitted are that work.

Step back from the tool

Before you write a single bullet, here is the advice Prof. Califf gives every quarter. Do not lead with the tool. Excel and Tableau are what you happened to use; in five years some of them will be replaced, and a recruiter already assumes you can learn a tool. (SQL is the exception, and the Lab 7 bullet says why.) What lasts is what you did with the data: the question you were asked, the way you decided which numbers would answer it, the pattern you noticed that nobody had pointed at, and what you told somebody to do because of it. That is analysis, and analysis is the thing that gets you hired and keeps you hired after the tools change.

So the formula stays the same, but the emphasis moves. Lead with the business question and what you found. Name the tool last, in passing, the way you would mention which car you drove to get somewhere.

Below is each lab written that way, using what the lab actually asked and the data you actually worked with. Cascadia Outfitters is a fictional company, so say so: "a simulated regional retail co-op" is honest and nobody will hold it against you. Use these as a starting point, not as text to paste. Rewrite each one in your own words, replace the findings with your own numbers, and cut anything you did not actually do. Recruiters see hundreds of resumes from the same programs, and a bullet copied from a course page reads like one; a bullet in your own words with your real result reads like you.

Lab 1 · GenAI orientation

Worked for a full quarter under a written AI-use policy, deciding assignment by assignment where AI would help my thinking and where it would replace it, and documented every use. Why it is worth a line: employers are asking how you use AI, and most applicants have no answer that involves judgment.

Lab 2 · First questions from a client

Answered a general manager's first questions about a year of sales at a simulated retail co-op (about 2,900 transactions): how much, how often, and what share of purchases came from loyalty members. Learned to read a question from a real person and decide which number actually answers it. (Excel.)

Lab 3 · Sharper questions

Broke a year of sales down by store, by product category, and by member status to answer targeted questions from the client, and caught a case where a misspelled criterion returned a confident zero, which taught me to check every number against what I expected before sending it. (Excel.)

Lab 4 · Choosing the right picture

Decided, for four different questions about the same sales data, which kind of chart would answer each one honestly (a comparison, a trend, a distribution, a share), built them, and wrote a one-line finding for each. Learned that the wrong chart can invent a finding that is not there. (Excel.)

Lab 5 · The board's question

Investigated why a retail co-op's member revenue was flat when every headline chart said the business was healthy. Produced the six numbers the board asked for from 12 months of transactions, found [your finding, for example: the member-attach rate had fallen in two of eight stores], and wrote a one-page memo recommending [your recommendation]. (Excel pivot tables.)

Lab 6 · A dashboard people can ask questions of

Designed an interactive dashboard so that a manager could click a region or a store and see every chart update, choosing which questions the dashboard should answer and which it should leave out. Used AI as a coach for tool questions and made every design decision myself. (Tableau.)

Lab 7 · Going to where the data lives

Wrote SQL queries against a database of eight related tables (sales, members, products, stores, suppliers) to answer questions the exported spreadsheets could not, including joining sales to the members who made them to find [your finding]. Learned to think about how facts are related before asking for them. Note the difference from the other bullets: SQL is not a tool that will be replaced next year. It is the language every database has spoken for forty years, and being able to write it as a beginner is worth saying plainly.

Lab 8 · Deciding what to trust

Evaluated seven inbound messages to a company, some of them attacks, by checking each one's claims against company records instead of judging by tone, classified what each attack was after, and recommended the first security control a board with no budget should put in place. (No tools; judgment.)

Lab 9 · Seeing what an interface does to people

Evaluated a real app against the ten standard usability heuristics, documented where it helped and where it trapped its users, and wrote a short evaluation report of the kind a UX designer produces on the first day of a project. (Nielsen's heuristics.)

Lab 10 · Deciding what belongs where

Ran a card-sorting exercise to find out how people actually group a set of items, compared that with how the organization had grouped them, and built a category model that fit the users rather than the org chart. (Information architecture.)

The skills section

This is the one place on the resume where you do name the tools, plainly and by their exact names, because most resumes are read by software before a person sees them, and that software is looking for words. A bullet that says "summarized a year of transactions" is better for the person; a skills line that says "Excel" is what gets the resume in front of the person. You need both.

So the skills section has three parts. First, the analysis skills, because those are the ones that last: turning a question from a manager into the numbers that answer it; checking a number against what you expected before trusting it; choosing a chart that tells the truth; finding the pattern a headline hides; writing a recommendation somebody can act on. Second, SQL on its own line, because it is a language rather than a product: "SQL (beginner: SELECT, WHERE, GROUP BY, JOIN)" is an honest, specific claim. Third, the tools by name, each with one clause about what you did: "Excel (pivot tables, SUMIF family, charts); Tableau (interactive dashboards with linked filters)." The scanner finds the names. The person reads the clause.

Build a portfolio, not just a resume

A resume tells someone what you did. A portfolio shows them. When a recruiter can open your dashboard, read your memo, and see the question you started from, a bullet on a page becomes work they can judge for themselves. The interview then starts from what you made instead of from what you claim. Most business students apply with a resume alone, which is the best reason to build one. It is the easiest way to stand out from a stack of applicants with the same major and the same courses.

One site for all of college, starting with MIS 320

Build the portfolio once and keep adding to it. The site has a short header about you and your skills, then a section for each class that produced work worth showing, with a card for each project. MIS 320 is the first section. Next quarter, when another class gives you a project you are proud of, you add its section. By graduation the site shows four years of work, and the way it got better.

Write every project card as a short case study, in the same order as the formula at the top of this page: the question somebody asked, the data you had, what you did, and what you found. Then add one thing a resume has no room for: what you would do next if the project kept going. That last line shows judgment, which is what an interviewer is trying to see.

Build it with AI, in an afternoon

You do not need to know how to write a website to build one, and you do not need to pay for anything. The free version of Claude can do all of it. This is a good place to use AI the way Lab 1 taught you to: as a coach that does the parts you have not learned yet, while every claim on the page stays yours.

  1. Gather your work. Put the files from the labs you chose in one folder: the memo as a PDF, the link to your dashboard on Tableau Public, your SQL queries, your reports, and screenshots. Write two or three sentences of notes for each: the question, what you found, and what you would do next.
  2. Fill in the prompt below, then paste it into Claude or ChatGPT. Replace every bracketed part with your own details: your name, email, LinkedIn link, and the rest of the "About me" list at the top, and the labs you chose and your notes at the bottom. Leave an optional line blank if it does not apply. The AI will build the page and ask you for anything it needs.
  3. Make it yours by talking it through. The first version is a starting point, not the finished site. Keep the conversation going and tell the AI what you want the site to look and feel like: "make it feel more professional," "the cards look crowded on my phone," "I want it to feel like a design portfolio, not a spreadsheet," "add a small photo of me in the header." When you are stuck, ask for ideas: "what would make this stand out to a recruiter in finance?" or "give me three different color schemes to choose from." It is good at recommendations, so let it suggest, then decide for yourself.
  4. Check every word. Read the page as a recruiter would. Change anything that is not in your own voice, and delete anything you did not actually do.
  5. Look at it on your laptop. Save the file as index.html and open it in your browser. Ask the AI to fix anything that looks wrong, on a laptop or a phone.
  6. Publish it. Put it on GitHub Pages, which is free. The AI will walk you through it, or use the step-by-step guide below the prompt. You will get a web address you can share.
  7. Link it everywhere. Put the address at the top of your resume and in the Featured section of your LinkedIn profile.
  8. Add a card every quarter. When a class gives you work worth showing, ask the AI to add a new class section, and paste in your notes the same way.

Optional: step-by-step guide to publishing on GitHub Pages (about 15 minutes, no coding)

GitHub is a free website where people store files, and GitHub Pages turns those files into a public website. You only need a web browser. Nothing here involves code.

  1. Make a free account. Go to github.com and sign up. Pick your username carefully, because it becomes part of your web address. Something close to your real name, like janedoe, looks best to a recruiter.
  2. Make a new repository. A repository is just a folder for your website. Click the + in the top right corner, then New repository.
  3. Name it exactly right. In the name box, type your username followed by .github.io. If your username is janedoe, type janedoe.github.io. Set it to Public, then click Create repository.
  4. Upload your files. On the next page, click uploading an existing file, or Add file, then Upload files. Drag in your index.html and any PDFs and images it links to. The website file must be named index.html. Then click the green Commit changes button, which just means save.
  5. Turn on the website. Click Settings near the top of the page, then Pages in the menu on the left. Under Source, choose Deploy from a branch. Under Branch, choose main, leave the folder as / (root), and click Save. If it is already set this way, you do not need to change anything.
  6. Wait a few minutes, then visit your site. Go to https://janedoe.github.io, using your own username. It can take up to ten minutes to appear the first time.
  7. To update it later, go back to the repository, click Add file, then Upload files, and drag in the new version of index.html. It replaces the old one, and your site updates within a few minutes.

If something looks different or goes wrong: GitHub moves its buttons around from time to time. Take a screenshot, paste it into your AI conversation, and ask, "Where do I find this now?" It is very good at this. If your site shows a 404 page, the two usual causes are a repository name that does not match your username exactly, or a website file that is not named index.html.

Then add one project that is only yours. The labs show that you can work from a brief. One project you chose yourself shows that you can find a question. Pick a public dataset about something you care about, from sports statistics to data.gov. Then take it through the same steps the labs taught: a clear question, data you cleaned and checked, a chart that tells the truth, and a recommendation somebody could act on. Give it its own card.

Four rules for anything you post

Your own words. The descriptions on the site are yours, for the same reason the resume bullets are. Say what was simulated. Cascadia Outfitters is a fictional company, so label it that way. Show your work, not an answer key. Post your memo, your dashboard, your queries, and your findings, but not a step-by-step solution to the lab instructions, so the next class still has to do its own thinking. Nothing private. Never post real personal data, or anything from an employer, without permission.

Keep the completion certificate from every lab, along with the files themselves. Even before the site exists, a single folder of your best work is a portfolio, and a working link at the top of a resume, pointing at real work, is worth more than any line on it.
