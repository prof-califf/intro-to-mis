Chapter 6 · Databases

Databases: How Walmart Built a Company on the Shape of Its Own Records

Every register in ten thousand stores writes down what just happened. That is not unusual; every retailer does it. What Walmart did was decide what to record, arrange it so questions were cheap to ask, and then hand the answers to its own suppliers. This chapter is about why the arrangement matters more than the facts.

Take the receipt from your last coffee or grocery run and count the facts on it. A store number, a date, a time, a register, a cashier. Then for every item a name, a quantity, a unit price, a line total. Then subtotal, tax, payment type, the last four digits of a card. A modest receipt easily holds thirty separate facts, and Walmart produces receipts like it at a rate of roughly 280 million customer visits a week.

1What data actually is

Chapter 2 defined data by its role: raw recorded facts, the bottom rung of a ladder that climbs to information and knowledge. This chapter asks the more physical question. What is the stuff itself, and what has to be true of it before anyone can ask it anything?

Notice where this sits in the five components. Data is the one component that is not bought and not built, but accumulated, one recorded fact at a time, according to rules somebody decided in advance. Hardware and software can be replaced over a weekend. The records cannot, because they are a history, and a history is the one thing money cannot buy in a hurry. That is Chapter 3's argument arriving from the other direction, and it is why this chapter comes before the ones on analytics and AI: those are things you do to data, and they inherit every decision made here.

Key conceptData

Data is any recorded representation of a fact: a number, a piece of text, a date, a true/false flag, an image, a sound. Two properties matter more than any others. First, data is recorded: a sale that no system wrote down generates no data, no matter how real the sale was. Second, data has a type: the same marks on a screen mean different things depending on whether a system treats them as a number, text, or a date, and most data disasters begin with a type being wrong.

Data comes in kinds, and the kinds determine what you can do with it:

Quantitative data is numeric and supports math: quantities, prices, temperatures, durations. Qualitative data is descriptive and supports grouping: a category, a state, a product name, a customer complaint. In your Cascadia work, revenue is quantitative and store name is qualitative, and you have already noticed that pivots treat the two completely differently: you sum one and group by the other.

Key conceptStructured, unstructured, and semi-structured data

Structured data fits a fixed grid of rows and columns, where every row has the same fields and every field has a type: transactions, inventory counts, member records. It is the easiest data to query and the kind databases were invented for. Unstructured data has no fixed grid: emails, reviews, photos, call recordings, social posts. It is most of the data in the world by volume and historically the hardest to use; the AI systems in Week 10 exist largely because they made unstructured data usable. Semi-structured data sits between: it has labels but not a rigid grid, like a JSON order from a website where one order has a delivery note and the next does not.

Walmart works all three. The transaction stream is structured. The company also analyzes on the order of a hundred million social media keywords to hear what people near each store are saying, which is unstructured text being mined for structure. The skill of a data organization is largely the skill of moving facts rightward: from unstructured, to labeled, to fully structured, because structure is what makes questions cheap to ask.

Records, fields, and why format matters

Structured data has an anatomy. A record is one complete fact-bundle: one sale, one member, one product. A field is one attribute inside it: the date, the quantity, the price. In a grid, records are rows and fields are columns. This vocabulary sounds trivial until you watch it fail.

You are about to watch it fail. In this week's lab, the controller's RAW export hands you three of these. Prices stored as text with dollar signs, so they would not sum. Quantities that were blank instead of zero, which are not the same thing. And Washington spelled fourteen different ways, so groups split that should have merged. Every one of those problems is a format problem: the fact was fine, the representation was wrong. "March 3, 2025," "3/3/25," and "2025-03-03" are one fact wearing three costumes, and software, which matches representations rather than facts, treats them as three different days.

The lesson under Lab 7

The cleaning this week's lab walks you through, standardizing formats, fixing types, resolving fourteen Washingtons into one, is exactly the discipline a database enforces automatically. A database column declared as a date refuses "March-ish"; a column declared numeric refuses "$18.00" with the sign. The mess you cleaned existed because the data was born in a system without rules. This chapter is about the systems with rules.

2Why one grid breaks, and what replaces it

So far the facts have been sitting in a list. The natural next instinct is to put them all in one giant sheet: every sale, with the store's name and address and the product's name and price and supplier repeated on every row. For a few hundred rows that works, and everyone in this course has done it. Then it breaks, in three ways that have textbook names and one that does not.

Before the mechanics, the reason this is a management topic and not a technical one. The structure you choose decides which questions are cheap to ask and which are effectively impossible, and nobody downstream can undo it. An analyst with a wrong-shaped database cannot analyze their way out of it, and neither can a machine learning model. In five-component terms, a bad data design makes data the weakest component, and the weakest component rule from Chapter 1 says the rest of the system is then limited by it however good it is.

The natural first instinct is to put everything in one giant sheet: every sale, with the store's name and address and the product's name and price and supplier repeated on every row. For a few hundred rows it works. Then it breaks, in three predictable ways that have textbook names.

First, update anomalies: the Bellingham store's phone number lives on every one of its thousands of sale rows, so changing it means changing it thousands of times, and missing one means the sheet now disagrees with itself. Second, insertion anomalies: you cannot record a brand-new product until it has sold at least once, because product facts only live inside sale rows. Third, deletion anomalies: delete a store's only sale and you have also deleted everything you knew about the store. The single grid ties facts together that have different lifetimes, and the knots become errors.

There is also a blunter limit: one grid is edited by one person at a time and tops out around a million rows. Walmart's registers write millions of new rows an hour, from thousands of locations at once. No sheet survives contact with that.

The relational model

Key conceptThe relational database

A relational database stores each kind of fact in its own table, one table for stores, one for products, one for sales, and connects them with keys. A primary key is a field that uniquely identifies each record in its table: store_id, product_id, sale_id. A foreign key is a field in one table that holds another table's primary key. Each sale row carries a store_id and a product_id, which is how a three-field sale row can "know" the store's address and the product's supplier without repeating them. Asking questions across tables by matching keys is called a join, and it is the move that makes the whole model work.

Run the receipt through it. The one big grid becomes a sales table (thin rows: date, store_id, product_id, quantity, price), a stores table (one row per store, with the phone number stored exactly once), and a products table (one row per product, with its category and supplier). Each fact now lives in one place, updates happen once, and a new product can exist before it sells. The grid's three anomalies are not patched; they are structurally impossible.

This is not an abstraction you have to take on faith. The join builder lets you draw these key connections against the real Cascadia database, and Lab 7's final checkpoints, finding a supplier's products and a store's real problem, are joins you will write yourself.

Where the tables came from, and the decade nobody wanted them

The model in this section is so standard now that it looks like a discovery about the nature of information rather than somebody's idea. It was somebody's idea, and it was resisted for years by the company best placed to build it.

In June 1970, Edgar Codd, a mathematician at IBM's research laboratory in San Jose, published a paper called "A Relational Model of Data for Large Shared Data Banks." Its argument was that data should be stored as simple tables of rows and columns, related to each other by values, and that programs asking questions should not have to know how any of it was physically stored. You should be able to describe what you want rather than how to go and get it.

At the time, databases worked the other way. A programmer navigated to data by following pointers through a hierarchy, which meant every question had to be written against the storage layout, and changing the layout broke the programs. IBM sold a very successful product built exactly that way. Its research division had just published a paper explaining why that approach was the wrong one.

IBM did eventually build a relational system, and its research project produced the query language that became SQL, the one you will write this week. But the company took years to bring a product to market, and it was a young outsider who read Codd's paper, took the idea seriously and shipped first, founding the company that became Oracle. Codd's employer had the paper, the researchers, the customers and the money, and still arrived late, because it already sold something the paper made obsolete.

Two things worth taking from that. The first is that the thing you are about to be taught as self-evident was argued for, against opposition, by a named person, in living memory. The second is a pattern you will meet again in Chapter 12 and in the AI chapter. The organization with the most to gain from an idea is frequently the one with the most to lose from it, and being right early is worth nothing by itself.

Normalization

Key conceptNormalization

Normalization is the design discipline of splitting data into tables so that every fact is stored exactly once, in exactly one place. The working rule: every field in a table should describe the thing the table's primary key identifies, and nothing else. A store's phone number describes the store, so it belongs in the stores table, keyed by store_id, and nowhere else. Normalize well and consistency stops being a virtue you maintain and becomes a property the structure guarantees. Normalization has formal levels, called normal forms, that a database course will drill; for this course, the instinct matters more than the formalism: if the same fact appears twice, the design is wrong.

Normalization has a cost, and honesty requires naming it. A perfectly normalized database scatters a simple question across many tables, so answering "revenue by state last October" means joining sales to stores to a calendar. That is why analysts often build deliberately flattened copies of the data for reporting, accepting some duplication in exchange for fast questions. The design goal differs by purpose, which is the door into the next two sections: one shape for running the business, another for understanding it.

3Three short failures of shape

Before the case, three short ones. In every one of them the facts existed, somebody needed an answer from those facts, and the answer was blocked by the shape the facts were stored in rather than by the facts themselves. They are separated by 130 years, which is the point. This is not a modern problem, and it did not stop being a problem when computers got fast.

Mini-case 1 · 1890: the census that would have taken thirteen years

The United States Constitution requires a population count every ten years, and by the 1880s the counting had stopped working. The 1880 census was recorded on paper schedules and tallied by hundreds of clerks by hand, and the final tabulations were not finished until 1887. The population was growing faster than the clerks could count it. Officials estimated that the 1890 census, done the same way, would take about thirteen years, which meant the results would arrive after the census that was supposed to replace them.

Herman Hollerith, who had worked on the 1880 count, proposed something that sounds obvious now and was not. Give every person a card, and give the card a fixed set of positions, where a hole in a particular place means a particular fact. Sex here. Age there. Occupation there. A machine passes pins through the card, the holes complete circuits, and counters advance.

Look at what that actually is. One card is a record. Each position on it is a field. Every card carries the same fields in the same order, which is what lets a machine handle them at all. Hollerith invented the row of a table forty years before there was a computer to put it in.

The Census Bureau ran a competition in 1888 to decide who got the contract. Capturing the test data took the two other entrants 144.5 and 100.5 hours; it took Hollerith 72.5. Preparing that data for tabulation took them 44.5 and 55.5 hours; it took Hollerith 5.5. The gap on the second task is roughly tenfold, and the second task is the one this chapter is about, because it is not recording facts, it is getting answers out of them. A rough national count for 1890 came back in about six weeks. The company Hollerith founded on the proceeds eventually became IBM.

What the shape decided: whether the question could be answered at all. The component: data, at the level of the field. The 1880 data was not wrong. It was unstructured, and an unstructured fact is one that a person has to read.

Mini-case 2 · 1999: two digits

For decades, programmers stored years as two digits. 1965 was 65. This was not carelessness. Storage was scarce and expensive, two characters per date across millions of records was real money, and the people making the choice reasonably assumed their software would be retired long before the century turned.

It was not retired. As 2000 approached, the problem was that a system storing years as two digits cannot tell 2000 from 1900, so any calculation involving a span of time might produce a negative number, a nonsense result, or a confident wrong answer. Governments and companies spent enormous sums auditing and correcting code. The reason so little went wrong on the night is that a great deal of work went into it beforehand, which is an awkward kind of success to point at.

Notice exactly what was wrong. Not the fact: the year was known. Not the logic: the programs calculated correctly given what they were told. The representation was wrong, and it was wrong in a way that was invisible for thirty years and then wrong everywhere at once.

What the shape decided: how long the answer stayed correct. The component: data, at the level of the type. A format decision made in 1965 by somebody with a good reason, on an assumption about how long the system would live, became everybody else's problem in 1999.

Check yourselfSection 1 says data has a type and most disasters begin with a type being wrong. Name one field in a system you use where the storage decision embeds an assumption about the future, and say what happens on the day that assumption stops being true.

Mini-case 3 · 2020: the rows that fell off the bottom

During the pandemic, positive coronavirus test results in England traveled from testing laboratories to the contact tracing service through Public Health England. The laboratories sent results as text files. PHE loaded them into a spreadsheet template and passed the spreadsheet on.

The template was saved in the old .xls format, which has a hard limit of 65,536 rows. Each test result occupied several rows, so the real ceiling was far lower. When a file reached the limit, the additional rows were not rejected with an error. They were silently discarded.

Between 25 September and 2 October 2020, 15,841 positive cases were never passed to contact tracers. The people who tested positive were told. Their contacts were not, and by the time the gap was found most of them were past the window in which isolating would have helped. Reporting at the time put the number of untraced contacts near 48,000. A later economics study estimated that the delay was associated with more than a hundred thousand additional infections; treat that figure as an estimate rather than a count, because it is modeled rather than observed.

This is section 2's argument about the single grid, at national scale, in a week. A spreadsheet is one big grid. It has a row limit, no rules about what a column may contain, no way to enforce that a result belongs to a person, and, crucially here, no obligation to complain when it drops something. A database would have refused the row and said so.

What the shape decided: whether anyone found out. The component: software. The failure was not that data was lost. It was that data was lost quietly, and a tool with rules is a tool that objects.

Check yourselfYou will spend this quarter working in spreadsheets, and this chapter is about databases. Name two properties a database has that a spreadsheet does not, and then name one situation where a spreadsheet is still the right choice.

What the three have in common

Put them side by side. In 1890 the facts had no structure, so no one could count them. In 1999 the structure was chosen sensibly and outlived the assumption underneath it. In 2020 the wrong container was used for the volume, and it failed without saying so. Different centuries, different technologies, one finding: the facts were never the problem.

There is a second thing they share, and it matters more for your career than the first. In all three cases the decision that caused the failure was made by a person, early, for a reason that was sound at the time, and it was made long before anyone could see the consequence. Somebody chose the fields. Somebody chose two digits. Somebody chose the file format. Those are not technical decisions that happen to have management consequences. They are management decisions that happen to be expressed in technology, and the chapter you are reading is about how to make them well.

4Walmart: a company built on a database

Imagine being a supplier in 1991 and being handed a login. Behind it is what sold, of your products, in every Walmart store, yesterday. No retailer had ever shown a supplier that. Walmart's founding advantages were about trucks and locations. Its modern advantage was built on the ideas in this chapter, and the pivotal move was one most companies would have considered unthinkable: it gave its data away.

Four numbers set the scale of what that decision produced:

~10,900stores worldwide
~280Mcustomer visits weekly
200+data streams in the Data Café
40 PBof recent transaction data

In 1991 the company launched Retail Link, a system that shares Walmart's own point-of-sale data with its suppliers. A supplier like Procter & Gamble can see how its products are selling, store by store, day by day, and manage its own inventory against real demand instead of against guesses. Chapter 7, which you meet on Wednesday of this same week, covers the bullwhip effect: demand distortion amplifying up a supply chain because each link guesses at what the next one will need. Retail Link attacks the bullwhip at the root by replacing the guesses with shared records. Suppliers plan against actual sales, Walmart's shelves stay stocked without warehouses of padding, and the whole chain runs on one version of the truth.

Whether giving away your most valuable data is genius or madness is your Monday activity, and it is a open question. What is not in question is the scale of what grew from it. Walmart's analytics hub, the Data Café, draws on more than 200 internal and external data streams, including roughly 40 petabytes of recent transaction data. Reporting from the mid-2010s described the company building a private cloud handling 2.5 petabytes an hour. The numbers age quickly; the design principle does not: every register is a sensor, and the database is the instrument the whole company reads.

One story from the Data Café makes the point better than the petabytes. A grocery team watched sales in one category slump and could not explain it. Drilling into the data showed a pricing error: the products were listed higher than intended. The fix was trivial once seen. A company analyst put the underlying principle plainly, noting that insights arriving after a week of analysis are sales already lost. That is Chapter 2's timeliness dimension, operating at Walmart scale: the same information, a week late, is worth a fraction as much.

From records to answers: analytics

Everything to this point is about storing facts correctly. Analytics is about asking the facts questions, and the two jobs pull database design in opposite directions.

This is also where the remaining components arrive. A normalized database is useless sitting still: the networks carry every register's writes to the center and carry Retail Link's answers back out to thousands of suppliers, and the people are the ones who think of the question. The Data Café did not notice the pricing error. A grocery team did, and then had somewhere to look.

Key conceptTransactions vs. analytics (OLTP and OLAP)

An operational system, formally OLTP, online transaction processing, records events as they happen: a register writing sale rows, a website placing orders. It handles millions of tiny writes and must never lose one. An analytical system, formally OLAP, reads huge sweeps of history to answer questions: revenue by region by month, attach rate by store. Companies typically copy data from the first into a separate data warehouse shaped for the second, because a query scanning two years of sales should never slow down the registers recording today's. When you pivot in Excel, chart in Tableau, or write a GROUP BY in SQL, you are doing analytics; the database underneath did the transaction processing first.

Seen through this lens, the second half of your course is one continuous story. Week 6 asked what to measure and what happens when an industry measures the wrong thing. This week supplies the machinery those measurements come from. Your Tableau dashboard and your SQL queries are analytics running on structured, normalized, joined data, and the reason your questions are cheap to ask is that someone designed the tables so they would be. Analytics is not a separate world from databases. It is what databases are for.

Where this has gone since, and it is not nostalgia

Retail Link has run for more than thirty years and still does, as the portal where suppliers manage orders. What Walmart retired, in 2024, was the reporting inside it. Supplier analytics moved onto a newer platform, launched in 2021 as Luminate and renamed Scintilla in 2025, built by a division created in 2021 called Walmart Data Ventures. The name changes are not the interesting part. Three things about the move are.

The data became a business. In 1991 sharing point-of-sale records was a supply-chain decision that happened to involve data. There is now a division whose job is to create value from Walmart's records, and access to the good tiers is a product suppliers pay for. Chapter 3 argued that accumulated data is the component nobody can buy. Walmart's answer, three decades later, is to sell carefully chosen views of it while keeping the accumulation.

The question changed from "how did I do" to "what should I do." The old platform reported what had happened. The new one adds recommendations. In 2025 Walmart began adding AI tools on top: a conversational interface so a supplier can ask the data a question in ordinary language, automatic summaries of customer survey results, and suggested actions rather than tables. That is the ladder from Chapter 2, climbing one rung in public. It is also why this chapter comes before the ones on analytics and AI: none of those tools would have anything to work with if somebody had not decided in 1991 what to record.

The scope widened. The platform now covers customer behavior and perception across roughly 140 million households, not just what moved off a shelf, and it is expanding into Canada and Mexico.

Hold that beside section 5 before you decide how you feel about it. The same joins that let a supplier see which stores are underperforming let somebody infer what a household is going through. The capability is identical; only the question differs. And notice that nothing in this paragraph required a new invention. It required thirty years of somebody having decided, correctly, what was worth writing down.

5What the basket reveals

A store's transaction database looks like the least sensitive data imaginable: no health records, no messages, just groceries. The discomfort arrives when you understand what joins and history do to innocent-looking records.

A single basket says little. A year of baskets, keyed to one identity, says a great deal: a household's size, schedule, budget, diet, religion by what is never bought, health by what suddenly is. Retail analytics openly works this inference, and the industry's most famous cautionary tale is about exactly this. In 2012 the New York Times reported that Target's statisticians had built a model that guessed, from purchases like unscented lotion and supplements, which shoppers were pregnant, and mailed them baby coupons. A father in Minneapolis complained to a store manager that his teenage daughter was receiving them. She was pregnant. The store had known before he did. Nothing in that story required surveillance in the spy-movie sense. It required exactly what this chapter teaches: clean records, a stable key per customer, and a join across time.

The lesson is not that databases are sinister. It is that the power to join is the power to reveal, and the same design choices that make honest questions cheap make invasive ones cheap too. A company that holds well-structured data about people holds a capability, and Chapters 10 and 11 are about what happens when that capability leaks, and who is supposed to govern it. The second question of this course, what does the system make easier, and for whom, applies to a schema as much as to an algorithm.

Key takeaways
  • Data is not collected, it is designed. The shape you record facts in decides which questions are cheap, which contradictions are possible, and who can answer without asking you.
  • One big grid works for a few hundred rows and breaks at scale, because every repeated fact is a place for two copies to disagree. Normalization puts each fact in exactly one place.
  • A key is a promise: this value identifies exactly one thing. Most data failures in this chapter were broken promises about keys and formats, not broken software.
  • Registers write data; analysts read a cleaned copy of it. Keeping the two apart is why a heavy query never slows a sale.
  • Walmart's data became a business because a supplier could answer its own questions from Walmart's records without asking anyone. The data component carried the load; the other four made it deliverable.

Since 1991, Walmart has shared its point-of-sale data, the record of what actually sells, store by store, with its suppliers through Retail Link, at no charge. Chapter 3 spent a week arguing that hard-to-copy information is the root of competitive advantage. Walmart hands its information to thousands of outside companies on purpose.

Where this landsThe trap is treating "the data" as the asset. The data ages worthless in weeks; the asset is the pipeline that makes more, and Walmart shares the product while keeping the machine. Sharing also moves inventory work and risk onto suppliers, and it deepens their dependence on Walmart's shelf. The strongest counter is that suppliers selling through rivals carry Walmart-grade demand intelligence with them. Both sides survive scrutiny, which is why the switching conditions matter more than the verdict.

Monday session plan · 80 minutes · concepts, with the three short failures as illustrations
0:00OpenHold up a receipt, real or on a slide. Ask how many separate facts are on it and count them out loud with the room. The number climbs past twenty fast, and everything in the session hangs off that pile of facts.
0:05LectureWhat data is: recorded, typed, quantitative against qualitative, then structured against unstructured. Then records and fields, and the point that format is not the same as fact. "March 3, 2025" and "3/3/25" are one fact in three costumes, and software matches representations rather than facts.
0:16LectureWhy one grid breaks. Draw the receipt as a single wide sheet on the board and walk the three anomalies on it, pointing at the actual cells. Then draw it becoming three tables with keys. This is the single most important drawing of the session; do it slowly and let them copy it. The figure in section 2 is the same drawing if you would rather project it, but drawing it live is better, because the room watches the repetition disappear.
0:30LectureNormalization as one rule, that every field describes the thing the key identifies. Then name the cost plainly: a normalized database scatters a simple question across tables, which is why analysts build flattened copies. That tension sets up OLTP against analytics on Wednesday.
0:38LectureCodd in 1970 and what IBM did with it. Keep it to four minutes. The point is not the history, it is that the model students are about to treat as obvious was resisted for a decade by the company best placed to build it.
0:42Cases 1–3The census, two digits, and the rows that fell off the bottom, about three minutes each. Name what the shape decided in each one and stop. The comparison is the activity. If the room is quick, the 1888 competition timings are worth putting on the board: 5.5 hours against 44.5.
0:52LectureChapter 7 at speed: ERP, CRM, SCM, silos, the bullwhip effect. The bridge is one sentence, that enterprise systems are what happens when a company insists on one database instead of twenty-seven.
