You have used AI every day for years, long before anyone called it that. This chapter explains what AI is, why it is an information system and not just a clever piece of software, what it is good for, what it costs, and who decides which of those you get.

Learning objectives

By the end of this chapter you should be able to:

  1. Explain what makes software AI rather than a set of written rules, and why your social media feed counts.
  2. Describe an AI system as an information system, using all five components, and locate a failure in the right component.
  3. Tell predictive, generative, and agentic AI apart, and say whether a system is a tool, autonomous but controlled, or uncontrolled.
  4. Explain how machine learning works, explain why a model is weakest in the long tail of its data, and identify training-data bias, proxy discrimination, and feedback loops.
  5. Explain what a large language model does, why it hallucinates, and what grounding and agents change.
  6. Weigh AI's benefits against its costs, and describe what AI governance and a human in the loop look like in practice.

1What AI is, and why your feed counts

Chapter 6 was about choosing the right measure and putting it where decisions get made. This chapter is about what happens when you hand the measuring to a machine that works out its own rules. Start with the plain version, because the marketing makes it sound harder than it is. Artificial intelligence (AI) is software that learns patterns from a large amount of data, then uses what it learned to make a judgment or to make something new.

1.1 Rules versus learning

For most of computing's history, people wrote the rules by hand. Tax software applies rules a person typed in from the tax code. A spreadsheet's lookup formula does exactly what the formula says. Every behavior is a line somebody wrote, and when the result is wrong, you can find the line and fix it.

Machine learning (ML), the approach behind nearly everything now called AI, flips that. Instead of writing rules, you show the system many examples, and it finds the patterns itself. A spam filter is the classic case. Nobody writes down every trick a spammer will try. The filter learns from millions of emails people marked as spam, and it keeps learning every time you click "mark as spam."

The difference matters for business because it moves responsibility. A rule-based system does what someone wrote. A learned system does what its data taught it, and that lesson lives in millions of numbers that no manager can read. Nobody at TikTok wrote a rule that says "show this person otter videos." The preference was learned from behavior, and nobody at TikTok can point to the place in the model where it is stored either. The opacity is harmless when the stakes are otters. Much of this chapter is about what happens as the stakes rise.

1.2 Five moments that got us here

AI did not arrive in November 2022. It arrived in pieces, over more than a decade, and most of it arrived without the name. Five moments are worth knowing, because each one added something the next one built on.

  • 2012 · ImageNetA research team trained a neural network on a million photos, each labeled by hand with what was in it. It won an image-recognition contest with an error rate of about 15 percent, against about 26 percent for the next-best entry. Computers had learned to see, and they had learned it from people's labels, mistakes included.
  • 2012 to 2018 · The feedYouTube began ranking recommendations by how long people kept watching instead of what they clicked. Instagram and Twitter replaced the timeline in posting order with a ranked one in 2016. TikTok reached the United States in 2018 with a feed that was nothing but a model. Almost nobody called any of it AI.1
  • 2016 · AlphaGoGoogle DeepMind's AlphaGo beat Lee Sedol, one of the best Go players alive, four games to one. It got that good by playing millions of games against itself. Its own games became its training data.
  • 2022 · ChatGPTOpenAI released a chatbot built on a large language model. Within about two months an estimated hundred million people had used it. AI became something you could talk to.2
  • 2025 to now · AgentsCompanies began giving language models tools and letting them take actions: editing code, issuing refunds, booking travel. AI moved from answering to doing.

Every moment on that list is the same recipe with more data and more computing behind it. Data goes in. A model finds the pattern. Out comes a judgment or a creation, and what happens next becomes more data.

1.3 "The algorithm" is AI

The second moment is the one most people skip, and it is the one you know best. Early social media showed you posts in the order they were posted. Then the platforms replaced that order with a prediction. The model behind the feed learned from what you lingered on, liked, shared, and came back to. It also learned from what millions of people like you did. Then it ranked every possible post by how likely you were to keep watching.

Everyone called this "the algorithm." That word makes it sound like a fixed formula somebody wrote, like the tax software. It is not. The YouTube sidebar, the Instagram Explore page, and TikTok's For You page are machine learning models, retrained constantly on the behavior of their users. They pass the only test that matters: does it learn from data, or does it follow rules somebody wrote? By that test, the first AI most people ever lived inside was a social media feed, and it was deciding what they saw for a decade before ChatGPT made "AI" a household word.

The same is true of a lot of ordinary software: the fraud text from your bank, the arrival time in Google Maps, the rows on your Netflix home screen. The name on the box does not matter. The recipe does.

2AI is an information system, not just a model

When people say "the AI," they usually mean the model: the trained piece of software that turns an input into an answer. That is one box in a much bigger picture, and the picture is the one this course has drawn since Chapter 1. An AI system is an information system. It needs all five components, and like any information system it is only as strong as its weakest one.

2.1 The model is one box

A model on its own does nothing. It has to be trained on data, run on computers, connected to the people and apps that use it, and attached to a decision somebody acts on. The figure shows the whole arrangement.

2.2 The five components of an AI system

Here is each component in an AI system, with TikTok's For You page as the example, because you have used it.

  • Hardware. AI runs on specialized chips called graphics processing units, packed into data centers. Most companies rent this hardware from a cloud provider, which is Chapter 13's subject. For TikTok, it is the data centers that train the model and answer every swipe.
  • Software. The model itself, plus the programs around it that collect the data, feed the model, and show the result. For TikTok, the ranking model and the app that displays its top pick.
  • Data. What the model learned from, and what it is fed every day. This is the component that decides what the system will conclude. For TikTok, every view, pause, replay, and swipe from more than a billion people.
  • Networks. The connections that carry data in and answers out. For TikTok, the link that sends your swipe to a data center and the next video back to your phone.
  • People. The people who decide what the system is for, gather its requirements, choose and label the data, build and tune the model, fit it into the work, act on its output, and govern it once it runs, answering for it when it fails. At TikTok, somebody decided that watch time was the thing to maximize. You are in the people component too. Your behavior is the data.

One thing in that list deserves a second look. Hardware sets what you can afford, and networks set who you can serve, but neither decides what the system concludes. That is decided by the data, and the data was chosen by people. So the weakest-component rule applies here in an uncomfortable way. In earlier chapters a weak component made a system slow, unreliable, or blind. In this one a weak data component makes the system confidently wrong in a way that looks like an answer, and no amount of quality in the other four corrects it.

2.3 Why this changes where you look

Almost every failure in this chapter happens outside the model. Amazon's hiring model learned exactly as designed; the problem was the data. Air Canada's chatbot had no one responsible for what it said; the problem was the process and the people. So when somebody tells you about "our AI," ask about the whole system. Where does the data come from? What runs it? What does it output, who acts on that output, and who checks whether it is right? None of them needs a line of code to answer.

This is also why Chapter 5's systems analysis and design matters more with AI, not less. A model can build a prototype in an afternoon, but it cannot tell you what the business actually needs. Somebody still has to talk to the people who will use the system and write down what a good answer looks like. Somebody has to decide which mistakes are acceptable, who reviews the output, and how the work will change on the day it launches. Chapter 5's lesson holds with more force here. A system can only be tested against what somebody specified, so an AI system whose requirements were never written down will be confidently wrong in ways nobody thought to check. Requirements, implementation, and governance stay with people. They are the parts of an AI project that decide whether it works.

3Three kinds of AI, and how much freedom each gets

"AI" is not one thing, and the first professional habit is to ask which kind you are dealing with, because each kind fails differently. The second habit is to ask how far it is allowed to go on its own before a person sees anything.

3.1 Predictive, generative, and agentic

Predictive AI takes in data and outputs a judgment: this purchase looks like fraud, this resume fits, you will keep watching this video. It is the oldest kind and by far the most common, and it is the kind most likely to be judging you without your knowing. Generative AI produces something new, such as text, images, audio, or code, by predicting what plausibly comes next. It is what most people now mean when they say "AI." Agentic AI takes actions. It is given a goal, uses tools to reach it, and decides its own next step, such as sending the email or issuing the refund. A chatbot that tells you how to change a flight is generative. One that changes the flight for you is agentic.

3.2 Tool AI is most of the AI you use

Now ask the second question. Tool AI does the task people give it, inside limits people set, and then stops. A person decides what happens next. Your spam filter is tool AI, and you can drag any message back out of the folder. So is the fraud alert that waits for you to confirm, the model that predicts how a protein folds, and a chatbot that answers your question and then waits for the next one. Most of the AI in your life, and most of the good news in this chapter, is tool AI. It can be very powerful and still be a tool.

3.3 Autonomous but controlled: how agents are deployed

An agent is different. It is given a goal instead of a task, and it picks its own steps. That makes it autonomous: it goes some distance on its own before a person sees anything. But autonomy is only one dial. The other is control: whether anyone can notice what the system is doing, stop it, and undo it. The two dials move separately.

Here is what a controlled agent looks like. A customer service agent reads a complaint, checks the order, and issues a refund. It can refund up to $50 on its own. Anything over that, or anything unusual, goes to a person. It can touch one system, the order database, and nothing else. Every action is logged, someone reviews the log, and there is an off switch. That agent is highly autonomous and tightly controlled, and it is the kind of agent most companies are actually putting to work. A driverless taxi is the same idea on the road: nobody in the driver's seat, but every mile logged and crashes reported to regulators. The leash is a management decision, not a property of the AI.

3.4 Autonomous and uncontrolled: the July 2026 test

The clearest example of the dangerous corner comes from a test. Before an AI company releases a powerful model, it checks what the model could do in the worst case. In the spring and summer of 2026, OpenAI was running agents built on two of its models through a test of their hacking skills. Because the test measured attack skill, OpenAI switched off the safeguards that normally make its models refuse that kind of work. The agents were autonomous on purpose, because autonomy was what was being measured.

What was missing was control. The test environment, called a sandbox, had one link to an internal company system. The agents found it, used it to pass messages to each other, and eventually used it to reach the internet. In July, hundreds of them broke into Hugging Face, a widely used site where developers share AI models, looking for the answers to their own test. Nobody told them to. Hacking the scorer was simply one way to raise the score. Hugging Face's own monitoring caught the intrusion and disclosed it on July 16. OpenAI did not learn that the attacker was its own agents until days later, and the two companies announced it together on July 21. By OpenAI's own later account, staff had seen signs of the agents' message board weeks earlier and let the run continue.3

Notice what failed. Not the model being "too smart." The people around it switched off the guardrails, left one door open, and were not watching. That is uncontrolled, not uncontrollable: people could have stopped the agents and eventually did. A truly uncontrollable system, one that nobody could stop even if they tried, has not been built. The same agent can sit in either corner of the figure, and people decide which. Section 9 comes back to that decision.

4How a model learns, and how it learns the wrong thing

Every kind of AI in section 3 is built on machine learning. This section says what that machine is doing, in plain terms, how to tell whether it works, and the three ways it goes wrong without anybody writing a bad instruction.

4.1 Learning from labeled examples

Supervised learning is the way most business machine learning works: show the system a large set of past cases where the answer is already known, and let it work out what predicts the answer. The past cases are the training data. Each one carries a label, the outcome you want predicted. The model adjusts itself until its predictions match the labels, and then you use it on new cases, which is called inference.

TikTok's For You page is the example you know best. The training data is videos, described by their content and by who watched them. The label is whether a person kept watching. Every time you swipe, the model predicts which of many candidate videos you will keep watching and shows you the winner, and your swipe becomes the next labeled example. Amazon's "customers who bought this also bought" works the same way. It learns from millions of past purchases through a method called collaborative filtering, which its engineers published in 2003.4 Its sibling, unsupervised learning, finds structure with no labels at all. It could, for example, sort Cascadia's members into groups nobody had defined in advance.

Compress the mechanism into one sentence and keep it: a supervised model is a machine for making the future resemble the labeled past. When the past is what you want more of, that is a superpower. The rest of this section is about when it is not.

4.2 Deep learning: billions of dials

Nearly everything currently sold as AI is built on deep learning. A neural network is a structure built from layers of simple units. Each unit takes numbers from the layer before it, does one small calculation, and passes the result forward. Deep learning just means a network with many layers, sometimes hundreds.

Take the simplest job: telling a cat from anything else. You show the network a picture and tell it "cat." You show it another and tell it "not a cat." You do that a few thousand times. Nobody writes down what a cat looks like. The network works out which features went with the label, because those reliably came with "cat" in the pictures it saw. The network does not know what a cat is; it knows what cats tended to look like in one pile of pictures. And you chose the pile.

4.3 The long tail: where the data runs thin

Every model comes with an accuracy number, and the number is usually high. The professional habit is to ask a different question: where did the examples come from, and where are there almost none? Data is never spread evenly, and a model is only as good as the examples it saw.

In 2004, the Wired editor Chris Anderson gave a name to a shape he saw in online stores like Amazon and Netflix.31 A few hits sell enormously. Behind them trail thousands of items that each sell only now and then. He called that trail the long tail. Statisticians had studied the shape for decades, and once you know it, you see it everywhere: a few songs get most of the plays, a few words make up most of what people say, and a few diseases get most of the research.

Here is why it matters for prediction. A model learns from examples, so it learns the head of the curve well, because that is where most of the examples are. Out in the tail, it has seen a handful of examples or none. It does not say "I don't know." It gives an answer that looks exactly as confident as every other answer. An overall accuracy number cannot warn you, because the head swamps it: a model can be right for nearly everyone in the head and much worse for the people in the tail.

You have probably met this yourself. In 2020, Stanford researchers tested the speech recognition systems from Amazon, Apple, Google, IBM, and Microsoft on recorded interviews. On average, the systems got about 19 words in 100 wrong for white speakers and about 35 in 100 wrong for Black speakers, nearly twice as many mistakes. The authors traced most of the gap to training data with too little African American English in it, and their proposed fix was more varied training data, not a cleverer model.5

Medicine has the starkest long tail of all. In the United States, a rare disease is one that affects fewer than 200,000 people. There are thousands of them, and together they affect an estimated 25 to 30 million Americans, about one person in ten. Only about 5 percent have a treatment approved by the Food and Drug Administration.32 Each one alone is too small a market to pay back the cost of developing a drug. That is why Congress passed the Orphan Drug Act in 1983, which offers drug companies tax credits and seven years of market exclusivity for treating rare diseases.33

Public research money does not fix the gap on its own, because it tends to go where it went before. A 2021 study of National Institutes of Health spending across 46 diseases found that what each disease received was based mainly on what it had received more than ten years earlier, even as the burden of disease changed.34 Money produces studies, and studies become data. So the diseases that are understudied stay understudied, and any model trained on medical research inherits the shape of the funding. Where the research is thin, the model's silence comes out as a low score, and a low score looks like an answer.

So for any model, ask three questions. Where is the data thick, and where is it thin? Who lives in the thin part? And when the model is wrong about them, will anyone notice? The main case in section 8 shows what those questions look like for one patient at the far end of the tail.

4.4 Training-data bias and proxy discrimination

The most common way a model goes wrong is the simplest. If the examples it learned from carry a skew, it learns the skew as if it were a rule, and it keeps applying it after the humans who produced the examples have been told to stop.

Training-data bias is a skew in the examples that the model faithfully learns and repeats. The model is not malfunctioning, which is what makes it dangerous. Proxy discrimination is the sequel. Remove a forbidden attribute, such as gender, and the model finds stand-ins that go along with it: a word, a college, a zip code. A company can delete the one stand-in it noticed, but it can never prove there is no subtler one among tens of thousands of terms.

One management lesson follows. "The algorithm decided" is never an explanation. A model's decisions are its training data's decisions, run through arithmetic. The engineers were responsible for building it, but the managers who chose the data and approved the system are the ones who answer for it.

4.5 The feedback loop

Now take the feed one step further. The For You page does not just learn from your behavior; it shapes the behavior it will learn from next. It serves what held your attention, your attention becomes the data, and the data narrows what gets served. In 2021 the Wall Street Journal ran automated accounts through TikTok and showed that watch time alone steered fresh feeds into narrow tunnels within hours. Accounts that lingered on sad content were fed darker and darker versions of whatever had held their attention.6 Engagement is a stand-in for satisfaction, the stand-in became the target, and Chapter 6 told you the rest.

Raise the stakes and the same loop stops being recoverable. Predictive policing systems forecast where crime is likeliest and send patrols there. More patrols in a neighborhood observe more offenses there, which enters the data as more recorded crime, which raises the forecast, which sends more patrols. The prediction makes its own evidence, and the people it is applied to cannot log off.

A feedback loop exists when a system's outputs change the data it will learn from next. The test to carry into any AI project is one question: does acting on this prediction change the data that will judge the prediction? If yes, the model can be confidently wrong and keep confirming itself.

5Generative AI and agents

Section 4 was about models that sort and score. Generative AI produces something new instead, usually text, and it is the kind of AI you have used all quarter under the Lab 1 rules. This section explains what it is actually doing, why it makes things up, and what changes when it starts taking actions.

5.1 What a large language model actually does

A large language model (LLM) is a deep learning model trained to do one thing: predict the next piece of text. During training it reads an enormous amount of writing, and for every passage it guesses the next word, checks the real one, and nudges its billions of dials. The label in section 4.1's sense is simply whatever word came next. After that, people rate its answers, and the model is trained further to give the kinds of answers people rated as helpful.

When you ask it something, it writes the reply one piece at a time, each time choosing a likely next word given everything so far. That is the whole mechanism, and it explains both what LLMs are good at and how they fail.

Notice what is missing from that process. There is no step where the model looks anything up. It produces text that resembles the text it was trained on, and on well-covered questions, resembling the training text and being true are nearly the same thing. On a question about one airline's refund rules, or a person who is not famous, they can come apart, and the model has no way to notice.

5.2 Hallucination, and grounding

A hallucination is fluent, confident output that is simply wrong: an invented citation, a policy that does not exist, a statistic nobody measured. It is not a bug in the usual sense. It is section 5.1's mechanism working as designed on a question where the likely answer and the true answer differ. You met it in Lab 1, in the calculator test, as a confident wrong total.

The main defense is grounding: giving the model the real documents or records it should answer from, inside the request, and instructing it to answer only from those. Instead of asking the model what an airline's refund policy is, the system fetches the policy page and asks the model to answer from it. Grounding does not make a model honest. It changes the question from "what sounds right?" to "what does this document say?", which a language model is much better at.

You met a grounded system in Chapter 1. Spotify had a language model write about 1.4 billion short Wrapped stories, each grounded in one user's listening records. Even so, a timezone bug in the data pipeline made some stories celebrate the wrong day, and the model wrote a convincing story anyway.7 The model was faithful to the data, and the data was wrong. Spotify's engineers said that at that scale, the call to the language model was the easy part. The data pipeline, the grounding, and the checking were the hard part, and all three are MIS work.

5.3 Agents: when the model takes actions

An AI agent is a system in which a language model does not just answer but acts. It breaks a goal into steps and uses tools to carry them out: searching, sending an email, updating a record, placing an order. Section 3 showed that the danger is how much the agent can reach and how rarely a person looks.

An assistant's mistake is a wrong sentence somebody can ignore. An agent's mistake is an action, already taken, and a mistake in step two is carried forward into every step after it. Chapter 4 asked you to decide which steps of a process to hand to an agent. Here is the rule underneath that decision. Give an agent the authority you would give a new employee in their first week: real work, a short list of things it may never do alone, and a person who reviews anything that cannot be undone.

5.4 Rent the model, or build it

Chapter 11's build, buy, or subscribe question applies to models too. Most businesses rent a model from one of a handful of companies and shape it with a prompt, the instructions and grounding documents sent with every request. Some go further with fine-tuning, training it on examples of one job, or distillation, training a smaller, cheaper model to imitate a big one. What a business owns is its data, its prompts, and its way of checking the results.

TikTok is the exception, and it shows why. Rivals copied TikTok's short videos easily. They could not copy the recommendation model trained on its users' behavior, and that model was the business. When the United States forced a sale of TikTok's American operations, the hardest question was who would control the algorithm. Under the deal that closed in January 2026, ByteDance licensed it to the new American company, which is retraining it on American users' data.8 When the model is what customers choose you for, you own it. When it is not, you rent it and own what you feed it.

6The upside: AI as a complement

A lot of what you hear about AI is about jobs disappearing. That question is real, and section 10 takes it on directly. But it is not the whole story, and so far it is not the best-supported one. The strongest evidence points somewhere else: AI is most useful as a complement to people, something that makes a person's skills worth more, not a substitute that makes them unnecessary.

6.1 In science and everyday life

AI flags card fraud in the moment between swipe and approval, which is why your bank texts you about a strange charge before you leave the store. Models trained on labeled medical scans screen for eye disease in clinics that have no specialist. DeepMind's AlphaFold predicted the shapes of essentially every known protein, work that shared the 2024 Nobel Prize in Chemistry, and biologists now start from those shapes instead of spending years finding them. Live captions and translation have become everyday accessibility tools for hundreds of millions of people. In every one of these, a person is still doing the work. They are just doing more of it, faster, or for people they could not reach before. The main case in section 8 is one of the best examples there is.

6.2 Co-intelligence: you plus the model

Wharton professor Ethan Mollick calls this co-intelligence: working with AI the way you would with a capable, fast colleague who is sometimes confidently wrong. You bring the judgment, the context, and the responsibility. It brings speed, breadth, and a first draft of almost anything. One of his rules for doing it well is one this chapter keeps coming back to: be the human in the loop.9

Two studies show what that looks like in real work. In the first, economists followed more than 5,000 customer support agents at a software company as an AI assistant that suggested replies was rolled out. Agents with the assistant resolved about 14 percent more issues per hour. The newest and least experienced agents gained the most, about 34 percent, because the assistant passed along the habits of the company's best agents. Customers were happier with the conversations, and agents with the assistant were more likely to stay in their jobs.10 In that study the assistant helped people rather than replacing them, and the new hires got good faster.

In the second, researchers from Harvard and other schools worked with Boston Consulting Group to give 758 of its consultants realistic consulting tasks, some with GPT-4 and some without. On tasks the AI was good at, consultants using it finished about 12 percent more tasks, worked about 25 percent faster, and turned in work rated about 40 percent higher in quality. But on one task chosen to sit just outside what the AI could do, consultants using it were 19 percentage points less likely to get the right answer than those working alone. They trusted a confident wrong answer.11

The researchers called the line between those two kinds of task the jagged frontier. AI is excellent at some tasks and poor at others that look just as hard, and the line is rarely where you would guess. That is the co-intelligence lesson in one sentence: AI makes a skilled person better inside the frontier and a careless person worse outside it. Knowing where the line falls in your own work is the skill, and no model can learn it for you.

6.3 In every part of the business

Here is what co-intelligence looks like in the jobs this college prepares you for. In each one, AI takes the tedious part, and the person spends more time on the part that needed a person all along.

Accounting. Models read invoices and receipts, match them to purchase orders, and flag journal entries that look unusual, so an auditor can look at every odd entry instead of a random sample. The auditor spends less time ticking boxes and more time deciding whether an entry is fraud, an error, or a legitimate one-off, and still signs the opinion that says the books are fair.

Finance. In 2023 Morgan Stanley gave its financial advisors an assistant built on an OpenAI model and grounded in roughly 100,000 of the firm's own research reports and documents. An advisor could ask a question in plain English instead of searching by keyword.12 The point was to give advisors more time with clients. The advice, and the relationship, stayed with the advisor.

Marketing. Generative AI can draft a dozen versions of an ad or a product description in a minute. A marketer picks the strongest, tests them on real customers, and keeps what the audience actually responds to. A small team can now try ideas that once needed an agency. Deciding what fits the brand is still the marketer's job, and now they have more good options to choose from.

Management and human resources. The support study in section 6.2 is a management story: when an assistant spreads the habits of your best people to your newest ones, training takes less time and people are less likely to quit. A manager's job shifts toward coaching.

Operations and supply chain. Forecasting demand, planning routes, and predicting when a machine will break are prediction problems, and Chapter 6's UPS routing system is one at work. The model handles thousands of routine calls. Planners spend their time on the exceptions: the storm, the strike, the supplier who suddenly stops answering.

MIS and analytics. Chapter 11 showed what AI does to building software. The bigger change is the new work around models. Somebody has to decide what "working" means for a model, ground it in the right data, and decide which decisions it may make alone. Section 9 calls that AI governance. Much of it is new work, and it tends to go to people who understand both the business and the system.

7Two systems nobody meant to build

Before the main case, here are two short ones. In both, a system produced an outcome nobody designed and nobody could find written down anywhere. The first is the oldest kind of machine-learning failure, and the second is the newest. What differs between them is which component the human decision was hiding in.

7.1 Mini-case 1 · 2014 to 2018: the hiring model that learned who got hired

Beginning in 2014, a team at Amazon built experimental models to do for recruiting what the company already did for shopping. The models would read each incoming resume and score the applicant from one to five stars, the way shoppers score products. One insider described the dream to Reuters: give the engine one hundred resumes, it picks the top five, and you hire those.13

To learn what a strong applicant looked like, the models trained on ten years of the company's past resumes and how those candidacies had turned out. The whole story fits in one sentence: the tech industry's past applicants were mostly men, so the model learned that resumes resembling men's resumes were what success looked like. By 2015 the team found the system penalizing resumes that contained the word "women's," as in "women's chess club captain," and downgrading graduates of two all-women's colleges. It favored verbs that happened to be more common on male engineers' resumes, such as "executed" and "captured."13

Amazon edited the model to neutralize those terms. Then the company faced the harder truth. With tens of thousands of terms in play, there was no way to be confident the model had not found other, subtler stand-ins for gender. It lost confidence in the project and shut it down. Amazon told Reuters the tool was never used by recruiters to evaluate candidates, though people familiar with it said recruiters had looked at its recommendations without relying on them alone.13

None of this was new. In 1988 Britain's Commission for Racial Equality found that a London medical school's admissions program, built to agree with the school's past decisions, had marked down women and applicants with non-European names.14 Thirty years apart, the same mechanism produced the same surprise.

Where the decision was hiding: in the examples. The component: data. Nobody wrote a biased rule. Nobody wrote any rule. The labels were history, and history had a skew. The least celebrated act in the story is the best one: the company tested its own system, found the failure, and killed a project it had wanted badly.

Check yourselfAmazon deleted the word "women's" from what the model could use, and still could not trust it. Explain why deleting the word could not fix the problem, and name the test that would show whether a hiring model treats groups fairly.

Because the word was a symptom. The cause was the labels: ten years of decisions in which men were hired more often. Delete one stand-in for gender and the model finds the next, such as a college or a verb. Checking the model against past hiring decisions cannot catch this, because agreeing with the past is what it was built to do. The test that can catch it compares outcomes across groups: do equally qualified men and women get equal scores? That is a fairness test, not an accuracy test, and it is the test section 9 calls a bias audit.

7.2 Mini-case 2 · 2024: the airline that blamed its own chatbot

A man booking a flight after a death in his family asked Air Canada's website chatbot about bereavement fares. The chatbot told him he could book now and apply for the discount within ninety days. That was wrong; the airline's actual policy did not allow a claim after travel. He booked on that basis, applied, and was refused.

When he took the case to a Canadian tribunal in 2024, the airline's argument is the part worth remembering. It suggested the chatbot was a separate legal entity responsible for its own actions. The tribunal did not accept this. The chatbot was part of the airline's website, it said, and the airline was responsible for all the information on it. A customer had no way of knowing which part of a company's own website to trust. Air Canada was ordered to pay.15

The sums involved were small. The principle is not, and it was the first clear statement many organizations had heard: a company owns what its systems say, in the same way it owns what its staff say. Read it beside section 5.2, too. A chatbot that invents a refund policy is a hallucination with a customer on the other end of it.

Where the decision was hiding: in who answers for it. The component: people. No training data caused this and no bias was involved. What failed was the assumption, never written down and never tested, that deploying a model does not carry any obligation with it.

It is tempting because it would make automation risk-free: a company that could disown its chatbot could disown anything it automated. That is exactly why the tribunal rejected it. The control is grounding: the chatbot should answer fare questions only from the airline's actual policy pages, passed into each request, and hand the customer to a person when the answer is not in them. The responsibility for building the tool sat with its developers; the accountability for what it told customers sat with the airline. That distinction is the one Chapter 10 is built on.

7.3 What the two have in common

Two systems, and not one biased rule or false instruction between them. Nobody at Amazon wrote down that women should score lower. Nobody at Air Canada told the chatbot to invent a refund policy. In both cases the machine did faithfully what it had been set up to do. The first is a data problem and the second is an accountability problem, and neither is about the algorithm.

Both cases are failures, and it would be easy to stop there. The main case is the same technology pointed at a problem the market had ignored for decades, with people deciding at every step. It is the best argument in this book for what machine learning can do, and it has the same two weak points: the data it learned from, and the person who has to decide what to do with its answer.

8Main case · Every Cure: the drugs already on the shelf

Everything so far in this chapter has been a concept with a short example. This case puts most of it in one organization. You will see a model trained on known answers, and a score that can mean "no" or "nobody looked." You will see a ranking that has to pass through a person before it reaches a patient, and the people who are and are not in the data. Start with the video. It runs about fourteen minutes and was made by Penn Medicine, and the two people who matter in it are a doctor named David Fajgenbaum and a patient named Joseph.

8.1 A doctor who ran out of options

In his third year of medical school at the University of Pennsylvania, Fajgenbaum's organs began shutting down. The diagnosis was idiopathic multicentric Castleman disease, a rare disorder in which the immune system attacks the body. It nearly killed him five times, and the approved treatments failed. So he studied his own disease. He found an overactive signal in his own blood, and then a decades-old drug, sirolimus, given to transplant patients to stop the body rejecting a new organ, that happens to switch that signal off. Nobody had used it for Castleman disease. He has been in remission for more than eleven years, on a drug that sat in every pharmacy the whole time he was dying.16

His question afterward is the one this case is built on: how many other treatments are already on the shelf?

8.2 Why nobody was looking

Drug repurposing is taking a drug already approved for one disease and using it for another. It happens more often than people think; aspirin was a painkiller before it was a heart drug. To see why it matters, look at the alternative. Developing a new drug usually takes ten to fifteen years, and one independent estimate puts the median cost of bringing a new drug to market, counting the failures along the way, at close to a billion dollars.17 That money comes from investors who expect it back, so new drugs get built for diseases with enough paying patients.

Repurposing skips most of that, because the expensive part, proving the drug is safe in people, has already been done. It is also commercially neglected, and the reason is worth saying plainly. A new drug earns back its cost through a patent. An old, off-patent drug has no patent left, so a company that paid to prove a cheap generic pill treats a rare disease could not stop rivals selling the same pill the next day. The cure might be in every pharmacy, and nobody is paid to notice.

The scale makes it worse. Every Cure counts roughly 4,000 approved drugs and more than 18,000 known diseases, about 9,000 of them with no approved treatment at all.18 That is tens of millions of possible pairs. The research that might connect them runs to millions of papers, written by scientists who mostly never read each other's work. No team of people could check every pair.

Before AI, a new use for an old drug was usually found one disease at a time, by a researcher who happened to read the right papers or a doctor who noticed a side effect. Fajgenbaum's own find, in section 8.1, happened that way. Repurposing existed long before machine learning. What machine learning changes is the method. It turns repurposing from an accident into a search: one that can score every pair, and score them all again every time the research grows. That, and not the word "AI," is why this is a machine-learning problem.

8.3 MATRIX: a map of everything known, and a score for every pair

In 2022 Fajgenbaum co-founded a nonprofit, Every Cure, and in February 2024 the federal health research agency ARPA-H gave it a three-year contract worth $48.3 million to build a platform called MATRIX.18 MATRIX is built on a knowledge graph, a way of storing information as connections instead of rows. Each point in the graph is something biomedical research has documented: a drug, a disease, a gene, a protein. Each line is a relationship somebody published: this drug blocks this protein; this protein drives this disease. In a 2026 release, the graph held about nine million points and 33 million connections, drawn from dozens of public databases.18

A path through the graph is not a finding. It is a place to look. MATRIX adds a model on top of the graph, and this is where section 4 comes back. The model is trained on treatments already known to work, so it learns what a real drug-to-disease connection tends to look like. Then it scores every approved drug against every disease and sorts the results into a ranked list. Every Cure publishes the scores online, with a warning that a score measures how biologically plausible a match looks, not proof that it works.18 People take it from there: scientists review the top matches, and doctors decide whether any of them belongs anywhere near a patient.

8.4 Joseph

Joseph was diagnosed with POEMS syndrome, a rare blood disorder. He lost forty pounds in weeks, his own oncology team's treatment did not work, and a planned stem cell transplant was called off because he was too sick to survive it. His doctor raised hospice. His partner emailed Fajgenbaum on a Friday afternoon.16

POEMS is rare, but it resembles Castleman disease and the blood cancer multiple myeloma, so drugs used for myeloma had paths into it. Fajgenbaum had already thought of three myeloma drugs, and an early version of Every Cure's model ranked those same drugs highly.18 Then came the hard part, which no model makes. Chemotherapy is the last thing you give a person who is barely surviving, because it could kill him. His doctors gave it. Joseph responded within two weeks, left intensive care, walked again, had the transplant after all, and reached full remission.16

One detail belongs beside the story, because it is easy to miss. In a newer public release of Every Cure's scores, one of those three drugs ranks 194th for POEMS.18 A score belongs to one version of the model and the data it had at the time. The recovery was real. So was the judgment of the doctors who decided to act on a ranking, and a ranking is all the model ever gave them.

8.5 The thin end of the record

Now look at the data component, because that is where this case teaches the most. Published research is not spread evenly across diseases. It piles up where money and patients are. A few hundred common diseases in wealthy countries get most of the studies, and thousands of rare diseases get almost none. That is the long tail from section 4.3, and every disease in this case lives in it.

A knowledge graph built from that record inherits its shape. A heavily studied disease is a hub with lines running everywhere, so if a drug connects to it, the model has thousands of paths to find. A disease with nine published studies is a point with almost nothing attached. Every drug scores near zero against it, including one that might cure it. The model is not saying "no." It is saying "nobody has looked," and it says it with exactly the same number.

That is the research gap becoming a prediction gap, and it is the failure to fear here, because nobody ever sees it. A drug that would have worked scores low, so nobody investigates, nobody publishes, and nobody ever knows there was anything to know. The second blind spot is quieter. We heard about Joseph. We rarely hear about patients for whom a top-ranked drug did nothing, because those stories do not get told. Judging a system only by the cases that made it into the news is called survivorship bias, and it means nobody outside Every Cure can yet say how often a top match works. Without the misses, nobody can count how often it works.

8.7 What's next: from candidates to trials

MATRIX was named one of TIME's best inventions of 2025. In February 2026 ARPA-H funded a second phase, worth up to $76 million, to take twenty top matches into laboratory testing and ten into clinical trials.18 That is the right next step, because a trial is the one way to answer the question this case leaves open: how often does a top-ranked match actually work? As of this writing, the honest sentence is that the system has produced candidates, some are headed for trials, and one patient recovered after a match it ranked highly. Watch what happens when that sentence gets shortened to "AI found a cure."

8.8 What a business technology manager takes from this case

Strip the Every Cure case down to what you can reuse in any company. Sections 4.3 and 9 already cover reading scores and keeping a person at the gate. Two more lessons are this case's own.

Ask what your records are thin on before you trust a model built on them. Every company's data has the same shape as MATRIX's: thick on its usual customers, thin on everyone else. The thin end is where a model will be confidently wrong, and nobody will notice.

Look for value in data you already own. Every Cure did not invent a drug. It connected records nobody had connected. Most organizations have their own shelf of records that were never put side by side. Before paying to build something new, ask what you already have that nobody has connected.

9Governance and the human in the loop

Every case in this chapter came down to the same two questions. How far was the system allowed to go on its own? And who was positioned to catch it when it went wrong? Those questions have names, and answering them on purpose is called governance.

9.1 In, on, or out of the loop

A human in the loop is a person who sets the goal, checks the work, or approves the action at the points where a mistake would cost something. It does not mean a person watches every step. There are three common settings. A person is in the loop when the system asks before it acts, the way a fraud alert waits for you to confirm. A person is on the loop when the system acts and reports, the way the refund agent in section 3.3 logs every refund for review. A person is out of the loop when the system runs until it is done, the way a resume screener might send rejections nobody reads.

None of the three is always right. A spam filter that asked before every email would be useless. A hiring decision with nobody in the loop invites a lawsuit. Match the setting to the cost of a mistake.

9.2 Where a person has to stay

The cases in this chapter draw the line clearly. Keep a person at the gate wherever a mistake cannot be undone, wherever it falls on someone who cannot see it coming, and wherever the law holds the company responsible. Every Cure's model ranked a drug, and doctors who knew Joseph decided whether to give chemotherapy to a man who might not survive it. Air Canada's chatbot spoke for the company with nobody checking what it said, and the company paid for it. OpenAI's agents in section 3.4 showed signs of trouble that nobody acted on. The difference each time was where the people were.

9.3 What AI governance looks like

AI governance is Chapter 10's IT governance applied to models: who decides which AI systems exist, what they may decide, and who answers when they fail. In practice it is five ordinary things.

  1. An inventory. A list of every AI system the company runs, including the ones employees signed up for on their own, each with a named owner.
  2. A rule for each one. What it may decide alone, what needs a person, and what it may never do. That is where the in, on, or out setting gets written down.
  3. A test before launch. A bias audit compares outcomes across groups, which is the test that caught Amazon's tool. It sits alongside a record of what each model was trained on and where it must not be used.
  4. Monitoring after launch. Somebody watches what the system actually does, because section 4.5 showed that models can change the world they are graded on. An off switch that has been tried before it is needed belongs here too.
  5. Someone with the authority to say no. None of the first four matters if nobody can stop a launch.

None of that requires writing code. You have run a small version all quarter: the Lab 1 traffic lights are an AI use policy, with a green tier, a tier that needs your own judgment, and a banned tier.

The law has started to require some of the same things, unevenly. New York City has required an annual independent bias audit of automated hiring tools since 2023.19 Federal guidance on AI in hiring was withdrawn in January 2025, but the discrimination law underneath it still applies whether a person or a model does the discriminating.20 The European Union's AI Act scales its rules to the stakes of each use, which is called risk-tier regulation. It treats hiring as high-risk, with those rules now due in December 2027. The United States has no broad federal AI law.19 Law moves in years and models change in months, so the protection that lasts is the one this course keeps handing you: measurement that is valid, and accountability with a name attached.

10The downside: what AI costs, and who pays

Start with what sections 6 and 8 established, because it is true. Machine learning catches fraud, screens scans, speeds up the newest workers most, and found a candidate treatment for a man who had been sent toward hospice. A chapter that only listed costs would be as wrong as one that only listed benefits.

But the costs are real, and most of them land on people who did not choose the system and do not appear on the company's books. Here are six.

10.1 The harms you have already met

Three costs have come up already, so here they are in one place. The first is bias. A model trained on a skewed history repeats the skew at scale, with the authority of a number, and the people at the thin end of the record are the most likely to be misjudged and the least likely to find out why. The second is hallucination. A language model's mistakes arrive fluent and confident, which is what made Air Canada's chatbot persuasive, and the same technology makes convincing false text, images, and audio cheap to produce. The third is the people in the data. Every system in this chapter learned from people who never signed up to be training examples: Amazon's applicants, your own swipes, and the millions of writers whose work trained language models without being asked or paid.

10.2 Hidden labor, and what AI does to jobs

Behind the models sits work the demos never show. Supervised learning needs labeled examples, and much of the labeling is done by contractors, often in lower-wage countries, including people paid to look at the disturbing content that moderation models are trained on.

On jobs, begin with a finding that rarely makes the headlines. So far, no study has found widespread job loss from AI across the economy. Yale's Budget Lab looked at the whole U.S. labor market in the 33 months after ChatGPT's release and found no discernible disruption. The shifts it did find in the mix of jobs had started before AI arrived.21 The Stanford study below agrees for most workers: employment held steady or kept growing in less-exposed jobs, and for experienced workers in the most exposed ones.22

The harder question is about entry-level jobs, and it deserves a straight answer: the evidence points in more than one direction. Start with the worry, because it is real. In 2025 Stanford economists studied payroll records from millions of American workers. In the jobs most exposed to AI, employment for workers aged 22 to 25 fell about 13 percent relative to other jobs after late 2022, and later versions of the study put the drop closer to 16 percent. The decline was concentrated where AI does the task instead of helping a person do it. The authors call it a pattern, not proof that AI caused it, and interest rates and remote work hit the same jobs at the same time.22 Employers are also asking more of new hires. PwC studied 2.4 million U.S. entry-level job postings and found that the ones most exposed to AI were seven times more likely to ask for skills usually expected of senior people, such as leadership and creativity.23

Now the other side. The two jobs AI was supposed to replace first are both growing. In 2016 Geoffrey Hinton, one of the pioneers of deep learning, said people should stop training radiologists, because AI would soon read scans better than they could. AI does now read scans. Yet in 2025 U.S. residency programs offered a record 1,208 radiology positions, radiologists are in short supply, and their average pay reached about $571,000.24 Part of the reason is that faster, cheaper scans led doctors to order more of them, and part is that fewer medical students chose the field after the prediction. Software engineers were next on the list. In April 2026, open software engineering roles at tech companies passed 67,000, the highest in more than three years and roughly double the low point of mid-2023.25 Most of that growth is in roles that ask for years of experience, so it is better news for experienced engineers than for new graduates.26

Meanwhile, AI skills have become something employers pay for. PwC's 2026 analysis covered more than a billion job ads. Postings asking for AI skills grew 69 percent in a year, against 9 percent for the job market as a whole, and they offered about 62 percent higher pay on average. That compares job ads, not the same person with and without the skill, but the direction is clear.23 Closer to home, employers surveyed by the National Association of Colleges and Employers in spring 2026 said more than a third of their entry-level jobs now require AI skills, nearly triple the share six months earlier. In the same year, about half of graduating seniors said they were not building any.27

Read together, the evidence says AI is changing which jobs grow and what they ask for more than it is erasing work. That is section 6's co-intelligence point, seen from the job market.

10.3 What entry-level work is turning into, and what you can do

Whatever the cause of the squeeze, the shape of a first job is changing in one direction. The routine tasks that used to fill a new hire's first year are the ones AI does best: the first draft, the data pull, the meeting summary. What is left asks for things employers used to expect only later, such as judgment, critical thinking, and the confidence to tell a manager the numbers look wrong.

The Stanford team behind the payroll study offers one reason. Employment fell most for young workers in jobs built on codified knowledge, the written-down kind you learn from textbooks and manuals. It held up for experienced workers in jobs built on tacit knowledge, the kind you only get by doing the work next to people who already have it.22 That makes sense. A language model learned from written text, so it reproduces written-down knowledge best. Knowing which client actually decides, or sensing that a number is off before you can say why, was never written down. And the squeeze works mostly through hiring, not firing: companies are opening fewer doors.

Here is the uncomfortable sentence. A degree is mostly a record of codified knowledge, and codified knowledge is what AI copies best. That does not make the degree worthless. It means the degree on its own no longer sets you apart the way it did. One AI company's report on its own hiring shows where this is heading. Juniors fell from 27 percent of its new technical hires to 9 percent in about a year. Its hiring managers now rank judgment and the ability to work on your own ahead of technical skill.28 That is one company describing itself, and it sells AI, but it matches the job ads in section 10.2.

None of the moves below guarantees a job. Each one holds up whichever explanation for the squeeze turns out to be right, and each one has a cost.

  • Turn your class work into proof. AI has made written applications cheap, so they count for less. After one job platform added an AI cover-letter tool, a letter that matched the job predicted a callback about half as strongly as before.29 What still counts is something an employer can open and ask you about. Your labs in this course are a start: the member analysis in Lab 5, the dashboard in Lab 6, the queries in Lab 7, the usability evaluation in Lab 9. Write each one up the way this course has taught you: lead with the question and what you found, say what decision it should change, and name the tool last. The cost: class projects that look like everyone else's will not set you apart, so add one thing of your own, such as an analysis for a club or a tool a local business actually uses.
  • Practice judgment where mistakes are cheap. The skills job ads now ask for early are learnable, and a classroom is one of the few places where getting them wrong costs nothing. Arguing a discussion question you did not pick and checking an AI's answer before you trust it are both practice. The cost is that it feels slower than letting the model answer.
  • Go where you can learn what nobody writes down. Tacit knowledge comes from internships, part-time jobs, and projects with real stakes, next to people who already have it. When you interview, ask how new hires learn and who they sit near. The cost is time now, and sometimes less remote flexibility than you wanted.
  • Look for complement work. Ask whether a role uses AI to do the task while a person checks it, or to extend what the person can do. Section 6 showed which one grows, and asking well in an interview is itself a signal. The cost: complement roles often ask for experience you are still building.
  • Find people who will vouch for you. A referral from someone who has watched you work cuts through a pile of AI-polished applications in a way no cover letter can. The cost is that relationships take time, so start before you need them.

Keep both halves in view. The bar for a first job is rising, the search may take longer than it did for people a few years ahead of you, and that is not your fault. But the bar is rising toward the skills this course has asked you to practice all quarter.

10.4 The environment

Training a large model and then serving it to hundreds of millions of people takes a great deal of electricity, and the data centers doing the work use water to stay cool. The International Energy Agency estimates that the world's data centers used about 415 terawatt-hours of electricity in 2024, roughly 1.5 percent of all electricity on Earth, and about 485 in 2025. Its base case has the figure roughly doubling by 2030, with servers built for AI driving close to half of the increase.30 How much of that becomes emissions depends on how much of the electricity comes from coal and gas.

A single question to a chatbot costs very little. At scale, computing is an industry, with an industry's appetite for power, water, and land, and the bills land on the communities where the buildings go. That is the hardware component of section 2, with its costs attached. Chapter 13 is about those buildings.

10.5 Dependence on a few providers

Section 5.4 said most businesses rent their models from a handful of companies. That is efficient, and it is a dependence. The provider sets the price, decides when the model changes underneath your product, and holds whatever your employees paste into it. That is Chapter 13's lock-in problem with a model on top.

10.6 Where you come in

Read this section's list again. Every cost on it comes from a decision a person made: which data to train on, what to measure, where to draw the line between the model and the person, and who answers when it goes wrong. People who understand both the business and the system make those decisions, which is the job this course has been preparing you for.

That is the positive note this chapter ends on. At Every Cure, the technology made the search possible. People decided what to look for, judged what the machine returned, and took responsibility for acting on it. You now know enough to be one of those people.

Key takeaways
  • AI is software that learns from data instead of following written rules. By that test, your social media feed has been AI for more than a decade.
  • An AI system is an information system: hardware, software, data, networks, and people. The model is one box. Most failures, and most fixes, live outside it.
  • Most AI in use is tool AI. Agents are autonomous, and the good ones are controlled. The July 2026 test shows what autonomy without control looks like, and people decided both dials.
  • A supervised model makes the future resemble the labeled past. It is confident where the data is thick and quietly wrong in the long tail, where the data is thin. Ask who lives in the tail.
  • A language model predicts likely text, not true text. Ground it in real documents, check it, and keep a person accountable for what it says.
  • AI works best as a complement. Inside the jagged frontier it makes a skilled person better; outside it, it makes a careless one worse. Knowing where the line falls is the skill.
  • Every Cure turns drug repurposing from an accident into a search. Its weak points are the thin end of the data and the person who has to decide what to do with a score.
  • AI governance is an inventory, a rule for each system, a test before launch, monitoring after, and someone who can say no.
  • First jobs are shifting from routine tasks toward judgment. Proof you can explain, practiced judgment, and people who will vouch for you hold up whatever the cause of the squeeze.

11Vocabulary

Artificial intelligence / machine learning
Software that learns patterns from data to make judgments or new content / the approach of learning from examples instead of following written rules.
AI system / model
The whole information system of hardware, software, data, networks, and people / the trained software at its center.
Predictive / generative / agentic AI
AI that outputs a judgment / AI that produces new content / AI that takes actions toward a goal.
Tool AI
AI that does a task people give it, inside limits people set, and stops.
Autonomy / control
How far a system goes on its own before a person sees anything / whether anyone can notice, stop, and undo what it does.
Training data / label
The past examples a model learns from, and the outcome attached to each.
Supervised / unsupervised learning
Learning from labeled outcomes vs. finding structure with no labels.
Inference
Using a trained model on new cases.
Neural network / deep learning
A model built from layers of simple units / a network with many layers, whose behavior is set by training rather than by written rules.
Collaborative filtering
Predicting what you will want from patterns across everyone's past behavior.
Complement / co-intelligence
Something that makes a person's skills worth more rather than replacing them / working with AI as a capable, fallible colleague, with a person in charge.
Jagged frontier
The uneven line between tasks AI does well and similar-looking tasks it does badly.
Long tail
A shape where a few common things have most of the examples and thousands of rare ones have very few each. Models are weakest there, and least likely to say so.
Rare disease
In the U.S., a disease affecting fewer than 200,000 people. Thousands exist; together they affect about one American in ten.
Training-data bias
A skew in the examples, faithfully learned and repeated.
Proxy discrimination
A model rediscovering a removed attribute through stand-ins that go with it.
Feedback loop
Outputs that shape the next training data, letting a model confirm itself.
Large language model (LLM)
A deep learning model trained to predict the next piece of text; the engine of generative AI.
Hallucination
Fluent, confident output that is simply wrong.
Grounding
Giving a model the real documents to answer from, inside the request.
Fine-tuning / distillation
Adapting a model to one job / training a smaller, cheaper model to imitate a big one.
AI agent
A system in which a model takes actions with tools, not just answers.
Knowledge graph
Information stored as connections, so a system can follow paths nobody wrote down.
Drug repurposing
Using an approved drug for a disease it was not approved for.
Survivorship bias
Judging something only by the cases that made it far enough to be seen.
Codified / tacit knowledge
Written-down knowledge that AI reproduces well / knowledge earned by doing the work next to people who have it.
Human in the loop
A person who sets the goal, checks the work, or approves the action where a mistake would cost something.
AI governance / bias audit
The accountability structure for models / testing whether outcomes differ across groups.
Risk-tier regulation
Rules that scale with the stakes of a use, from banned to lightly regulated.
