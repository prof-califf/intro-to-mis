Chapter 12 · AI, Machine Learning, and Generative AI

AI and Machine Learning: Scoring Every Drug Against Every Disease

A medical student nearly died of a rare disease, and saved himself with a drug that had been sitting in pharmacies for decades. His nonprofit now uses machine learning to score every approved drug against every known disease, looking for more cures that are already in pharmacies. The same technique, pointed at ten years of resumes, taught Amazon's hiring model to prefer men. This chapter is about why one technique produces both outcomes, and who is responsible for the difference.

You have used a large language model all quarter. This chapter explains what it is doing when it answers you, what machine learning in general can and cannot do, and how it is already changing the work of every major in this college.

Learning objectives

By the end of this chapter you should be able to:

  1. Explain what machine learning is, why an AI system is more than its model, and why a model's behavior comes from its training data.
  2. Read a model's accuracy correctly, using false positives, false negatives, and base rates.
  3. Identify training-data bias, proxy discrimination, and feedback loops in a deployed system.
  4. Explain what a large language model does, why it hallucinates, and what grounding and agents change.
  5. Describe how AI is changing work in accounting, finance, marketing, management, operations, and MIS.
  6. Weigh AI's benefits against its costs, including the environment, and explain what AI governance consists of.

1How a model learns, and how it learns the wrong thing

Chapter 6 was about choosing the right measure and putting it where decisions get made. This chapter is about what happens when you hand the measuring to a machine that works out its own rules. This section says what that machine is, in plain terms, how to tell whether it works, and the three ways it goes wrong without anybody writing a bad instruction.

Before the vocabulary arrives, locate machine learning in the five components, because where it sits is the whole argument. Machine learning is a software component that is almost entirely determined by the data component. Hardware sets what you can afford and networks set who you can serve, and neither decides what the system will conclude. What decides that is the examples you show it, and those were chosen by people.

That means the weakest-component rule applies here in an uncomfortable way. In earlier chapters a weak component made the system slow, or unreliable, or blind. In this one a weak data component makes the system confidently wrong in a way that looks like an answer, and no amount of quality in the other four corrects it.

1.1 Three terms that get used as one

The press uses three words interchangeably. They are three generations of one family, and the differences matter for what a manager can expect from each.

Artificial intelligence (AI) is the broad project of making computers do things that seem to require intelligence. For decades the main approach was writing explicit rules, the way a tax program applies rules a person typed in. Machine learning (ML), the approach behind nearly everything now called AI, flips that. Instead of writing rules, you show the system many examples and it finds the patterns itself, the way a spam filter learns from millions of emails people marked as spam. Generative AI is the newest member. It includes the large language models (LLMs) you have used all quarter under the Lab 1 rules: machine learning trained on so much text that it can produce new text, not just sort or score existing things.

The difference between rules and learning matters for business because it moves responsibility. A rule-based system does what someone wrote, and a bad outcome traces to a bad rule someone can find and fix. Nobody at TikTok wrote a rule that says "show this person otter videos." The preference was learned from behavior, and it lives in millions of numbers inside the model that no manager can read. Nobody at TikTok can read them either. That is not a dodge; it is how the technology works. The opacity is harmless when the stakes are otters, and the rest of this chapter is about what happens as the stakes rise.

1.2 An AI system is more than a model

When people say "the AI," they usually mean the model: the trained piece of software that turns an input into an answer. A model on its own does nothing. An AI system is the whole arrangement that turns a model into decisions. It includes the data the model learned from and the data it is fed every day. It includes the infrastructure, the computers and networks that train it and run it. It includes the model itself and the output it produces, whether a score, a ranking, or a sentence. And it includes the people and processes that decide what happens with that output, which in turn produces the next round of data.

That is the five components again. The data is data. The infrastructure is hardware and networks, usually rented, which is Chapter 13's subject. The model and the programs around it are software. And the output matters only when it reaches a decision, which belongs to people. A model is one part of one component.

This matters because almost every failure in this chapter happens outside the model. Amazon's hiring model learned exactly as designed; the problem was the data. Air Canada's chatbot had no grounding and no owner; the problem was the process and the people. Spotify's stories went wrong because of a bug in a data pipeline, not in the language model. So when somebody tells you about "our AI," ask about the whole system. Where does the data come from? What runs it? What does it output, who acts on that output, and who checks whether it is right?

1.3 How a model learns from examples

Supervised learning is the way most business machine learning works: show the system a large set of past cases where the answer is already known, and let it work out what predicts the answer. The past cases are the training data. Each one carries a label, the outcome you want predicted. The model adjusts itself until its predictions match the labels, and then you use it on new cases, which is called inference.

TikTok's For You page is the example you know best. The training data is videos, described by their content and by who watched them. The label is whether a person kept watching. Every time you swipe, the model predicts which of many candidate videos you will keep watching and shows you the winner, and your swipe becomes the next labeled example. Amazon's "customers who bought this also bought" works the same way, learning from millions of past purchases through a method called collaborative filtering that its engineers published in 2003.1 Its sibling, unsupervised learning, finds structure with no labels at all. It could, for example, sort Cascadia's members into groups nobody had defined in advance.

Compress the mechanism into one sentence and keep it: a supervised model is a machine for making the future resemble the labeled past. When the past is what you want more of, that is a superpower. The rest of this section is about when it is not.

1.4 Deep learning: when nobody writes the rules

Nearly everything currently sold as AI, from the feed on your phone to the language model you used last night, is built on deep learning. A neural network is a structure loosely modeled on how brains process information. It is built from layers of simple units, and each unit takes numbers from the layer before it, does one small calculation, and passes the result forward. The first layer takes in the raw input, such as the pixels of a photo or the words of a resume. The last layer gives the answer. Deep learning just means a network with many layers in between, sometimes hundreds.

Take the simplest version of the job: teaching a computer to tell a cat from anything else. You show it a picture and tell it "cat." You show it another and tell it "not a cat." You do that a few thousand times. Nobody writes down what a cat looks like. The network works out which features went with the label, such as pointy ears and whiskers, because those reliably came with "cat" in the pictures it saw. Two things in that are worth slowing down for. The network does not know what a cat is; it knows what cats tended to look like in one pile of pictures. And you chose the pile.

Now change two words. Instead of cat and not a cat, label the examples hired and not hired, and feed the network ten years of a company's resumes. The machinery does not change at all. It works out which features went with the label, and it has no way to know which of those features you would be embarrassed to write down as a rule. Mini-case 1 in section 3 is that exact system.

Here is the part that matters to a manager. Think of every connection inside the network as a dial, and a large modern network as having billions of them. Training shows the network an example, checks whether it was right, and nudges every relevant dial slightly toward a better answer, millions of times. Nobody tells it what to look for, and nobody at the company can point at a dial and say what it is for. A rule you can read is a rule you can argue with and correct. Billions of dials are neither. So when you want to know why a system decided something, the only place left to look is the data it learned from.

1.5 Measuring a model: accuracy is not enough

Every model comes with an accuracy number. The first professional habit is to ask what the number is hiding. A model makes two different kinds of mistake. A false positive says yes when the answer is no: a fraud alert on an honest purchase. A false negative says no when the answer is yes: real fraud that gets through. The two almost never cost the same.

The second habit is to ask about the base rate, how common the thing being predicted actually is. Suppose a bank checks 10,000 card transactions and 20 of them are fraud, a base rate of 0.2 percent. A model that simply says "not fraud" every time is 99.8 percent accurate, and completely useless. The figure shows a model that is actually trying.

That model is about 98 percent accurate, and the number sounds excellent. But of the 216 transactions it flagged, only 16 were fraud. When it raises an alarm it is wrong more than nine times in ten, and behind every false alarm is a customer whose card was declined at a register. Whether that trade is worth it is a business decision, not a statistical one.

The same arithmetic applies to people. When a hiring model makes a false negative, a qualified person never hears back, and nobody records that anything went wrong. Chapter 6 asked whether a measure tracks what you care about. For a model, ask three questions every time. What is the base rate? Which mistake does this model make more often? And who pays for each kind?

1.6 Training-data bias and proxy discrimination

The most common way a model goes wrong is the simplest. If the examples it learned from carry a skew, it learns the skew as if it were a rule, and it keeps applying it after the humans who produced the examples have been told to stop.

Training-data bias is a skew in the examples that the model faithfully learns and repeats. The model is not malfunctioning, which is what makes it dangerous. Proxy discrimination is the sequel. Remove a forbidden attribute, such as gender, and the model finds stand-ins that go along with it: a word, a college, a zip code. A company can delete the one stand-in it noticed, but it can never prove there is no subtler one among tens of thousands of terms.

One management lesson follows, and mini-case 1 shows it in full. "The algorithm decided" is never an explanation. A model's decisions are its training data's decisions, run through arithmetic. Accountability for them belongs to the people who chose the data and shipped the system. The engineers were responsible for building it, but the managers who approved it are the ones who answer for it.

1.7 The feedback loop

Now take the feed one step further. The For You page does not just learn from your behavior; it shapes the behavior it will learn from next. It serves what held your attention, your attention becomes the data, and the data narrows what gets served. In 2021 the Wall Street Journal ran automated accounts through TikTok and showed that watch time alone steered fresh feeds into narrow tunnels within hours. Accounts that lingered on sad content were fed darker and darker variants of whatever had held their attention.2 Engagement is a proxy for satisfaction, the proxy became the target, and Chapter 6 told you the rest.

Raise the stakes and the same loop stops being recoverable. Predictive policing systems forecast where crime is likeliest and send patrols there. More patrols in a neighborhood observe more offenses there, which enters the data as more recorded crime, which raises the forecast, which sends more patrols. The prediction manufactures its own confirmation, and past over-policing hardens into policy, applied to people who cannot log off.

A feedback loop exists when a system's outputs change the data it will learn from next. The test to carry into any AI project is one question: does acting on this prediction change the data that will judge the prediction? If yes, the model can be confidently wrong and keep confirming itself.

2Generative AI, and what AI is good for

Section 1 was about models that sort and score: this video or that one, fraud or not, hire or not. Generative AI produces something new instead, usually text, and it is the kind of AI you have used all quarter. This section explains what it is actually doing, why it makes things up, what a business can control, and then what AI in all its forms is good for, discipline by discipline.

2.1 What a large language model actually does

A large language model (LLM) is a deep learning model trained to do one thing: predict the next piece of text. During training it reads an enormous amount of writing, and for every passage it guesses the next word, checks the real one, and nudges its billions of dials. The label in section 1.3's sense is simply whatever word came next. After that, people rate its answers, and the model is trained further to give the kinds of answers people rated as helpful.

When you ask it something, it writes the reply one piece at a time, each time choosing a likely next word given everything so far. That is the whole mechanism, and it explains both what LLMs are good at and how they fail. The figure shows the same process on two questions.

Notice what is missing from that process. There is no step where the model looks anything up. It produces text that resembles the text it was trained on, and on well-covered questions resembling the training text and being true are nearly the same thing. On a question about one airline's refund rules, or a person who is not famous, they can come apart, and the model has no way to notice.

2.2 Hallucination, and grounding

A hallucination is fluent, confident output that is simply wrong: an invented citation, a policy that does not exist, a statistic nobody measured. It is not a bug in the usual sense. It is section 2.1's mechanism working as designed on a question where the likely answer and the true answer differ. You met it in Lab 1, in the calculator test, as a confident wrong total.

The main defense is grounding: giving the model the real documents or records it should answer from, inside the request, and instructing it to answer only from those. Instead of asking the model what an airline's refund policy is, the system fetches the policy page and asks the model to answer from it. Grounding does not make a model honest. It changes the question from "what sounds right?" to "what does this document say?", which a language model is much better at.

Spotify gave the clearest public example in 2025. For its Wrapped Archive, rules picked out up to five "remarkable days" in each of roughly 350 million users' listening years. A language model then wrote a short story about each one, about 1.4 billion stories, all written before launch day. Every story was grounded in the user's actual listening records, because an ungrounded model will cheerfully invent a touching day you never had. Even so, a timezone bug in the data pipeline made some stories celebrate the wrong "discovery day," and the model wrote a compelling story about it anyway.3 That is grounding failing one layer down. The model was faithful to the data, and the data was wrong.

2.3 What a business can actually control

Most businesses never train a language model. They rent one, and control it in four ways. The prompt is the instructions and context sent with every request, including the grounding documents. Fine-tuning adapts a general model to one job by training it further on examples of that job. Distillation trains a smaller, cheaper model to imitate a big one on a narrow task. Spotify used both, because 1.4 billion calls to a large model would have cost too much.3 And evaluation decides how you will know it works.

Evaluation is where most of the management lives. No person can read 1.4 billion stories, so Spotify had larger language models judge a sample of about 165,000, backed by targeted human review. That is how it caught the timezone bug. Spotify's engineers summed up the project in one line worth remembering: at that scale, the call to the language model was the easy part.3 The data pipeline, the grounding, the evaluation, and the capacity planning were the achievement, and every one of those is MIS work, not model-building work.

2.4 Agents: when the model takes actions

An AI agent is a system in which a language model does not just answer but acts. It breaks a goal into steps and uses tools to carry them out: searching, sending an email, updating a record, placing an order. A chatbot that tells you how to change a flight is an assistant. One that changes the flight for you is an agent.

Agents raise the question Chapter 6 asked about Zillow: how far can a model's output travel before a person checks it? An assistant's mistake is a wrong sentence somebody can ignore. An agent's mistake is an action, already taken, and a mistake in step two is carried forward into every step after it. Chapter 4 asked you to decide which steps of a process to hand to an agent. Here is the rule underneath that decision. Give an agent the authority you would give a new employee in their first week: real work, a short list of things it may never do alone, and a person who reviews anything that cannot be undone.

2.5 Rent the model, or build it

Chapter 11's build, buy, or subscribe question applies to models too, and the answer is usually the same. A handful of companies train the largest models, at a cost few others could carry, and most businesses rent them through an API, from Chapter 11, and run them in a cloud, from Chapter 13. What a business actually owns is its data, its prompts, its grounding documents, and its evaluation. Those are the differentiators. The model underneath is increasingly a commodity, and it can change when the provider releases a new version.

TikTok is the exception, and it shows why. TikTok's short videos were easy to copy, and much larger companies did copy them. The recommendation model, trained on its users' behavior, was not, and it was the business. When the United States forced a sale of TikTok's American operations, the hardest question was who would control the algorithm. In the deal that closed in January 2026, ByteDance kept a 19.9 percent stake and licensed the algorithm to the new American company, which is retraining it on American users' data inside Oracle's cloud.4 When the model is what customers choose you for, you own it. When it is not, you rent it and own what you feed it.

2.6 What AI is good for, in every part of the business

Start with the plain fact, because the rest of this chapter spends a long time on costs. Machine learning works, at scale, every day. It flags card fraud in the moment between swipe and approval, which is why your bank texts you about a strange charge before you leave the store. Models trained on labeled medical scans screen for eye disease in clinics that have no specialist. DeepMind's AlphaFold predicted the shapes of essentially every known protein, work that shared the 2024 Nobel Prize in Chemistry. Translation and speech recognition have become accessibility tools for hundreds of millions of people. None of this is hype, and the main case in section 4 is one of the best examples there is.

For a business student the more useful question is what AI does in your own future job. The pattern is consistent enough to state first. AI takes the first draft and the routine case. A person keeps the exception, the judgment, and the accountability. The details differ by discipline.

Accounting. Models read invoices and receipts, match them to purchase orders, and flag journal entries that look unusual so an auditor can check them, instead of the auditor sampling at random. What stays with a person is deciding whether an unusual entry is fraud, an error, or a legitimate one-off, and signing the opinion that says the books are fair.

Finance. In 2023 Morgan Stanley gave its financial advisors an assistant built on an OpenAI model and grounded in roughly 100,000 of the firm's own research reports and documents. An advisor could ask a question in plain English instead of searching by keyword.5 The firm presented it as a way to free advisors' time for clients, not to replace the advisor, who still owns the advice.

Marketing. Generative AI makes content cheap: product descriptions, ad variations, images. In November 2024 Coca-Cola remade its famous 1995 "Holidays Are Coming" commercial with generative AI. An advertising research firm scored the ad well in early testing. Then public reaction turned against it, much of it from viewers and artists who objected to a brand known for its holiday animation replacing its animators.6 Cheaper content is real. So is the judgment about what a brand's audience will accept, and that judgment stayed with people.

Management and human resources. The strongest evidence on AI and everyday work comes from a study of more than 5,000 customer support agents at a software company. An AI assistant that suggested replies raised the number of issues resolved per hour by about 14 to 15 percent on average. The newest and least experienced agents gained roughly 30 percent or more, and the most experienced gained almost nothing.7 The authors' evidence suggests the assistant was spreading the habits of the best agents to everyone else. For a manager, that changes how you train people and whom you can hire. Mini-case 1 is the warning on the other side: AI used to choose whom to hire.

Operations and supply chain. Forecasting demand and planning routes are prediction problems, and Chapter 6's UPS routing system is prescriptive analytics at work. Customer service is the operations story everyone watched. In early 2024 Klarna said its AI assistant was handling two-thirds of its customer service chats, the work of about 700 agents. In 2025 its chief executive said the push to cut costs had lowered quality, and the company began hiring human agents again, while the assistant kept handling most chats.8 The lesson was not that AI failed. It was that where the line sits between the model and the person is a decision, and it can be drawn in the wrong place.

MIS and analytics. Chapter 11 showed what AI does to building software. The bigger change is in the work around models. Somebody has to decide what "working" means for a model and measure it, ground it in the right data, decide which decisions it may make alone, and keep a record of every model the company runs. Section 5 calls that AI governance, and it is business work, not engineering.

3Two systems nobody meant to build

Before the main case, here are two short ones. In both, a system produced an outcome nobody designed and nobody could find written down anywhere. The first is the oldest kind of machine-learning failure, and the second is the newest. What differs between them is where the human decision was hiding.

3.1 Mini-case 1 · 2014 to 2018: the hiring model that learned who got hired

Amazon had spent two decades proving that prediction from history works: what you will buy, where to stock it, what to recommend. Beginning in 2014, a team at the company built experimental models to do the same for recruiting. The models would read each incoming resume and score the applicant from one to five stars, the way shoppers score products. One insider described the dream to Reuters: give the engine one hundred resumes, it picks the top five, and you hire those.9 This was not a rogue project. It was the company's most proven idea, aimed at its own front door.

To learn what a strong applicant looked like, the models trained on ten years of the company's past resumes and how those candidacies had turned out. The whole story fits in one sentence: the tech industry's past applicants were mostly men, so the model learned that resumes resembling men's resumes were what success looked like. By 2015 the team found the system penalizing resumes that contained the word "women's," as in "women's chess club captain," and downgrading graduates of two all-women's colleges. It favored verbs that happened to be more common on male engineers' resumes, such as "executed" and "captured."9

Amazon edited the model to neutralize those terms. Then the company faced the harder truth. With tens of thousands of terms in play, there was no way to be confident the model had not found other, subtler stand-ins for gender. It lost confidence in the project and shut it down. Amazon told Reuters the tool was never used by recruiters to evaluate candidates, though people familiar with it said recruiters had looked at its recommendations without relying on them alone.9

None of this was new. In 1988 Britain's Commission for Racial Equality found that a London medical school's admissions program, built to agree with the school's past decisions, had marked down women and applicants with non-European names.10 Thirty years apart, the same mechanism produced the same surprise.

Where the decision was hiding: in the examples. The component: data. Nobody wrote a biased rule. Nobody wrote any rule. The labels were history, and history had a skew. The least celebrated act in the story is the best one: the company tested its own system, found the failure, and killed a project it had wanted badly.

Check yourselfAmazon deleted the word "women's" from what the model could use, and still could not trust it. Explain why deleting the word could not fix the problem, and name the test that would show whether a hiring model treats groups fairly.
Show the answer

Because the word was a symptom, not the cause. The cause was the labels: ten years of decisions in which men were hired more often. Delete one stand-in for gender and the model finds the next one, such as a college, a sport, or a verb, because the pattern it was trained to reproduce is still in the answer key. Checking the model against past hiring decisions cannot catch this, because agreeing with a skewed past is exactly what the model was built to do. The test that can catch it compares outcomes across groups: do equally qualified men and women get equal scores? That is a fairness test, not an accuracy test, and it is the test section 5 calls a bias audit.

3.2 Mini-case 2 · 2024: the airline that blamed its own chatbot

A man booking a flight after a death in his family asked Air Canada's website chatbot about bereavement fares. The chatbot told him he could book now and apply for the discount within ninety days. That was wrong; the airline's actual policy did not allow a claim after travel. He booked on that basis, applied, and was refused.

When he took the case to a Canadian tribunal in 2024, the airline's argument is the part worth remembering. It suggested the chatbot was a separate legal entity responsible for its own actions. The tribunal did not accept this. The chatbot was part of the airline's website, it said, and the airline was responsible for all the information on it. A customer had no way of knowing which part of a company's own website to trust. Air Canada was ordered to pay.11

The sums involved were small. The principle is not, and it was the first clear statement many organizations had heard: a company owns what its systems say, in the same way it owns what its staff say. Read it beside section 2.2, too. A chatbot that invents a refund policy is a hallucination with a customer on the other end of it.

Where the decision was hiding: in who answers for it. The component: people. No training data caused this and no bias was involved. What failed was the assumption, never written down and never tested, that deploying a model does not carry any obligation with it.

Check yourselfAir Canada argued the chatbot was responsible for itself. Say why that argument is tempting for a company to make, then name the specific control from section 2 that would have stopped the chatbot inventing a policy in the first place.
Show the answer

It is tempting because it would make automation risk-free: if a company could disown what its chatbot said, it could disown anything it automated. That is exactly why the tribunal rejected it. A system's output is its owner's statement, and a customer cannot know which page of a company's website is allowed to be wrong. The control is grounding: the chatbot should answer fare questions only from the airline's actual policy pages, passed into each request, and hand the customer to a person when the answer is not in them. Grounding would not have made the chatbot perfect, but it would have changed its question from "what sounds right?" to "what does our policy say?" The responsibility for building the tool sat with its developers; the accountability for what it told customers sat with the airline. That distinction is the one Chapter 10 is built on.

3.3 What the two have in common

Two systems, and not one biased rule or false instruction between them. Nobody at Amazon wrote down that women should score lower. Nobody at Air Canada told the chatbot to invent a refund policy. In both cases the machine did faithfully what it had been set up to do. The first is a data problem and the second is an accountability problem, and neither is about the algorithm.

Both cases are failures, and it would be easy to stop there. The main case is the same technology pointed at a problem the market had ignored for decades, with people deciding at every step. It is the best argument in this book for what machine learning can do, and it has the same two weak points: the data it learned from, and the person who has to decide what to do with its answer.

4Main case · Every Cure: the drugs already on the shelf

Everything so far in this chapter has been a concept with a short example. This case puts most of it in one organization. You will see a model trained on known answers, and a score that can mean "no" or "nobody looked." You will see a ranking that has to pass through a person before it reaches a patient, and the people who are and are not in the data. Start with the video. It runs about fourteen minutes and was made by Penn Medicine, and the two people who matter in it are a doctor named David Fajgenbaum and a patient named Joseph.

4.1 A doctor who ran out of options

In his third year of medical school at the University of Pennsylvania, Fajgenbaum's organs began shutting down. The diagnosis was idiopathic multicentric Castleman disease, a rare disorder in which the immune system attacks the body. It nearly killed him five times, and the approved treatments failed. So he studied his own disease. He found an overactive signal in his own blood, and then a decades-old drug, sirolimus, given to transplant patients to stop the body rejecting a new organ, that happens to switch that signal off. Nobody had used it for Castleman disease. He has been in remission for more than eleven years, on a drug that sat in every pharmacy the whole time he was dying.12

His question afterward is the one this case is built on: how many other treatments are already on the shelf?

4.2 Why nobody was looking

Drug repurposing is taking a drug already approved for one disease and using it for another. It happens more often than people think; aspirin was a painkiller before it was a heart drug. To see why it matters, look at the alternative. Developing a new drug usually takes ten to fifteen years, and one independent estimate puts the median cost of bringing a new drug to market, counting the failures along the way, at close to a billion dollars.13 That money comes from investors who expect it back, so new drugs get built for diseases with enough paying patients.

Repurposing skips most of that, because the expensive part, proving the drug is safe in people, has already been done. It is also commercially neglected, and the reason is worth saying plainly. A new drug earns back its cost through a patent. An old, off-patent drug has no patent left, so a company that paid to prove a cheap generic pill treats a rare disease could not stop rivals selling the same pill the next day. The cure might be in every pharmacy, and nobody is paid to notice.

The scale makes it worse. Every Cure counts roughly 4,000 approved drugs and more than 18,000 known diseases, about 9,000 of them with no approved treatment at all.14 That is tens of millions of possible pairs, and the research that might connect them runs to millions of published papers, written by scientists who mostly never read each other's work. No team of people could check every pair. That, and not the word "AI," is why this is a machine-learning problem.

4.3 MATRIX: a map of everything known, and a score for every pair

In 2022 Fajgenbaum co-founded a nonprofit, Every Cure, and in February 2024 the federal health research agency ARPA-H gave it a three-year contract worth $48.3 million to build a platform called MATRIX.14 MATRIX is built on a knowledge graph, a way of storing information as connections instead of rows. Each point in the graph is something biomedical research has documented: a drug, a disease, a gene, a protein. Each line is a relationship somebody published: this drug blocks this protein; this protein drives this disease. In a 2026 release, the graph held about nine million points and 33 million connections, drawn from dozens of public databases.14

A path through the graph is not a finding. It is a place to look. MATRIX adds a model on top of the graph, and this is where section 1 comes back. The model is trained on treatments already known to work, so it learns what a real drug-to-disease connection tends to look like. Then it scores every approved drug against every disease and sorts the results into a ranked list. Every Cure publishes the scores online, with a warning that a score measures how biologically plausible a match looks, not proof that it works.14 People take it from there: scientists review the top matches, and doctors decide whether any of them belongs anywhere near a patient.

4.4 Joseph

Joseph was diagnosed with POEMS syndrome, a rare blood disorder. He lost forty pounds in weeks, his own oncology team's treatment did not work, and a planned stem cell transplant was called off because he was too sick to survive it. His doctor raised hospice. His partner emailed Fajgenbaum on a Friday afternoon.12

POEMS is rare, but it resembles Castleman disease and the blood cancer multiple myeloma, so drugs used for myeloma had paths into it. Fajgenbaum had already thought of three myeloma drugs, and an early version of Every Cure's model ranked those same drugs highly.14 Then came the hard part, which no model makes. Chemotherapy is the last thing you give a person who is barely surviving, because it could kill him. His doctors gave it. Joseph responded within two weeks, left intensive care, walked again, had the transplant after all, and reached full remission.12

One detail belongs beside the story, because section 1.5 taught you to look for it. In a newer public release of Every Cure's scores, one of those three drugs ranks 194th for POEMS.14 A score belongs to one version of the model and the data it had at the time. The recovery was real. So was the judgment of the doctors who decided to act on a ranking, and a ranking is all the model ever gave them.

4.5 The thin end of the record

Now look at the data component, because that is where this case teaches the most. Published research is not spread evenly across diseases. It piles up where money and patients are. A few hundred common diseases in wealthy countries get most of the studies, and thousands of rare diseases get almost none. Statisticians call that shape a long tail, and every disease in this case lives in it.

A knowledge graph built from that record inherits its shape. A heavily studied disease is a hub with lines running everywhere, so if a drug connects to it, the model has thousands of paths to find. A disease with nine published studies is a point with almost nothing attached. Every drug scores near zero against it, including one that might cure it. The model is not saying "no." It is saying "nobody has looked," and it says it with exactly the same number.

That is the research gap becoming a prediction gap, and it is the failure to fear here, because nobody ever sees it. A drug that would have worked scores low, so nobody investigates, nobody publishes, and nobody ever knows there was anything to know. The second blind spot is the one section 1.5 warned about. We heard about Joseph. We rarely hear about patients for whom a top-ranked drug did nothing, because those stories do not get told. Judging a system only by the cases that made it into the news is called survivorship bias, and it means nobody outside Every Cure can yet say how often a top match works. Without the misses, there is no base rate.

4.7 What's next: from candidates to trials

MATRIX was named one of TIME's best inventions of 2025. In February 2026 ARPA-H funded a second phase, worth up to $76 million, to take twenty top matches into laboratory testing and ten into clinical trials.14 That is the right next step, because a trial is the one way to answer the question this case leaves open: how often does a top-ranked match actually work? It is also the kind of claim a student can check. As of this writing, the honest sentence is that the system has produced candidates, some are headed for trials, and one patient recovered after a match it ranked highly. Watch what happens when that sentence gets shortened to "AI found a cure."

4.8 What a business technology manager takes from this case

Strip the Every Cure case down to what you can reuse in any company. Four decisions came up in it, and each one is a decision you will be in the room for.

Ask what your records are thin on before you trust a model built on them. MATRIX is strongest where research piled up and silent where it did not. Every company's data has the same shape: thick on its usual customers, thin on everyone else. That thin end is where a model will be confidently wrong, and it is where nobody will notice.

Read every score with its denominator. A ranked list, a 98 percent accuracy, and a moving recovery story all sound like evidence. Ask what the base rate is, which mistakes the system makes, and how many tries stand behind the one success you heard about.

Keep a person at the gate where a mistake cannot be undone. The model ranked a drug. Doctors who knew Joseph decided to give chemotherapy to a man who might not survive it. Decide in advance which outputs a model may act on and which must pass through a person who answers for the result.

Look for value in data you already own. Every Cure did not invent a drug. It connected records nobody had connected. Most organizations have their own shelf: years of repair logs, research projects, customer requests, and sales records that were never put side by side. Before paying to build something new, ask what you already have that nobody has connected.

5The downside: what AI costs, and who pays

Start with what section 2.6 and section 4 established, because it is true. Machine learning catches fraud, screens scans, speeds up the newest workers most, and found a candidate treatment for a man who had been sent toward hospice. A chapter that only listed costs would be as wrong as one that only listed benefits.

But the costs are real, and most of them land on people who did not choose the system and do not appear on the company's books. Here are six, each in a few sentences, and then what a company can do about them.

5.1 Bias and discrimination

Section 1.6 and mini-case 1 showed the mechanism. A model trained on a skewed history repeats the skew, and it repeats it at scale, quickly, and with the authority of a number. In hiring, lending, housing, and policing, the people at the thin end of the record are the people most likely to be misjudged, and the least likely to find out why. "The algorithm decided" is not a defense, legally or ethically.

5.2 Hallucination and misinformation

A language model's mistakes do not look like mistakes. They arrive fluent, confident, and formatted like an answer, which is what made Air Canada's chatbot persuasive. Inside a company, the risk is a wrong figure in a report nobody checked. Outside it, the same technology makes it cheap to produce convincing false text, images, and audio at a volume no fact-checker can match. The defense is the same in both places: grounding, evaluation, and a person accountable for anything that leaves the building.

5.3 The people in the data

Every system in this chapter runs on data generated by people who never signed up to be training examples. The applicants in Amazon's ten years of resumes were job-seekers, not volunteers for an experiment that would score people like them. The residents of a heavily patrolled neighborhood never agreed to have their arrest records forecast their children's police coverage. Spotify's Archive drew on years of retained listening history: delight built on how much a platform remembers about you. And the writing that language models learn from was produced by millions of people who were never asked and mostly never paid.

5.4 Hidden labor, and entry-level jobs

Behind the models sits work the demos never show. Supervised learning needs labeled examples, and much of the labeling is done by contractors, often in lower-wage countries, including people paid to look at the disturbing content that moderation models are trained on.

The labor question closer to you is about entry-level jobs. In 2025 a team of Stanford economists used monthly payroll records from millions of American workers to look for early signs. In the occupations most exposed to AI, such as software development and customer service, employment for workers aged 22 to 25 fell about 13 percent relative to other jobs after late 2022. Later versions of the study report a larger drop, around 16 percent. Older workers in the same occupations held steady or grew. The decline was concentrated where AI automates tasks rather than assisting with them, and the authors cautioned that other factors may be involved.15 Read it the way section 2.6 suggests. The work that is easiest to hand to a model is often the routine work a new graduate used to learn on, which makes the judgment and accountability side of every job in the table more valuable, not less.

That is half the story, and it is the half that makes headlines. The other half is just as real. A 2026 study linked the actual AI spending of 21,559 U.S. firms to their staffing records. The firms that spent the most on AI grew their entry-level headcount about 12 percent in the two years after they started, the opposite of the story in which AI arrives and the junior jobs leave. Light users showed no change either way. The heavy spenders were already larger and growing faster, so AI may not be the reason, and the authors work for companies that sell business-spending and workforce data.16

A second 2026 study complicates the timeline. Using unemployment insurance records, it found that the risk of unemployment in AI-exposed occupations began rising in early 2022, months before ChatGPT, which points to forces that were already at work. And from millions of college syllabi, it found that graduates since ChatGPT whose coursework overlapped most with what AI can do earned more in their first jobs and found them faster.17 None of these studies settles the argument. Each shows a pattern, not a proven cause. Read together, they say the effect of AI on jobs depends on how a company uses it and what the people using it know, which is a better question than whether AI is good or bad for jobs.

5.5 The environment

Training a large model and then serving it to hundreds of millions of people takes a great deal of electricity, and the data centers doing the work use water to stay cool. The International Energy Agency estimates that the world's data centers used about 415 terawatt-hours of electricity in 2024, roughly 1.5 percent of all electricity on Earth, and about 485 in 2025. Its base case has the figure roughly doubling by 2030, with servers built for AI driving close to half of the increase.18 How much of that electricity comes from coal and gas, and how much from wind, sun, water, and nuclear, decides how much of it becomes emissions.

A single question to a chatbot costs very little. At scale, computing is an industry, with an industry's appetite for power, water, and land, and the bills land on the communities and grids where the buildings go. Chapter 13 is about those buildings. Carry one question there: when an AI answer feels free, who is paying, and in what currency?

5.6 Dependence on a few providers

Section 2.5 said most businesses rent their models from a handful of companies. That is efficient, and it is a dependence. The provider sets the price, decides when the model changes underneath your product, and holds whatever your employees paste into it. A business that has built its customer service, its analysis, and its documents around one provider's model has a supplier it cannot easily replace, which is Chapter 13's lock-in problem with a model on top.

5.7 Governing all of it

The treatments for these costs exist, and running them is business work. AI governance is Chapter 10's IT governance applied to models: who decides which AI systems exist, what they may decide, and who answers when they fail. In practice it means keeping an inventory of every model in use, each with a named owner. It means running a bias audit before launch, comparing outcomes across groups, which is the test that caught Amazon's tool. It means writing down what each model was trained on and where it must not be used, and deciding which decisions a model may never make alone. And it means monitoring models after launch, because section 1.7 showed they can change the world they are graded on. None of that requires writing code. All of it requires someone with the authority to say no. You have run a small version all quarter: the Lab 1 traffic lights are an AI use policy.

The law has started to require the same things, unevenly. New York City has required an annual independent bias audit of automated hiring tools since 2023, with published results.19 Federal guidance on AI in hiring was withdrawn in January 2025, but the discrimination law underneath it still applies whether a person or a model does the discriminating.20 The European Union's AI Act scales its rules to the stakes of each use, which is called risk-tier regulation. It bans a few uses outright and treats hiring as high-risk, but in 2026 it pushed those high-risk rules back to December 2027. The United States has no comprehensive federal law, and Colorado repealed its broad AI discrimination law in May 2026 before it ever took effect.19 Law moves in years and models change in months, so the durable protection is the one this course keeps handing you: measurement that is valid, and accountability with a name attached.

5.8 Where you come in

Read this section's list again and notice what it is a list of. Every cost on it comes from a decision a person made: which data to train on, what to measure, where to put the line between the model and the person, and who answers when it goes wrong. None of those decisions is made by the model. They are made by people who understand both the business and the system, which is the job this course has been preparing you for.

The evidence so far points the same way. In the customer support study, the people who gained most from AI were the newest and least experienced, because the tool passed on what the best workers knew.7 In the Stanford payroll study, the young workers losing ground were in jobs where AI was doing the task instead of them. Where AI was helping people do the work, employment grew. And in the study of 21,559 firms, the heaviest AI users hired more new graduates, not fewer.1615 The difference between those two outcomes is how a company chooses to use the tool, and how well the person using it understands what it can and cannot do.

That is the positive note this chapter ends on, and it is not a small one. A medical student with a rare disease, a library of research nobody had connected, and a team that asked the right question found a treatment for a man who had been sent toward hospice. The technology made the search possible. People decided what to look for, judged what the machine returned, and took responsibility for acting on it. You now know enough to be one of those people: to use these tools well, to ask what they were trained on and how anyone knows they work, and to keep a person where a person belongs. The companies you join will need exactly that.

Key takeaways
  • An AI system is data, infrastructure, a model, its output, and the people who act on it. Most failures, and most fixes, live outside the model.
  • A supervised model is a machine for making the future resemble the labeled past. When the past is what you want more of, that is a superpower.
  • Accuracy hides more than it shows. Ask the base rate, which mistake the model makes, and who pays for each kind.
  • A model's decisions are its training data's decisions. Accountability belongs to the people who chose the data and shipped the system.
  • A language model predicts likely text, not true text. Ground it in real documents, evaluate it, and keep a person accountable for what it says.
  • AI takes the first draft and the routine case in every discipline. The exception, the judgment, and the accountability stay with people.
  • Every Cure shows the best of the technology and its two weak points: the thin end of the data, and the person who has to decide what to do with the score.
  • Every cost in section 5 traces to a human decision, which means every one can be decided better. People who understand both the business and the model are the ones who make those decisions.

6Vocabulary

Artificial intelligence / machine learning
Computers doing tasks that seem to require intelligence / learning patterns from examples instead of following written rules.
AI system / model
The whole arrangement of data, infrastructure, model, output, and people / the trained software at its center.
Training data / label
The past examples a model learns from, and the outcome attached to each.
Supervised / unsupervised learning
Learning from labeled outcomes vs. finding structure with no labels.
Inference
Using a trained model on new cases.
Neural network / deep learning
A model built from layers of simple units / a network with many layers, whose behavior is set by training rather than by written rules.
Collaborative filtering
Predicting what you will want from patterns across everyone's past behavior.
False positive / false negative
Saying yes when the answer is no / saying no when the answer is yes. They rarely cost the same.
Base rate
How common the predicted thing actually is; the number that makes accuracy meaningful.
Training-data bias
A skew in the examples, faithfully learned and repeated.
Proxy discrimination
A model rediscovering a removed attribute through stand-ins that go with it.
Feedback loop
Outputs that shape the next training data, letting a model confirm itself.
Large language model (LLM)
A deep learning model trained to predict the next piece of text; the engine of generative AI.
Hallucination
Fluent, confident output that is simply wrong.
Grounding
Giving a model the real documents to answer from, inside the request.
Fine-tuning / distillation
Adapting a model to one job / training a smaller, cheaper model to imitate a big one.
AI agent
A system in which a model takes actions with tools, not just answers.
Knowledge graph
Information stored as connections, so a system can follow paths nobody wrote down.
Drug repurposing
Using an approved drug for a disease it was not approved for.
Survivorship bias
Judging something only by the cases that made it far enough to be seen.
AI governance / bias audit
The accountability structure for models / testing whether outcomes differ across groups.
Risk-tier regulation
Rules that scale with the stakes of a use, from banned to lightly regulated.
Green · AI as coach and sparring partner

Fitting week for it: have an AI quiz you on this vocabulary, then run one live experiment on the chapter's own subject. Ask it a question about your hometown or a hobby you know deeply, and audit the answer for confident errors. You are performing evaluation, from section 2.3, on the tool itself. One standing rule extends the red light from Lab 1: never paste real people's personal or identifying data, resumes included, into an AI tool. You would be adding them to somebody's training data, which after this chapter needs no further explanation.
