Lab 10 · Week 10

Information Architecture: Making Sense of a Mess

Lab session · 80 minPairs with Chapter 13 · The Cloud →

Is a hotdog a sandwich? Come to lab this week and find out. The question sounds like a joke, and it is also the entire subject of this lab: who decides what category a thing belongs in, by what rule, and for whom. That decision is called information architecture, and every menu, folder, aisle, and website you have ever used is somebody's answer to it.

Contents Part 1 · Read  ·  Part 2 · Watch  ·  Part 3 · Find an example  ·  Part 4 · Model  ·  What to submit

Attending lab in person gives you the mini lecture and discussion that the assignment assumes. It is possible to complete the work without it, but you will be missing a lot of the context, and this week's lab quiz draws on both the lecture and the reading below.

Yellow · AI can explain the reading; it cannot do the sorting

Green: ask an AI to explain a term from Covert's book, or to describe a diagram type. Red: asking it to sort the card list, to draw your model, or to pick your real-world example. Card sorting is the exercise; an AI's categories tell us how it thinks, not how you do. Hand-sketched models are preferred for the same reason.

Part 1Read: excerpts from How to Make Sense of Any Mess

Abby Covert's book is free online, and the reading is short. Read these three sections; the quiz draws on them.

  • Chapter 2, part: State your intent (pages 32 to 36)
  • Chapter 3, part: Expand your diagramming toolbox (pages 65 to 75)
  • Chapter 6, all: Play with structure (pages 123 to 145)
Key conceptInformation architecture

The structure that makes a body of information navigable: what the categories are, what goes in each, what the labels say, and how a person moves between them. It is not the visual design and not the content. It is the decision about how the content is arranged, and Chapter 6 already told you why that decision matters: the shape you store facts in decides which questions are cheap to answer.

Part 2Watch: modeling and card sorting

  • Modeling for Clarity, The Understanding Group (16 minutes)

Part 3Find information architecture in the world

Choose one real information architecture: a grocery store's aisles, a university website, a streaming app's menu, a hospital's signage, a syllabus. Then answer, in writing:

  • Who matters? Use Covert's seven questions.
  • Is there a clear hierarchy? If so, how is it structured? Are there established taxonomies or relationships between terms?
  • Does the structure make sense to the people who matter? Or could it be improved? Remember that you are not your user.

Include a photo, screenshot, or sketch if it helps. This part is worth 10 points.

Part 4Practice diagramming: model the student experience

Create an information model that organizes the terms of your own student experience into a structure that makes sense. The rules:

  • Use one or more of the diagram types from Covert's Chapter 3.
  • Use at least twenty terms from the card sort list (in Canvas).
  • Hand-sketched is preferred; digital is fine.
  • Keep it low-fidelity. The diagram's job is to show the structure of your idea, not to look finished.

It does not have to be perfect. It is a rough model, and the interesting part is the choices it forces: which terms go together, what the groups are called, and what did not fit anywhere. This part is worth 10 points.

The hotdog question, answered the IA way

The question is not whether a hotdog is a sandwich. It is who needs the answer and what they will do with it. A menu designer, a tax authority, and a nutrition label all sort it differently, and each is right for their user. That is the whole lesson: categories are made, not found, and the maker's intent is the first thing to state.

Why this is the last lab

Every lab this quarter handed you a structure somebody else built: seven tables, fifteen columns, a schema. This one hands you the blank page. Chapter 13 ends on the same move, deciding what to rent and what to build; information architecture is deciding what to call things and where they go, which is the decision underneath every other one in this course.
