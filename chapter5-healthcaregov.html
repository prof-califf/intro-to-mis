On the morning of October 1, 2013, the United States government launched a website that had cost roughly half a billion dollars to build. Four million people visited it that day. Six of them managed to buy health insurance.

Learning objectives

By the end of this chapter you should be able to:

  1. Explain why requirements are the cheapest place to be wrong, and what feasibility analysis is for.
  2. Name the phases of the software development life cycle and place a failure in the phase it escaped.
  3. Distinguish waterfall from agile, and say what each one bets on.
  4. Write a testable requirement and explain what scope creep does to one.
  5. Read Healthcare.gov as a failure of analysis and governance rather than of code.

1Why systems analysis exists

Chapter 4 argued that a process has to be understood before it is worth automating. This chapter is about what happens when an organization has to build an information system anyway, at scale, for people it has never served before.

Nobody builds an information system by sitting down and writing code on day one. Before that, somebody has to figure out two things: what the system is supposed to do, and how it is going to do it. Figuring out what is called systems analysis. To do that, analysts ask questions such as: Who will use this system? What does it need to keep track of? What other systems does it have to talk to? How will we know it works? Figuring out how is called systems design. Systems design involves more technology-related questions like: How will the data be organized? Which parts of the information system do we build? Which parts can we buy? What will the person at the screen actually see (and what do they actually need to see)? Put the analysis and design together and you have systems analysis and design, which is what this chapter is about.

Think of building a house. Analysis is the conversation with the family about how many bedrooms they need and whether anyone uses a wheelchair. Design is the blueprint. Nobody pours concrete until both are done, because concrete is expensive to move. Software is the same, except that people skip the first two steps far more often than builders do, and that is where most of this chapter's disasters come from.

Notice that neither step writes any code, and that both are where the expensive decisions get made, because everything built afterwards assumes those decisions were right.

1.1 The cheapest place to be wrong

So, why should businesses bother with understanding analysis and design instead of just starting to build? Because mistakes get more expensive the longer they go unnoticed. A misunderstanding caught in a conversation costs a conversation. The same misunderstanding caught after system launch costs rework, retraining, and sometimes a congressional hearing, depending on the importance of the system. A common estimate is that the cost of fixing a mistake goes up about ten times at every phase it survives. So, analysis is not about being careful for its own sake. It is the cheapest place to be wrong.

Notice what is being decided during analysis, in the terms we discussed in Chapter 1. Nobody is choosing hardware yet, and nobody is writing software. What gets settled is what the system will record, which people will be responsible for which steps, and which other systems it must exchange data with. Those are the people, data, and network components, and they are being understood by conversations with human analysts, in meetings, months before anyone can point at a machine.

All of this has a point that you may notice throughout the chapter. Hardware and software can be replaced late; organizations do it constantly and it is merely expensive. A missing data requirement, an unassigned responsibility, or an unspecified boundary between two systems cannot be replaced late, because by then everything else has been built on the assumption that somebody had decided. Chapter 1's weakest component rule usually gets taught as a fact about running systems. It is at least as true about building them, and analysis is the phase where you choose which component will be the weak one.

Put it this way. You build every system twice, once in the specification and once in the code, and building it well the second time cannot rescue a bad job the first time. Nearly every large failure you will read about in this course is a failure of the first build, discovered during the second. So the question worth asking on any project you join is not whether the team is careful. It is where the team is spending its attention, and how early.

1.2 Feasibility, requirements, and scope creep

Three ideas do most of the work in analysis, and all three are ways of asking early a question somebody will otherwise ask late.

The first is feasibility analysis, which asks whether the project should exist at all, before anyone commits to building it. It runs on three axes. Technical: can this be built, with the people and the technology we can actually get? Economic: is it worth what it will cost? And operational: will this organization use it the way we are assuming, on this schedule? The third axis is the one that gets waved through, because it is the only one that requires somebody to say something unwelcome about their own employer.

The second is the system requirement, a statement of what the system must do, written down before anybody builds anything. Requirements are the hardest thing a project produces, because they ask people to state a need they have never had to put into words. When a large project fails, this is usually where it failed: the team built exactly what was specified, and what was specified was a misunderstanding. Section 2.5 comes back to how they are gathered and written.

The third is scope creep, which is what happens to requirements after the estimates are fixed. Changes keep arriving, each one reasonable on its own, and nobody moves the schedule to pay for them. Scope creep is rarely the result of anybody behaving badly. It is the result of every stakeholder, meaning every group with a stake in the system, pushing requirements in while no one person owns the whole.

Key conceptFeasibility and scope creep

Feasibility analysis asks whether a project should exist at all, on three axes: technical, economic, and operational. Scope creep is the slow expansion of what the system must do after the estimates are fixed. The two are connected. An operational answer of "only if the date moves" is the finding that would stop scope creep from being free, which is why it is the finding that gets softened.

Analysis also decides how to build the system in a sequence that keeps the risk survivable, which is what section 2 is about.

2The software development life cycle

Every information systems project, whatever it calls itself, passes through the same six phases: working out what is needed, deciding how to build it, building it, checking it, turning it on, and keeping it running. That sequence is called the software development life cycle (SDLC). The important thing to note about the SDLC is that each phase has a question attached, and skipping the question does not skip the consequence.

Keep one thing in mind as you read through the phases. A systems project is not just building software. It is assembling an information system, which means all five components have to arrive at the same moment and fit each other. The hardware has to be sized for a load somebody estimated. The software has to be written to a specification somebody wrote. The data structures have to be agreed on with whoever owns the data. The network connections have to be negotiated with other organizations. And the people have to be trained to use the result. Software is the component teams talk about most, because it is the one they build themselves. But it is not usually the one that fails. In the main case at the end of this chapter, the software mostly worked. Every other component was late, wrong, or belonged to somebody who had not been asked.

2.1 The six phases, and two ways through them

The SDLC is the standard sequence organizations use to move from "we need something" to "the system is running." The SDLC names vary by textbook and by firm; the phases do not. (Do not memorize any one firm's labels. Learn the six activities and you can translate whatever your employer calls them.) Here are the six phases:

  1. Planning, what problem are we solving, is it worth solving, what will it cost?
  2. Analysis, what exactly must the system do? Whose needs, in what order, measured how?
  3. Design, how will it be structured? Data, interfaces, integrations, architecture.
  4. Implementation, build, configure, and test it.
  5. Deployment, put it in front of real users.
  6. Maintenance, fix, extend, and eventually retire it.

The phases are a checklist of questions somebody has to answer, not a calendar. What varies between methods is how many times you go through them, which is the subject of 2.3.

2.2 The phase that never ends

Students, and a surprising number of executives, treat launch day as the end of a project. It is closer to the beginning of the system's life, the point where it starts serving real people. Most of the money is spent after launch day rather than before, and industry estimates commonly put maintenance above half of total lifetime cost.

Maintenance is also not one activity. It comes in four kinds, and only the first is the one you are probably picturing:

  • Corrective. Fixing defects that reached production.
  • Adaptive. Keeping up with a changing world outside the system: a new tax rule, a new browser, a supplier changing a data format.
  • Perfective. Improving what already works, because users have asked or because the team has learned something.
  • Preventive. Work done now to stop a future failure. It is the easiest to defer, it never shows up as a visible benefit, and deferring it is how technical debt accumulates.

One more activity belongs in the maintenance phase, and businesses almost always skip it. A post-implementation review asks, some months after launch, whether the system delivered what the business case promised, and what the organization should do differently next time. It is often skipped because everyone is exhausted, because the answer may be embarrassing, and because the people who made the decisions have often moved on. An organization that never does it cannot learn from its own projects, which is why the same failures keep appearing in this chapter across sixty years.

2.3 Waterfall versus agile, properly

Choosing between the two is a decision you will actually face. Waterfall runs through the phases once, in order, with signed-off documents at each one. Its strength is predictability: fixed scope, fixed price, a contract an organization can procure against. Its weakness is that all learning arrives at the end, when acting on it is most expensive. Agile inverts the bet. Work proceeds in sprints of one to four weeks, each ending in working software a real user can react to, and a product owner re-ranks what gets built next, so the plan absorbs learning instead of resisting it. Its weakness is the mirror image: scope and cost are uncertain up front, which contracts and legislatures hate.

The rule is this. Waterfall suits stable, well-understood requirements, such as a payroll migration. Agile suits discovery, meaning anything customer-facing being built for the first time. So the question to ask is not which method is better but how much of this system somebody already knows.

Neither one is a virtue, and you should be suspicious of anyone who presents them that way. They are two different bets about how much you can know in advance, and the only question worth asking is which bet the work in front of you supports.

2.4 Where these phases came from, and the sentence everyone misquotes

This argument is nearly sixty years old, which is worth knowing, because you are going to hear it presented as new every few years for the rest of your career.

  • 1968. A NATO conference in Garmisch, Germany gathers the people building large computer systems to discuss what they are calling the software crisis: projects late, over budget, and unreliable, at a rate that hardware engineering did not have. They coin the term software engineering to describe a discipline that does not exist yet.
  • 1970. Winston Royce publishes "Managing the Development of Large Software Systems," the paper universally cited as the origin of waterfall.
  • 1975. Fred Brooks publishes The Mythical Man-Month, written out of managing IBM's OS/360, and states the law named after him: adding people to a late software project makes it later. New people must be taught, and the teaching is done by the people already behind.
  • 2001. Seventeen practitioners meet at a ski lodge in Utah and write the Agile Manifesto, which is largely a list of things they had watched go wrong.

Now for the part about Royce that is worth knowing, because it is one of the most consequential misreadings in the history of this field. His paper does contain the famous diagram of phases flowing downward, one into the next. Immediately underneath it he writes that he believes in the concept, but that the implementation just described is risky and invites failure. The rest of the paper is about how to avoid doing it that way. His central recommendation, several figures later, is a heading that reads: attempt to do the job twice.

He never uses the word waterfall. The industry took his first diagram, discarded his warning and his proposed fix, gave the diagram his name, and built four decades of contracting practice on it. Then a later generation rebelled against "Royce's waterfall" without reading Royce either.

Take two things from that. The smaller one is a fact you can use: the founding document of waterfall says waterfall does not work. The larger one is about how ideas travel. A diagram is portable and a caveat is not, so the diagram survives the journey and the caveat does not. You will watch this happen to some framework in your own career, and it is worth being the person who has read the original.

2.5 Requirements: what they are, and where they come from

A requirement is a statement of something the system must do, or some quality it must have. Functional requirements describe behavior: "the system shall determine subsidy eligibility from household income and size." Non-functional requirements describe qualities: how fast, how many at once, how secure, how available. The non-functional ones are what organizations skip, and they are what produce spectacular public failures, because you cannot see them missing until load arrives.

Missing non-functional requirements are also the ones that spread. A functional requirement nobody wrote produces one feature nobody built. A non-functional requirement nobody wrote, such as how many people will use this at once, gets inherited by everything downstream: how many servers to buy, how to structure the database, what to do with people who arrive while the system is busy. Each of those decisions has to assume a number. If nobody supplied one, each team assumes its own, and nobody finds out whose was wrong until the real number shows up.

Watch for this pattern

When a deadline is fixed by forces outside the project (a law, an earnings call, a trade show), requirements become the only variable left that leadership can quietly compress. Scope, schedule, and quality form a triangle, usually called the iron triangle: fix two and the third moves on its own. On Healthcare.gov, schedule and scope were both politically untouchable. Quality was the only side left free to give.

Requirements also do not arrive on their own. Somebody goes and gets them, and the work has a name, requirements elicitation. Each standard method has a characteristic blind spot, which is why serious projects use several.

  • Interviews. Ask people what they need. Fast and essential, and limited by the fact that people describe the job they think they do rather than the job they do.
  • Observation. Watch the work happen. Slow, and the only method that reliably finds the workarounds, the second spreadsheet, and the step everybody performs and nobody mentions.
  • Document analysis. Read the forms, reports, and rules the current process produces. Good for regulated work, and it tells you what was true when the document was written.
  • Workshops. Put the disagreeing parties in one room and make them settle it in front of each other. Expensive in calendar time, and the only method that surfaces conflicts between stakeholders rather than collecting them separately.
  • Prototypes. Show people something and watch them react. People are far better at criticizing a thing than describing one, which is the single most useful fact in this section.

The person who does this work has a job title, and it is one many of you will hold: business analyst. The role sits between the people who need the system and the people who will build it, and its output is the specification everything downstream depends on.

Notice what none of these methods can do on its own. Every one collects requirements from somebody who already exists and can be asked. When a system is for people who have never done this before, there is no process to observe and no experienced user to interview, and prototypes and pilots stop being good practice and become the only honest source of information. Hold that until the main case.

2.6 Writing a requirement down

Gathering a requirement and recording it are different jobs, and the second is where projects quietly go wrong. A requirement that lives in somebody's memory of a meeting is not a requirement. The standard way to write one down is the user story, and the format is deliberately rigid:

As a [kind of person], I want [to do something], so that [I get some result].

The third clause is the one that matters and the one people drop. "As a shopper, I want to create an account" is not a user story, because it never says what the account is for. Written properly, "so that I can see the prices I qualify for" invites the obvious question: does the shopper need an account before seeing prices, or after? Healthcare.gov answered that late and wrongly, and the format would have forced the argument to happen earlier.

A story on its own is still not enough, because two people can agree with a sentence and mean different things by it. So each story carries acceptance criteria: specific, checkable conditions that decide whether it is finished. Not "the site should be fast," which nobody can pass or fail, but "a page returns in under two seconds with fifty thousand people on the site at once." Notice what writing that sentence required: a number. It is the number CMS never had.

The rule to remember: a requirement nobody can test is not a requirement, it is a hope. Teams also agree on a definition of done, a standing checklist that applies to everything, covering things like code review, tests written, and documentation updated, so that "done" means the same thing in March as it does in September.

2.7 Deciding what not to build

Every project asks for more than it can have, so somebody has to choose, and one day that will be you. If you do it by argument in a meeting, the result is whatever the most senior person in the room wants. If you do it with a method, the result is something you can defend later.

The common method is MoSCoW, which sorts every requirement into four boxes:

  • Must have. Without it the system has no reason to exist. On Healthcare.gov, a person can compare plans and enroll in one.
  • Should have. Important, and the system is still usable without it for a while.
  • Could have. Desirable, and the first thing dropped when time runs short.
  • Won't have, this time. Explicitly excluded, written down, and agreed. This box is the one teams skip, and it is the most useful of the four, because an excluded requirement stops being an argument.

The related idea is the minimum viable product, the smallest version that delivers real value to a real user, released so you can learn from what happens. It is often misused as a polite word for unfinished. The distinction is whether you are shipping less in order to learn something, or shipping less because you ran out of time.

Keep that fourth box in mind when you read the rescue in the main case. The team that fixed Healthcare.gov did not have better technology than the team that broke it. It had permission to move things into the fourth box and say so out loud.

Prioritizing once is not enough, because requirements keep arriving. So what stops scope creep? Not willpower. A procedure. First, agree on a baseline of what is being built. Then require every addition to arrive as a change request that names its cost in time and money, and gets approved or refused by someone accountable. The account-creation mandate that arrived weeks before Healthcare.gov's launch was a substantial change to the system's busiest path. Requirements that arrive that late are not automatically wrong. Requirements that arrive that late without anyone pricing them are.

2.8 Testing is not one thing

"We tested it" is not a statement you should accept, because testing happens at four different levels and they catch entirely different problems. A project can do three of them thoroughly and fail on the fourth.

The four levels of testing
LevelWhat it checksWhat it cannot catch
UnitOne piece of code does what its author intended, on its ownAnything about how that piece talks to another piece
IntegrationTwo or more parts work correctly together, including systems owned by other organizationsWhether the assembled whole behaves under real conditions
SystemThe whole thing, end to end, including how it behaves under expected loadWhether it fits the way people actually work
User acceptanceReal users, doing real tasks, decide whether it is fit to releaseNothing, but only if the users are real and the tasks are real

Now take the two failures from section 3 and place them on that table. Mars Climate Orbiter passed unit testing twice, once on each team's software, and had no integration testing that would have compared the two across the boundary. Healthcare.gov, which you will meet in section 4, had no realistic system testing under load and no user acceptance testing worth the name, because the public met the system on the morning it went live. CrowdStrike's update passed its testing and was destroyed by none of these four, because the thing that failed was not the software but the decision to give it to everyone at once, and nobody tests a rollout.

Load testing deserves its own sentence, because it is the level that turns a missing requirement into a missing test. You can only test under the volume somebody specified. Where nobody specified one, there is no load test that can be designed, and the gap is invisible, because the test plan looks complete and passes. Missing requirements do not just produce missing features. They produce missing tests.

Integration testing deserves a second look, because it raises a question testing alone cannot answer: whose job is the boundary? Most systems of any size are several systems, built by different teams or companies, exchanging data across boundaries. Each boundary is a place where two groups can both be right and the result still be wrong, because each tested against its own understanding of what crosses.

So somebody has to own the whole, and that role has a name. The systems integrator is the party accountable for making independently built components work as one system. It is not the same job as building the biggest piece, and it cannot be done part-time by whoever happens to be largest.

Where the role is vacant, a distinctive failure follows, and it does not look like failure until the end. Every vendor delivers its own piece to its own specification. Every piece passes its own tests. The assembled system does not work, nobody has breached a contract, and there is no clause to invoke. The main case has this in its most expensive published form, plus the version that makes it worse: the role was not merely empty, it was believed to be filled.

2.9 Rollout strategy: how you turn it on

Finally, the system has to be turned on. You have four ways to move users from an old world to a new one, and choosing among them is a risk decision rather than a technical one:

  • Direct cutover ("big bang"), everyone moves at once. Cheapest, fastest, and the only option with no fallback.
  • Parallel, old and new run side by side until the new one is trusted. Safest and most expensive; you pay for both.
  • Phased, release functionality in stages.
  • Pilot conversion, one region, office, or user group first, then expand.

Notice that the four are not equally risky, and that the riskiest one is the cheapest to plan and the most expensive to be wrong about. The consequence of direct cutover is that the first honest test of the system is also the moment of maximum public exposure, and there is nothing to return to. A big-bang cutover of a system whose requirements were never settled is the most repeated failure pattern in this book. It is about to appear in section 3.2, at a company with a great deal of money and very good engineers, and again in section 4 at national scale. Learn the shape of it now, because when you hear "big bang" in a meeting the useful question is short: what is the fallback? If the answer is that there is none, ask again, because the first answer is usually optimistic.

3Two short failures, two phases

Before the main case, here are two short ones. The chapter has just claimed that the cost of fixing a mistake rises by roughly ten times at each phase it survives. That claim is easier to believe when you watch it happen. Each failure below escaped a different phase, and in each one the fix was available one phase earlier and would have cost almost nothing there. Both had working code.

3.1 Mini-case 1 · 1999: the spacecraft that used the wrong units

NASA launched the Mars Climate Orbiter in December 1998 on a mission costing around $193 million. Nine months later it reached Mars, fired its engine to enter orbit, and was never heard from again. It had come in far too low and broke apart in the atmosphere.

The investigation found no broken equipment and no failed component. Ground software built by Lockheed Martin reported the force of each small thruster firing in pound-force seconds, the unit used in the United States. The navigation software at NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory read those numbers as newton-seconds, the metric unit. One pound-force is about 4.45 newtons, so every reading was wrong by that factor, consistently, for nine months.

Both programs worked exactly as written. What nobody had specified was the interface between them: which unit crosses the boundary between one team's software and another team's software. That is a requirement, and it is the kind of requirement that feels too obvious to write down.

The phase it escaped: analysis. The component: data. A single line in a specification, agreed in a meeting years earlier, would have cost nothing. Instead the error survived design, coding, testing, launch, and nine months of flight, and was found by the planet.

Unit testing: each team tested its own software against its own specification, and each passed, because each was internally consistent. What was missing was integration testing, the test that runs the two together across the boundary and checks whether what one sends is what the other expects. That test would have caught the units in an afternoon, because a value in pounds-force fed to software expecting newtons is off by a factor of about 4.5, which is not subtle. The cheap phase was requirements: one line in the interface specification saying which unit crossed the boundary. It was never written, so nobody tested for it, and a spacecraft went into the atmosphere of Mars.

3.2 Mini-case 2 · 2024: the update that went everywhere at once

Early on 19 July 2024, airport check-in screens around the world went blue at the same moment and gate agents reached for pens. CrowdStrike sells security software that runs deep inside Windows, which is what lets it stop attacks and also what makes it dangerous when it is wrong. That morning the company released a routine content update to that software, and it went to customers worldwide at essentially the same moment.

The update carried a fault that crashed Windows machines on startup, and again on the next startup, so they could not be repaired remotely. Microsoft later estimated about 8.5 million devices were affected. Airlines grounded flights and went back to handwritten boarding passes, hospitals postponed procedures, and broadcasters went off air. Because the machines would not boot, much of the recovery had to be done by a person physically visiting each one.

The software had been through testing. What it had not been through was a rollout. Updates of this kind went to everyone at once, so there was no group of customers who would hit the fault first and no interval in which anyone could notice and stop it. Among the changes the company committed to afterwards were releasing these updates in stages and letting customers choose when to take them.

The phase it escaped: deployment. The component: software, released without a rollout. Every customer was, in effect, in the first wave. Read that against the four rollout strategies in section 2, and note that the remedy adopted afterwards was one of them, available all along.

The argument for speed is real: an update that arrives a day late leaves every machine exposed for a day. It does not survive the numbers. The update was correct on almost every machine and wrong on a class of them, and a staged rollout to one percent would have found that class in minutes with the other 99 percent still running. The delay was worth hours; the outage cost airlines, hospitals, and banks days. So the special case does not exempt security software from rollout strategy. It changes the size of the first wave, not whether there is one.

3.3 What the two have in common

Look at what was working in each case. Two spacecraft teams whose software both passed its own tests. A security update that had been tested and was correct on almost every machine that received it. In neither case was the failure a matter of somebody writing bad instructions.

What each of them lacked was a check that belonged to an earlier phase and would have been cheap there: an interface specification, a staged release. That is the cost pattern from section 1, shown twice. The mistake does not become expensive because it is serious. It becomes expensive because it survives.

Now read the main case with one idea in front of you. Healthcare.gov is not one of these two failures. It is both of them at once, plus a rollout with nothing to fall back to, at national scale, on a date set by law.

4Main case · Healthcare.gov: the first day

You now have every concept this case needs. Here it is in the order it happened, which is the only order in which it makes sense. Start with the dates, because the dates are the argument. The decisions that settled the outcome are three years older than the day you have heard about.

  • Mar 2010The Affordable Care Act becomes law. It requires health insurance marketplaces to be running by 1 January 2014, with enrollment opening 1 October 2013.
  • Sep 2011CMS contracts for development of the federal marketplace, with key requirements still unwritten.
  • 2012–2013Contracts multiply. No lead systems integrator is ever named.
  • Sep 2013Governance reviews are delayed. The decision is made to launch on schedule with the full feature set.

  • Early OctThe site is largely unusable. The failure becomes a national political event.

4.1 Before the technology: a deadline that came from a law

In March 2010 the Affordable Care Act became law. Among many other things, it required that health insurance marketplaces be operating by 1 January 2014, with enrollment opening on 1 October 2013. States could build their own marketplace or leave it to the federal government. Thirty-six of them left it to the federal government, which meant that the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services, an agency whose existing job was paying claims, now had to build a consumer shopping website for most of the country.

Read that as a feasibility question, using the three axes from section 1.2. Technically it was demanding but ordinary: show people a catalog, check their eligibility, take an order. Economically nobody seriously asked, because the law had already decided. Operationally it was in trouble from the first day, and this is the part worth slowing down for. The launch date did not come from the work. It came from a statute, which meant it could not move, which meant that of the three sides of the iron triangle two were fixed before anyone estimated anything. There was one side left.

Notice also what this does to the people component. The organization that had to make the judgment calls did not employ many people who had built a consumer website, so the judgment was bought rather than held. That is a legitimate thing to do, and it is also the decision that everything after this follows from.

4.2 What they built, and what they expected from it

The thing being built was not one system. The federal marketplace had to answer one question about each visitor, in real time, while that visitor waited: who is this person, and what are they entitled to? No single agency holds that answer. Income is at the IRS. Identity is at the Social Security Administration. Immigration status is at the Department of Homeland Security. Whether somebody qualifies for Medicaid instead is a state question. The plans and prices belong to private insurers.

So the marketplace was really a set of live connections to organizations that CMS did not control, each one a hand-off, assembled under contract. By 2013 CMS had awarded 60 contracts across 33 different companies.

Now the expectation, which matters more than it looks. It would be convenient to say everyone involved knew this was going badly. They did not. The job really was unremarkable in its parts, each contractor really was competent at its own piece, and the pieces really were being delivered. Senior people believed the site would open and work, and said so in public. That belief was the predictable result of a structure in which everybody could see their own piece working and nobody was responsible for the whole. If you do not believe the expectation, the launch is just a fact. It is a lesson only if you can see why intelligent people held it.

4.3 October 1, 2013

Four million people visited on the first day. The site failed within about two hours. Demand of roughly 250,000 simultaneous users, around five times what had been planned for, took it down almost immediately, and most visitors met crashes, timeouts, and errors for the rest of the week. Congress had shut down the federal government that same morning in an attempt to defund the law, so the launch happened in front of the largest possible audience.

The next morning, in a government crisis meeting, somebody read out the number of people who had managed to buy insurance on day one. Six. Across five insurers.

4.4 The diagnosis: four breaks, none of them code

It would be comfortable to file this away as a website that could not handle traffic. That reading is wrong in a way that would cost you money if you carried it into a job. Capacity was a symptom. Underneath it were four decisions, and you have met all four already as concepts.

A requirement nobody wrote. CMS issued task orders to build the marketplace and the data hub while key technical requirements were still unknown, including which states it would serve and how many people might enroll. Contracts were signed to build a thing whose size nobody had specified, so every capacity decision inherited a blank and no load test could be designed against a number that did not exist.

An integration nobody owned. The Inspector General's review found that CMS awarded those 60 contracts across 33 companies and never designated any one of them as the project lead. The agency "missed the opportunity," in the review's language, to name a single point of contact responsible for integrating the contractors' work. Worse than vacant, the role was misunderstood: CMS believed the contractor CGI was serving as lead integrator, and CGI did not share that understanding of its role.

A rollout with nothing to fall back to. CMS chose direct cutover. Every feature, available to every American, on one date. A pilot was available and obvious: one state, or federal employees, or a limited regional beta months ahead of the deadline. It was not taken.

Gates that were not allowed to say no. This is the fourth break and the one that let the other three survive. With months left, CMS raised concerns about the lead contractor's performance without holding it accountable, moved staff into the contractor's offices in September 2013, delayed the governance reviews that would have surfaced the problems, and proceeded without the required approvals. Read that order again. The reviews that existed to stop the launch were postponed because the launch was near.

The contracting structure removed the last brake. CMS did not check the federal past-performance database before awarding to CGI Federal, a contractor with prior missteps at the department. For five of the six main contracts it chose contract types that put the risk of cost overruns on the government rather than the vendor. Those six contracts grew from $464 million to $824 million, one of them from $58 million to $207 million.

Notice what is absent. Nobody wrote catastrophically bad code. The failure lives in a requirement never specified, a role never assigned, a rollout never staged, and gates never enforced, which are decisions made by managers rather than engineers. That is why this material sits in a business school course.

4.5 The recovery, and what it proves

The repair is as instructive as the failure, because it shows the problem was solvable the whole time. In late October, management was centralized under a single accountable leader, Jeffrey Zients. The White House technology leadership was restructured, underperforming staff and contractors were replaced, and senior engineers were recruited from industry. A small team was given one narrow mandate and the authority to prioritize ruthlessly.

Over roughly six weeks, about 400 defects were resolved, concurrency was raised to 25,000 users, and response time was brought to about one second. By 1 December the system was handling something like 35,000 concurrent users. Federal marketplace enrollment went from 26,794 in October to roughly 975,000 in December alone, and the first open enrollment period closed in March 2014 with 5.4 million plan selections.

Six weeks. The same law, the same insurers, the same essential technical problem. What changed was accountability, prioritization, and permission to ship less. Shipping less is not free: every feature deferred and every shortcut taken to make the site work in December became technical debt, a cost the organization pays later in exchange for moving now. It was the right trade, and it was still a debt.

The durable lesson

The rescue team did nothing that could not have been done during the original build. It made decisions the original governance structure was not organized to make: name one owner, cut scope to what works, test under real load, ship in stages. The failure was not a technical limit. It was an organizational choice, and organizational choices can be made differently.

4.6 The five components at Healthcare.gov

4.7 What happened next, which is not a tidy ending

The failure had institutional consequences, and they are worth following, because most textbooks stop at the recovery.

Britain had created the Government Digital Service in 2011 to build public services with government employees rather than contractors. In 2014, directly in response to Healthcare.gov, the United States created two similar organizations. One was the United States Digital Service, inside the Executive Office of the President. The other was 18F, inside the General Services Administration, named for the agency's address at 1800 F Street. Both hired designers, engineers and product managers onto the public payroll, and both worked on the procurement practices this chapter has been criticizing.

They built things you have probably used: Login.gov, the single sign-on for federal services; cloud.gov; and the U.S. Web Design System, which is why federal websites now look and behave alike. 18F supported the IRS in building Direct File, free online tax filing, which ran for two filing seasons.

Then the arrangement came apart, and it is worth being precise, because the loose version of this story is wrong in both directions. In January 2025 an executive order renamed the Digital Service as the United States DOGE Service and created a temporary organization inside it, due to dissolve on 4 July 2026. On 1 March 2025, weeks before its eleventh anniversary, 18F was eliminated and its staff laid off. In early November 2025 the IRS told its Direct File partner states the service would not run in the 2026 filing season and that no future date had been set. Later that month the head of the Office of Personnel Management said the temporary organization no longer existed as a centralized entity, while a spokesperson said it did and named an acting administrator. The renamed Digital Service itself continued, and in April 2026 one of its directors described it as growing, with roughly 90 staff placed across agencies.

Set that beside the rest of the chapter without deciding how you feel about it yet. The lesson Healthcare.gov taught in 2013 was that capability has to live inside the organization that needs it, because you cannot contract for judgment. Of the two organizations built on that lesson, one lasted about eleven years and the other still exists under a different name and a different mandate. Whether that is evidence the response was wrong, evidence that this capability is unusually hard to sustain, or evidence of something else is a real question, and this book will not hand you the answer. What is not in question is the finding underneath it, which is older than both agencies: somebody has to own the whole, and that somebody has to be allowed to say no.

4.8 What's next: systems analysis in 2026

This case is from 2013, and you are entitled to ask whether any of it still applies. There are two answers and they point in different directions.

The cost of doing it properly has collapsed. Almost every failure in this chapter was expensive to prevent at the time and is close to free to prevent now.

  • The pilot is now a setting. A feature flag lets a team release something to one percent of users, watch, and expand or switch it off within minutes. CMS was told a pilot would cost months it did not have. Today it costs an afternoon, which removes the last respectable excuse for direct cutover.
  • Releases go out in stages. The tooling to give an update to one percent of customers and then expand is standard, which is why the company in section 3.2 had it available and why its own remedy afterwards was to start using it.
  • Capacity is rented by the minute. Load testing against a realistic volume no longer requires buying the hardware to test with, which is how Chapter 1's Spotify example ran.
  • Systems watch themselves. Monitoring now routes an alert to a named person who is on duty and treats an unanswered alert as its own failure, rather than sending mail nobody reads.

And yet projects still fail. Not as often at this scale, but often enough that the industry keeps producing case studies, and the explanation is the whole chapter: none of the tools above touches what actually broke here. No pipeline tells you which states the system must serve. No feature flag tells you nobody owns the integration. No monitoring system tells a government that a legal deadline is not a project plan. The tooling removed the excuses and left the decisions exactly where they were.

AI has sharpened that rather than softened it. When writing code was the slow part, a vague requirement could hide behind the build, because the build took long enough for somebody to notice. Now that a model can produce a working draft in an afternoon, the specification is a larger share of what is left, not a smaller one, and a model that builds the wrong system builds it faster and more convincingly. Chapter 4 made this point about processes. It is the same point about projects.

4.9 What a business technology manager takes from this chapter

Strip the case down to what you can reuse anywhere. Four decisions came up in it, and you will be in the room for every one of them.

Write the requirements down, including the ones that sound obvious. Nobody wrote down how many people the site had to serve at once, so contracts were signed to build a thing whose size nobody had specified. The requirement that feels too obvious to state is the one that will not get tested. If a number matters, it goes on paper before anyone builds anything.

Give every connection an owner. The site depended on live links to the IRS, Social Security, and Homeland Security, and no single person owned the whole chain. Every hand-off between two teams or two organizations is a place a system fails, and the cheapest fix in this book is a name next to each one.

Never launch everything to everyone on a date you cannot move. A pilot was available and obvious and was not taken. Every rollout strategy in section 2.9 was cheaper than the one CMS chose. When you hear "big bang," ask what the fallback is, and if there is none, ask again.

Make sure somebody is allowed to say no. The reviews that existed to stop the launch were postponed because the launch was near. Governance is not paperwork. It is the decision, made in advance, about who can stop a project and on what evidence. If the answer is nobody, the project will launch on the date regardless of what the tests say, because that is what a deadline does to an organization with no brake.

5The downside: who waited

The engineering story has a human side that is easy to lose. The people refreshing Healthcare.gov on October 1 were not users testing a product. They were people without health insurance, many attempting to buy it for the first time in their lives, some with conditions that had made them uninsurable until that morning. Every week the site did not work was a week they stayed uninsured, and the enrollment deadline bearing down on the project was also bearing down on them. When this course says a launch failed, keep asking the second question: failed for whom, and what did the failure cost people who had no seat in any status meeting. The contractors were paid for the overruns. The waiting was done by the public.

It is worth saying who was inside the recovery too: a small team with clear ownership, real usage data, and permission to tell the truth about what was broken. The difference between the three years and the six weeks was not talent. It was structure, honesty, and the political cover to have both.

Key takeaways
  • Requirements are the cheapest place to be wrong, because analysis is where you choose which of the five components will be the weak one.
  • Every project passes through the same activities. The names vary; the phase a mistake escapes from decides what it costs.
  • A requirement nobody can test is not a requirement, it is a hope. Healthcare.gov never had the number of users it was supposed to handle.
  • Waterfall bets that you can know everything up front. Agile bets that you cannot, and builds so the plan absorbs learning. Royce, who is credited with waterfall, recommended doing the job twice.
  • Modern tooling made every failure in this chapter cheap to prevent. Projects fail anyway, because the tools removed the excuses and left the decisions where they were.

6Vocabulary

User story
A requirement written as: as a kind of person, I want to do something, so that I get some result. The third clause is the one people drop.
Acceptance criteria
Specific, checkable conditions that decide whether a requirement is finished. A requirement nobody can test is not a requirement, it is a hope.
MoSCoW
Sorting requirements into must have, should have, could have, and won't have this time.
Minimum viable product
The smallest version that delivers real value to a real user, released in order to learn from what happens.
Change request
A new or altered requirement arriving after the baseline is agreed, priced in time and money, and approved or refused by someone accountable.
Business analyst
The person who gathers requirements and specifies what a system must do, working between the people who need it and the people who will build it.
Requirements elicitation
The work of finding out what a system must do, through interviews, observation, document analysis, workshops, and prototypes. Each method has a different blind spot.
Integration testing
Checking that two or more parts work correctly together, including systems owned by other organizations. The level that would have caught the Mars Climate Orbiter. User acceptance testing is the level above it: real users doing real tasks, deciding whether the system is fit to release.
Iron triangle
Scope, schedule, and quality. Fix any two of them and the third moves on its own.
Systems analysis
Determining what a system must do and for whom, before construction begins; its output is the requirements.
Systems design
Determining how a system will meet its requirements: how its data, software, hardware, networks, and people fit together; its output is the plan the builders work from.
SDLC
The phased sequence from planning through maintenance that structures system development.
Functional requirement
A statement of behavior the system must perform. A non-functional requirement states a required quality instead: speed, capacity, availability, security.
Waterfall
One pass through the phases with document sign-offs; predictable, and all learning arrives at the end.
Agile / sprint
Short cycles each ending in working software; a product owner re-ranks priorities as learning arrives.
Systems integrator
The party accountable for making independently built components work as one system.
Direct cutover
Moving all users to a new system at once, with no fallback. "Big bang." Its opposite is pilot conversion, deploying to a limited group first so defects surface at low exposure.
Stage gate
A scheduled review with authority to halt a project before the next phase.
Technical debt
Future cost incurred by shipping a shortcut now.
