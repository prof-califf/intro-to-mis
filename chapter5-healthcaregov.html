Chapter 5 · Systems Analysis and the Life Cycle

Systems Analysis: Why Healthcare.gov Failed Before Anyone Wrote a Line of Code

A website that had cost roughly half a billion dollars opened to four million people and enrolled six of them. Nobody wrote catastrophically bad code. This chapter is about the decisions made three years earlier that had already settled the outcome.

On the morning of October 1, 2013, the United States government launched a website that had cost roughly half a billion dollars to build. Four million people visited it that day. Six of them managed to buy health insurance.

Learning objectives

By the end of this chapter you should be able to:

  1. Explain why requirements are the cheapest place to be wrong, and what feasibility analysis is for.
  2. Name the phases of the software development life cycle and place a failure in the phase it escaped.
  3. Distinguish waterfall from agile, and say what each one bets on.
  4. Write a testable requirement and explain what scope creep does to one.
  5. Read Healthcare.gov as a failure of analysis and governance rather than of code.

01Why systems analysis exists

Chapter 4 argued that a process has to be understood before it is worth automating. This chapter is about what happens when an organization has to build the system anyway, at scale, for people it has never served before. The work of deciding what a system must do, before anyone builds it, is called systems analysis, and it is unglamorous enough that most projects do less of it than they should.

The reason to do it is arithmetic rather than virtue. A misunderstanding caught in a conversation costs a conversation. The same misunderstanding caught after launch costs rework, retraining, and sometimes a congressional hearing, and the cost rises by roughly ten times at every phase it survives. Analysis is not carefulness for its own sake. It is the cheapest place to be wrong.

Notice what is being decided during analysis, in the terms of Chapter 1. Nobody is choosing hardware yet, and nobody is writing software. What gets settled is what the system will record, which people will be responsible for which steps, and which other systems it must exchange data with. Those are the data, people and network components, and they are being fixed by conversation, in meetings, months before anyone can point at a machine.

That has a consequence worth carrying through the chapter. Hardware and software can be replaced late; organizations do it constantly and it is merely expensive. A missing data requirement, an unassigned responsibility, or an unspecified boundary between two systems cannot be replaced late, because by then everything else has been built on the assumption that somebody had decided. Chapter 1's weakest component rule usually gets taught as a fact about running systems. It is at least as true about building them, and analysis is the phase where you choose which component will be the weak one.

Two ideas do most of the work in this section, and both are ways of asking a question early that somebody will otherwise ask late.

Key conceptFeasibility and scope creep

Before the SDLC begins, feasibility analysis asks whether the project should exist at all, on three axes: technical (can it be built?), economic (is it worth what it costs?), and operational (will the organization actually use it as intended, on this schedule?). Healthcare.gov passed the first, never seriously ran the second, and failed the third before a line of code existed: the launch date was set by law, not by the work. Scope creep is the slow expansion of what the system must do after estimates are fixed. The late requirement forcing users to create accounts before browsing is scope creep in its natural habitat, arriving with good intentions and no schedule adjustment. Every stakeholder, each group with a stake in the system: insurers, states, regulators, the uninsured public, pushed requirements in, and no one owned the whole.

Key conceptRequirements

A requirement is a statement of what a system must do, written before anyone builds anything, and requirements are the hardest artifact in software because they demand that people articulate needs they have never had to state. Most catastrophic projects fail here, not in the code: the system that gets built faithfully implements a misunderstanding. Healthcare.gov’s late decision to force account creation before browsing, which multiplied load on the weakest part of the system, was a requirements change arriving after the architecture was set.

Systems analysis is the work of figuring out what a system must do before anyone builds it, and of deciding how to build it in a sequence that keeps the risk survivable. It is the least glamorous phase of any technology project and the one that most reliably determines whether the project lives or dies.

The reason is arithmetic. A misunderstanding caught during analysis costs a conversation. The same misunderstanding caught after go-live costs rework, emergency labor, lost customers, and as Healthcare.gov demonstrated, congressional hearings. The cost of fixing a defect rises by roughly an order of magnitude at each stage it survives. Analysis is where that arithmetic is cheapest.

The core idea

Every system is built twice: once in the specification, and once in the code. Building it well the second time cannot rescue a bad job the first time. Most large failures you will read about in this course are failures of the first build, discovered during the second.

02The software development life cycle

Every project, whatever it calls itself, passes through the same handful of activities: working out what is needed, deciding how to build it, building it, checking it, turning it on, and keeping it running. That sequence is the software development life cycle, and the useful thing about it is not the diagram. It is that each phase has a question attached, and skipping the question does not skip the consequence.

What projects actually differ on is the order and the size of the loop: whether you finish each phase before starting the next, or run all of them repeatedly on a small piece of the system. That is the argument between the two approaches below, and it is the one decision in this section that Healthcare.gov got wrong before a line of code existed.

One thing to hold on to as you read the phases. A project is not building software. It is assembling an information system, which means five components have to arrive at the same moment and fit each other. The hardware sized for a load somebody estimated. The software written to a specification somebody wrote. The data structures agreed with whoever owns the data. The network connections negotiated with other organizations. And the people trained to use the result. Software is the component teams talk about most because it is the one they build themselves. It is not usually the one that fails. In the case at the end of this chapter, the software mostly worked. Every other component was late, wrong, or belonged to somebody who had not been asked.

Key conceptWaterfall and agile

Waterfall builds in sequential phases, requirements through testing, each completed before the next begins; it assumes you can know the requirements up front. Agile builds in short cycles, shipping small working slices and correcting course from real feedback; it assumes you cannot. Neither is virtue. Waterfall suits stable, regulated, contract-bound work; agile suits uncertainty. Healthcare.gov ran waterfall against maximal uncertainty with a fixed public deadline, which is the worst available pairing, and the rescue that followed was agile in everything but name.

The SDLC is the standard sequence organizations use to move from "we need something" to "it is running." Names vary by textbook and by firm; the phases do not. (Do not memorize any one firm's labels. Learn the six activities and you can translate whatever your employer calls them.) The phases:

  1. Planning, what problem are we solving, is it worth solving, what will it cost?
  2. Analysis, what exactly must the system do? Whose needs, in what order, measured how?
  3. Design, how will it be structured? Data, interfaces, integrations, architecture.
  4. Implementation, build, configure, and test it.
  5. Deployment, put it in front of real users.
  6. Maintenance, fix, extend, and eventually retire it.

The phases are a checklist of questions that must be answered, not a calendar. What varies between methodologies is how many times you go around the loop. A waterfall approach runs the sequence once, at full scale. An agile or iterative approach runs it many times on small slices, so that each pass produces something real and each mistake is small. Healthcare.gov ran it once, at national scale, on a fixed political deadline. Hold that thought.

The phase that never ends

Students, and a surprising number of executives, treat launch day as the end of a project. It is closer to the beginning. Across a system's life, most of the money is spent after go-live, not before, and industry estimates commonly put maintenance at more than half of total lifetime cost. Healthcare.gov did not finish in December 2013. It has been maintained, changed and re-tested every year since.

Maintenance is also not one activity. It comes in four kinds, and only the first is what most people picture:

  • Corrective. Fixing defects that reached production.
  • Adaptive. Keeping up with a changing world outside the system: a new tax rule, a new browser, a supplier changing a data format.
  • Perfective. Improving what already works, because users have asked or because the team has learned something.
  • Preventive. Work done now to stop a future failure. It is the easiest to defer, it never shows up as a visible benefit, and deferring it is how technical debt accumulates.

One more thing belongs here and is almost always skipped. A post-implementation review asks, some months after launch, whether the system delivered what the business case promised, and what the organization should do differently next time. It is skipped because everyone is exhausted, because the answer may be embarrassing, and because the people who made the decisions have often moved on. An organization that never does it cannot learn from its own projects, which is why the same failures keep appearing in this chapter across sixty years.

Waterfall versus agile, properly

The two methodologies deserve more than a sentence, because choosing between them is a decision you will actually face. Waterfall runs the phases once, in order, with signed-off documents at each gate. Its strength is predictability: fixed scope, fixed price, a contract a government can procure against, which is exactly why federal contracting pushed Healthcare.gov toward it. Its weakness is that all learning arrives at the end, when it is most expensive to act on. Agile inverts the bet. Work proceeds in sprints of one to four weeks, each ending in working software a real user can react to. A product owner continuously re-ranks what gets built next, so the plan absorbs learning instead of resisting it. Its weakness is the mirror image: scope and cost are uncertain up front, which contracts and legislatures hate.

The honest rule: waterfall suits stable, well-understood requirements (a payroll migration); agile suits discovery (anything customer-facing being built for the first time). A national insurance marketplace nobody had ever built, for users nobody had met, was a discovery problem procured as a stable one. The recovery team's six weeks of ship-test-fix loops was, in everything but name, agile: adopted at last, under duress, in production.

Where these phases came from, and the sentence everyone misquotes

It is worth knowing that this argument is nearly sixty years old, because you are going to hear it presented as new every few years for the rest of your career.

  • 1968. A NATO conference in Garmisch, Germany gathers the people building large computer systems to discuss what they are calling the software crisis: projects late, over budget, and unreliable, at a rate that hardware engineering did not have. They coin the term software engineering to describe a discipline that does not exist yet.
  • 1970. Winston Royce publishes "Managing the Development of Large Software Systems," the paper universally cited as the origin of waterfall.
  • 1975. Fred Brooks publishes The Mythical Man-Month, written out of managing IBM's OS/360, and states the law named after him: adding people to a late software project makes it later. New people must be taught, and the teaching is done by the people already behind.
  • 2001. Seventeen practitioners meet at a ski lodge in Utah and write the Agile Manifesto, which is largely a list of things they had watched go wrong.

Now the part worth knowing about Royce, because it is one of the most consequential misreadings in the history of this field. His paper does contain the famous diagram of phases flowing downward, one into the next. Immediately underneath it he writes that he believes in the concept, but that the implementation just described is risky and invites failure. The rest of the paper is about how to avoid doing it that way. His central recommendation, several figures later, is a heading that reads: attempt to do the job twice.

He never uses the word waterfall. The industry took his first diagram, discarded his warning and his proposed fix, gave the diagram his name, and built four decades of contracting practice on it. Then a later generation rebelled against "Royce's waterfall" without reading Royce either.

Two things to take from that. The smaller one is a fact you can use: the founding document of waterfall says waterfall does not work. The larger one is about how ideas travel. A diagram is portable and a caveat is not, so the diagram survives the journey and the caveat does not. You will watch this happen to a framework in your own career, and it is worth being the person in the room who has read the original.

Requirements: the part everyone rushes

A requirement is a statement of something the system must do or some quality it must have. Functional requirements describe behavior: "the system shall determine subsidy eligibility from household income and size." Non-functional requirements describe qualities: how fast, how many at once, how secure, how available. Non-functional requirements are the ones organizations skip, and they are the ones that produce spectacular public failures, because you cannot see them missing until load arrives.

Healthcare.gov's most consequential missing requirement was a number. The agency issued task orders to build the federal marketplace and the data hub while key technical requirements were still unknown, including which states the system would have to serve and, critically, how many people might enroll. Contracts were signed to build a thing whose size nobody had specified. Every capacity decision downstream (servers, database design, queueing) inherited that blank.

Watch for this pattern

When a deadline is fixed by forces outside the project (a law, an earnings call, a trade show), requirements become the only variable left that leadership can quietly compress. Scope, schedule, and quality form a triangle, usually called the iron triangle: fix two and the third moves on its own. On Healthcare.gov, schedule and scope were both politically untouchable. Quality was the only side left free to give.

How requirements are actually gathered

Requirements do not arrive. Somebody goes and gets them, and the work has a name, requirements elicitation. How they go about it decides what they come back with. The standard methods each have a characteristic blind spot, which is why serious projects use several.

  • Interviews. Ask people what they need. Fast and essential, and limited by the fact that people describe the job they think they do rather than the job they do.
  • Observation. Watch the work happen. Slow, and the only method that reliably finds the workarounds, the second spreadsheet, and the step everybody performs and nobody mentions.
  • Document analysis. Read the forms, reports, and rules the current process produces. Good for regulated work, and it tells you what was true when the document was written.
  • Workshops. Put the disagreeing parties in one room and make them settle it in front of each other. Expensive in calendar time, and the only method that surfaces conflicts between stakeholders rather than collecting them separately.
  • Prototypes. Show people something and watch them react. People are far better at criticizing a thing than describing one, which is the single most useful fact in this section.

The person who does this work has a job title, and it is the one many of you will hold: business analyst. The role sits between the people who need the system and the people who will build it, and its output is the specification that everything downstream depends on. It is the translation job from Chapter 1, performed at the phase where translation is cheapest.

Notice what none of these methods produces on its own. Every one of them collects requirements from somebody who already exists and can be asked. Healthcare.gov's users were, by definition, people who had never bought insurance this way before, and many of whom had never had insurance at all. There was no current process to observe and no experienced user to interview. That is precisely the situation where prototypes and pilots stop being good practice and become the only honest source of information, and it is the situation in which the project ran neither.

Writing a requirement down

Gathering a requirement and recording it are different jobs, and the second one is where projects quietly go wrong. A requirement that lives in somebody's memory of a meeting is not a requirement. The standard way to write one is the user story, and the format is deliberately rigid:

As a [kind of person], I want [to do something], so that [I get some result].

The third clause is the one that matters and the one people drop. "As a shopper, I want to create an account" is not a user story, because it never says what the account is for. Written properly, "so that I can see the prices I qualify for" invites the obvious question: does the shopper need an account before seeing prices, or after? Healthcare.gov answered that late and wrongly, and the format would have forced the argument to happen earlier.

A story on its own is still not enough, because two people can agree with a sentence and mean different things by it. So each story carries acceptance criteria: specific, checkable conditions that decide whether it is finished. Not "the site should be fast," which nobody can pass or fail, but "a page returns in under two seconds with fifty thousand people using the site at once." That is a testable statement, and notice that writing it requires knowing the number of people, which is the requirement CMS never had.

The rule to remember: a requirement nobody can test is not a requirement, it is a hope. Teams also agree a definition of done, a standing checklist that applies to everything, covering things like code review, tests written, and documentation updated, so that "done" means the same thing in March as it does in September.

Deciding what not to build

Every project asks for more than it can have, so somebody has to choose. Doing this by argument in a meeting produces whatever the most senior person wants. Doing it with a method produces something you can defend later.

The common method is MoSCoW, which sorts every requirement into four boxes:

  • Must have. Without it the system has no reason to exist. On Healthcare.gov, a person can compare plans and enroll in one.
  • Should have. Important, and the system is still usable without it for a while.
  • Could have. Desirable, and the first thing dropped when time runs short.
  • Won't have, this time. Explicitly excluded, written down, and agreed. This box is the one teams skip, and it is the most useful of the four, because an excluded requirement stops being an argument.

The related idea is the minimum viable product, the smallest version that delivers real value to a real user, released so you can learn from what happens. It is frequently misused as a polite word for unfinished. The distinction is whether you are shipping less in order to learn something, or shipping less because you ran out of time.

Now reread the recovery in section 04 with this in front of you. The rescue team did not have better technology than the original one. What it had was permission to decide that some things were Could-haves and say so out loud. That is prioritization, performed under emergency conditions, eight weeks after the moment when it would have been cheap.

Prioritizing once is not enough, because requirements keep arriving. What stops scope creep is not willpower but a procedure. Agree a baseline of what is being built. Then require every addition to arrive as a change request that names its cost in time and money, and gets approved or refused by someone accountable. The account-creation mandate that arrived weeks before Healthcare.gov's launch was a substantial change to the system's busiest path. Requirements that arrive that late are not automatically wrong. Requirements that arrive that late without anyone pricing them are.

Testing is not one thing

"We tested it" is not a statement anyone should accept, because testing happens at four different levels and they catch entirely different problems. A project can do three of them thoroughly and fail on the fourth.

The four levels of testing
LevelWhat it checksWhat it cannot catch
UnitOne piece of code does what its author intended, on its ownAnything about how that piece talks to another piece
IntegrationTwo or more parts work correctly together, including systems owned by other organizationsWhether the assembled whole behaves under real conditions
SystemThe whole thing, end to end, including how it behaves under expected loadWhether it fits the way people actually work
User acceptanceReal users, doing real tasks, decide whether it is fit to releaseNothing, but only if the users are real and the tasks are real

Now place the three failures from section 03 on that table. Mars Climate Orbiter passed unit testing twice, once on each team's software, and had no integration testing that would have compared the two across the boundary. London Ambulance had no realistic system testing under load and no user acceptance testing worth the name, because the operators met the system on the morning it went live. CrowdStrike's update passed its testing and was destroyed by none of these four, because the thing that failed was not the software but the decision to give it to everyone at once, and nobody tests a rollout.

Load testing deserves its own sentence, since it is the one that produces public humiliation. Non-functional requirements are only checkable by testing under the volume you expect, and if nobody ever specified the volume, as at Healthcare.gov, there is no load test that can be designed. Missing requirements do not just produce missing features. They produce missing tests.

Integration: the seam nobody owned

Healthcare.gov was never one system. It was a federal marketplace that had to talk in real time to the IRS, the Social Security Administration, the Department of Homeland Security, and dozens of private insurers, to answer one question: who is this person and what are they entitled to? Each connection was a seam. Seams are where systems fail, and someone has to own them.

Nobody did. The Inspector General's review found that CMS awarded 60 contracts across 33 different companies, and never designated any one of them as the project lead. The agency "missed the opportunity," in the IG's language, to name a single point of contact responsible for integrating the contractors' work and communicating a common goal. The gap was not merely unfilled but actively misunderstood: CMS believed the contractor CGI was serving as lead integrator, while CGI did not share that understanding of its role.

This is the systems integrator problem, and it is not unique to government. Any project with multiple vendors needs one accountable party for the whole. Where that role is vacant, each vendor delivers its own piece correctly and the assembled system does not work, and no one has breached a contract.

Rollout strategy: how you turn it on

There are four ways to move users from an old world to a new one, and choosing among them is a risk decision, not a technical one:

  • Direct cutover ("big bang"), everyone moves at once. Cheapest, fastest, and the only option with no fallback.
  • Parallel, old and new run side by side until the new one is trusted. Safest and most expensive; you pay for both.
  • Phased, release functionality in stages.
  • Pilot conversion, one region, office, or user group first, then expand.

Healthcare.gov chose direct cutover: every feature, available to every American, on one date. A pilot was available and obvious. One state, or federal employees, or a limited regional beta months ahead of the deadline. It was not taken. The consequence of big bang is that the first honest test of the system is also the moment of maximum public exposure, and there is nothing to roll back to.

Connect it forward

Two chapters from now, Nike will make the same rollout choice on a $400 million supply chain system and get the same class of result. Watch for it. The pattern, a big-bang cutover of a system whose requirements were never nailed down, is the single most repeated failure mode in this course.

03Three short failures, three phases

Before the case, three short ones. The chapter has just claimed that the cost of fixing a mistake rises by roughly ten times at each phase it survives. That claim is easier to believe when you watch it happen. Each failure below escaped a different phase, and in each one the fix was available one phase earlier and would have cost almost nothing there. Two of the three had working code.

Mini-case 1 · 1999: the spacecraft that used the wrong units

NASA launched the Mars Climate Orbiter in December 1998 on a mission costing around $193 million. Nine months later it reached Mars, fired its engine to enter orbit, and was never heard from again. It had come in far too low and broke apart in the atmosphere.

The investigation found no broken equipment and no failed component. Ground software built by Lockheed Martin reported the force of each small thruster firing in pound-force seconds, the unit used in the United States. The navigation software at NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory read those numbers as newton-seconds, the metric unit. One pound-force is about 4.45 newtons, so every reading was wrong by that factor, consistently, for nine months.

Both programs worked exactly as written. What nobody had specified was the interface between them: which unit crosses the boundary between one team's software and another team's software. That is a requirement, and it is the kind of requirement that feels too obvious to write down.

The phase it escaped: analysis. The component: data. A single line in a specification, agreed in a meeting years earlier, would have cost nothing. Instead the error survived design, coding, testing, launch, and nine months of flight, and was found by the planet.

Check yourselfTwo teams, each competent, each testing its own software successfully. Name the specific document that should have existed between them, and say who in an organization is responsible for noticing that it does not.

Mini-case 2 · 1992: the ambulance service that went live on a Monday

The London Ambulance Service, then the largest in the world, had already spent about £7.5 million on a failed attempt to computerize its dispatch operation. The second attempt went live on Monday, 26 October 1992, across the whole service at once.

It went wrong within hours. The system could not reliably tell duplicate calls about the same incident apart, so multiple ambulances were sent to some emergencies and none to others. It lost track of which vehicles were free, so it had fewer and fewer to allocate as the day went on. Automatic alerts about unacknowledged calls piled up faster than operators could clear them, which slowed the system further. Response times ran to hours. The service reverted to a semi-manual method on 4 November, nine days after launch, and the chief executive resigned.

The inquiry did not find a single defect at fault. It found a system that nobody had tested under realistic load, staff who had not been adequately trained on it, and a decision to switch the entire service over in one step, with no way to return to the old method. Press reports at the time linked deaths to the delays; the official inquiry did not establish a death toll, and you should be careful repeating the numbers that circulate.

The phase it escaped: implementation and deployment. The component: people. The requirements were roughly right. What was missing was testing under real conditions, training, and a rollout that would have let anyone stop it. One borough for a month would have found all of this.

Check yourselfOf the four rollout strategies in section 02, which one did London choose, and which one would you have chosen? Name the specific cost of your choice, because every safer option costs something.

Mini-case 3 · 2024: the update that went everywhere at once

Early on 19 July 2024, airport check-in screens around the world went blue at the same moment, and gate agents reached for pens. CrowdStrike sells security software that runs deep inside Windows, which is what lets it stop attacks and also what makes it dangerous when it is wrong. That morning the company released a routine content update to that software. It went out to customers worldwide at essentially the same moment.

The update contained a fault that caused Windows machines to crash on startup, and then crash again on the next startup, so the machines could not be repaired remotely. Microsoft later estimated that about 8.5 million devices were affected. Airlines grounded flights and reverted to handwritten boarding passes. Hospitals postponed procedures. Broadcasters went off air. Because the machines would not boot, much of the recovery had to be done by a person physically visiting each one.

The software had been through testing. What it had not been through was a rollout. Content updates of this kind went to everyone at once, which meant there was no group of customers who would have hit the fault first and no interval in which anyone could have noticed and stopped it. Among the changes the company committed to afterwards was releasing these updates in stages and letting customers choose when to take them.

The phase it escaped: deployment. The component: software, released without a rollout. Every customer was, in effect, in the first wave. Read that against the four rollout strategies in section 02, and note that the remedy adopted afterwards was one of them, available all along.

Check yourselfSecurity software is argued to be a special case, because a delayed update leaves customers exposed to a threat that is already circulating. Make the strongest version of that argument, then say what it would take to hold both things at once: fast protection and a staged release.
Try it · Which phase did it escape?

Six things that went wrong on real projects, including three from this chapter. For each one, name the phase where the mistake was made, not the phase where it was noticed. Those are almost never the same, and the gap between them is what this chapter is about.

If you find yourself wanting to answer "testing" for everything, that is the instinct to argue with. Testing is where problems surface. It is rarely where they were created.

What the three have in common

Look at what was working in each case. Two spacecraft teams whose software both passed its own tests. An ambulance dispatch system whose requirements were broadly correct. A security update that had been tested and was correct on almost every machine that received it. In none of the three was the failure a matter of somebody writing bad instructions.

What each of them lacked was a check that belonged to an earlier phase and would have been cheap there: an interface specification, a pilot, a staged release. That is the arithmetic from section 01, shown three times. The mistake does not become expensive because it is serious. It becomes expensive because it survives.

Now read the case that follows with one question in front of you. Healthcare.gov is not one of these three failures. It is all of them at once, at national scale, on a date set by law.

04Healthcare.gov: the first day

On the morning of 2 October 2013, in a government crisis meeting, somebody read out how many people had managed to buy insurance through the new federal website on its first day. Six. Across five insurers. The site had cost roughly half a billion dollars and millions of people had tried to use it. Healthcare.gov was the insurance marketplace created by the Affordable Care Act. Residents of the 36 states that had not built their own exchange were supposed to come to this one website, compare plans, learn whether they qualified for a subsidy, and enroll. The political stakes were enormous: Congress had shut down the federal government that same morning in an attempt to defund the law, but the technical job was, on its face, unremarkable. Show people a catalog. Check their eligibility. Take an order.

It failed within two hours. Demand of roughly 250,000 simultaneous users, about five times what had been planned for, brought the site down almost immediately after launch. Most visitors met crashes, timeouts, and errors for the rest of the week. Internal notes from the government's own crisis meetings recorded the tally bluntly: as of the morning of October 2, six enrollments had been completed, spread across five different insurers.

It would be comfortable to file this away as a story about a website that couldn't handle traffic. That reading is wrong, and the rest of this chapter is about why. Capacity was a symptom. The disease was a set of decisions made over three years about how to analyze, specify, sequence, and govern the building of a system: decisions that had already determined the outcome before a single user arrived.

Where it actually broke

Governance is the last piece, and it is where analysis either holds or collapses. A project has scheduled moments where leadership must review evidence and decide whether to proceed, stage gates. Their whole value is that they are allowed to say no.

They said yes. With months left before launch, CMS raised concerns about the lead contractor's performance without taking steps to hold it accountable. The agency moved staff into the contractor's offices in September 2013, delayed the governance reviews that would have surfaced the problems, and proceeded without the required approvals. It decided nonetheless to launch on schedule with the full feature set. The reviews that existed to stop the launch were postponed because the launch was near.

The contracting structure removed the last brake. CMS did not check the federal past-performance database before awarding to CGI Federal, a contractor with prior missteps at the department. For five of the six main contracts, it chose contract types that placed the risk of cost overruns on the government rather than the vendor. The financial results followed: the six major contracts grew from $464 million to $824 million, with one alone rising from $58 million to $207 million.

Notice what is absent from this account. No one wrote catastrophically bad code. The failure lives in requirements never specified, a role never assigned, a rollout never staged, and gates never enforced. Those are all decisions made by managers, not engineers, which is exactly why this material sits in a business school course.

The recovery, and what it proves

The repair is as instructive as the failure, because it shows the problem was tractable all along. Management was centralized under a single accountable leader, Jeffrey Zients, in late October. The White House CTO restructured the technology leadership, replaced underperforming staff and contractors, and recruited senior engineers from industry. A small "tiger team" was given one narrow mandate and the authority to prioritize ruthlessly.

  • Oct 1, 2013Launch. Site down within two hours; six enrollments completed.
  • Early OctSite taken largely offline as unusable. Failure becomes a national political event.
  • Late OctSingle accountable leader named; tech leadership restructured; tiger team formed.
  • Nov–DecRoughly 400 defects resolved over six weeks, concurrency raised to 25,000 users, response time brought to about one second.
  • Dec 1System handling about 35,000 concurrent users.
  • WinterEnrollment climbs from roughly 27,000 in October to about 975,000 in December alone.

Six weeks. The same law, the same insurers, the same essential technical problem. What changed was accountability, prioritization, and permission to ship less. Shipping less is not free: every feature deferred and every shortcut taken to make the site work in December became technical debt, a cost the organization pays later in exchange for moving now. Under those circumstances it was the right trade, and it was still a debt. Every one of those is an analysis-and-governance variable, and every one was available in 2011.

The durable lesson

The tiger team did not do anything that could not have been done during the original build. It made decisions that the original governance structure was not organized to make: name one owner, cut scope to what works, test under real load, ship in stages. Failure here was not a technical limit. It was an organizational choice, and organizational choices can be made differently.

The five components at Healthcare.gov

Every chapter maps its case onto the same five components. The point of the exercise is to locate the break, because a system is only as strong as its weakest component, no matter how strong the others are.

The Five Components · Healthcare.gov
Hardware

Sized for a number nobody had specified; buckled at 250,000 users. A symptom, not the disease.

Software

Sixty contracts of components, each defensible alone, that never worked as one system.

Data

Income, household, identity: held by IRS, SSA, DHS. Accurate and available.

Networks

Real-time links to the IRS, SSA, and DHS. The seams existed; ownership of them didn't.

People

33 contractors, no integrator, gates waved through. Accountability diffused to zero.

Break located at Software and People. The data was good, and the hardware shortfall was downstream of a requirement no person wrote.

Systems analysis in 2026, and what did not change

This case is from 2013, and you are entitled to ask whether any of it still applies. Two answers, and they point in different directions.

The cost of doing it properly has collapsed. Almost every failure in this chapter was expensive to prevent at the time and is nearly free to prevent now.

  • The pilot is now a setting. A feature flag lets a team release something to one percent of users, watch what happens, and expand or switch it off within minutes. CMS was told a pilot would cost months it did not have. Today the pilot costs an afternoon, which removes the last respectable excuse for direct cutover.
  • Releases go out in stages. The tooling to give an update to one percent of customers, watch, and expand exists and is standard. CrowdStrike had it available and did not use it for the class of update that failed, which is why its own remedy afterwards was to start.
  • Capacity is rented by the minute. Load testing against a realistic volume no longer requires buying the hardware to test with, which is Chapter 1's Spotify example and Week 10's subject.
  • Systems watch themselves. Monitoring now routes an alert to a named person who is on duty and treats an unanswered alert as its own failure, rather than sending mail nobody reads.

And projects still fail. Not as often at this scale, but often enough that the industry keeps producing case studies. That is the part worth explaining, and the explanation is in this chapter: none of the tools above touches the things that actually broke here. No pipeline tells you which states the system must serve. No feature flag tells you that nobody owns the integration. No monitoring system tells a government that a legal deadline is not a project plan. The tooling removed the excuses and left the decisions exactly where they were.

AI has sharpened that rather than softened it. When writing code was the slow part, requirements could hide behind the build. Now that a model can produce a working draft in an afternoon, the specification is a larger share of what is left, not a smaller one, and an AI that builds the wrong system builds it faster. Chapter 4 made this point about processes; it is the same point about projects.

What happened next, which is not a happy ending

The failure had institutional consequences, and they are worth following, because most textbooks stop at the recovery.

Britain had created the Government Digital Service in 2011 to build public services in-house rather than through contractors. In 2014, directly in response to Healthcare.gov, the United States created two similar organizations: the United States Digital Service inside the Executive Office of the President, and 18F inside the General Services Administration, named after the agency's address at 1800 F Street. Both hired designers, engineers and product managers as government employees, and both worked on the procurement practices this chapter has been criticizing.

They built things you have probably used. Login.gov, the single sign-on for federal services. cloud.gov. The U.S. Web Design System, which is why federal websites now look and behave alike. 18F supported the IRS in building Direct File, free online tax filing, which ran for two filing seasons before the IRS closed it in November 2025.

In January 2025 an executive order reorganized the United States Digital Service into the United States DOGE Service. On 1 March 2025, weeks before its eleventh anniversary, 18F was eliminated and its staff laid off. By the end of 2025 the DOGE Service itself had stopped operating as a single organization, with its remaining staff placed inside other agencies.

Set that beside the rest of the chapter without deciding how you feel about it yet. The lesson Healthcare.gov taught in 2013 was that capability has to live inside the organization that needs it, because you cannot contract for judgment. The institutional response lasted about eleven years. Whether that is evidence the response was wrong, evidence that this kind of capability is unusually hard to sustain, or evidence of something else entirely is a genuine question, and the answer is not obvious. What is not in question is the finding underneath it, which is older than the agencies and outlived them: somebody has to own the whole, and that somebody has to be allowed to say no.

05Who waited

The engineering story has a human denominator it is easy to lose. The people refreshing Healthcare.gov on October 1 were not users testing a product. They were people without health insurance, many attempting to buy it for the first time in their lives, some with conditions that had made them uninsurable until that morning. Every week the site did not work was a week they stayed uninsured, and the enrollment deadline bearing down on the project was also bearing down on them. When this course says a launch failed, keep asking the second question: failed for whom, and what did the failure cost people who had no seat in any status meeting. The contractors were paid for the overruns. The waiting was done by the public.

It is also worth saying who was inside the recovery: a small team with clear ownership, real usage data, and permission to tell the truth about what was broken. The lesson students most reliably take from this case is that the difference between the three years and the eight weeks was not talent. It was structure, honesty, and the political cover to have both.

Key takeaways
  • Requirements are the cheapest place to be wrong, because analysis is where you choose which of the five components will be the weak one.
  • Every project passes through the same activities. The names vary; the phase a mistake escapes from decides what it costs.
  • A requirement nobody can test is not a requirement, it is a hope. Healthcare.gov never had the number of users it was supposed to handle.
  • Waterfall bets that you can know everything up front. Agile bets that you cannot, and builds so the plan absorbs learning. Royce, who is credited with waterfall, recommended doing the job twice.
  • Modern tooling made every failure in this chapter cheap to prevent. Projects fail anyway, because the tools removed the excuses and left the decisions where they were.

06Vocabulary

User story
A requirement written as: as a kind of person, I want to do something, so that I get some result. The third clause is the one people drop.
Acceptance criteria
Specific, checkable conditions that decide whether a requirement is finished. A requirement nobody can test is not a requirement.
MoSCoW
Sorting requirements into must have, should have, could have, and won't have this time.
Minimum viable product
The smallest version that delivers real value to a real user, released in order to learn from what happens.
Change request
A new or altered requirement arriving after the baseline is agreed, priced in time and money, and approved or refused by someone accountable.
Post-implementation review
Asking, months after launch, whether the system delivered what was promised and what to do differently next time.
Business analyst
The person who gathers requirements and specifies what a system must do, working between the people who need it and the people who will build it.
Requirements elicitation
The work of finding out what a system must do, through interviews, observation, document analysis, workshops, and prototypes. Each method has a different blind spot.
Integration testing
Checking that two or more parts work correctly together, including systems owned by other organizations. The level that would have caught the Mars Climate Orbiter.
User acceptance testing
Real users doing real tasks, deciding whether a system is fit to release.
Iron triangle
Scope, schedule, and quality. Fix any two of them and the third moves on its own.
Systems analysis
Determining what a system must do, and in what order to build it, before construction begins.
SDLC
The phased sequence from planning through maintenance that structures system development.
Functional requirement
A statement of behavior the system must perform.
Non-functional requirement
A required quality: speed, capacity, availability, security.
Waterfall
One pass through the phases with document sign-offs; predictable, and all learning arrives at the end.
Agile / sprint
Short cycles each ending in working software; a product owner re-ranks priorities as learning arrives.
Systems integrator
The party accountable for making independently built components work as one system.
Direct cutover
Moving all users to a new system at once, with no fallback. "Big bang."
Pilot conversion
Deploying to a limited group first to surface defects at low exposure.
Stage gate
A scheduled review with authority to halt a project before the next phase.
Technical debt
Future cost incurred by shipping a shortcut now.
Monday activity · Which check was missing?12 minutes

Three failures, three phases. A spacecraft lost because nobody wrote down which unit crossed the boundary between two teams. An ambulance service that went live everywhere at once without testing under load or training its operators. A trading firm whose correct code was installed on seven servers out of eight, with nobody required to check.

For each one, name the single cheapest check that would have caught it, and say which phase that check belongs to. Then rank the three by how easy the check would have been to skip.

Think 3 min

Write one check per failure. A check is something a named person does at a named moment, not a wish for more care.

Pair 4 min

Compare with a neighbor. Where you disagree, it is usually because you put the check in different phases. Work out which of you is right.

Share 5 min

We collect the checks on the board, sorted by phase, and look at what the column shapes tell us.

Where this landsTwo things usually emerge. First, every check the room names is small: a line in a specification, one borough for a month, a second person reading a checklist. That is the tenfold arithmetic from section 01 seen from the cheap end. Second, the easiest checks to skip are the ones that protect against something that has not happened yet, which is every check on the list. Push on the ranking question, because it is really a question about organizations rather than about projects: a check survives only if somebody's job is to perform it and somebody else notices when they do not. Carry that into Wednesday and ask which checks CMS had, and which of them were postponed because the launch was near.
AI and these questions

Green: after drafting your own answer, ask an AI to argue against it and press on your weakest claim, sparring partner mode, before you present. Red: submitting or reading out an AI-written answer. The Wednesday discussion grades your reasoning, and everyone in the room can tell the difference.

07Discussion questions

None of these get handed in. On Wednesday you run the discussion and I steer it, which means the class is only as good as what you walk in with. Read the chapter, pick the question you have the strongest opinion about, and be ready to say it first.

  1. Walk the SDLC phases and name the specific decision at each phase that pushed Healthcare.gov toward failure. Which single decision, reversed, would have helped most, and which phase was it made in? Apply
  2. The site had four million visitors and six enrollments. Explain to someone with no technical background why "the servers were overloaded" is an incomplete explanation, and name what it is an explanation of. Analyze
  3. Design the pilot that should have happened. Who is in it, when does it run, and what specific number would have told CMS to delay the October 1 launch? Be concrete about the threshold, because a pilot with no threshold is a demonstration. Apply
  4. CMS never designated a lead systems integrator, and believed one of its contractors was doing the job while that contractor did not. Whose job was it to notice the gap, at what point in the life cycle should it have been caught, and what would have made it visible? Systems
  5. The launch date came from a law rather than a project plan. When a deadline is immovable and the system is not ready, what are a manager's real options, what does each one cost, and who pays that cost? Answer for Healthcare.gov specifically before you answer in general. Ethics
