Every December, a music app produces a slideshow about you: your top artist, your minutes, your “listening personality”. Within hours it is everywhere, posted voluntarily by hundreds of millions of people.

Learning objectives

By the end of this chapter you should be able to:

  1. Name the five components of an information system and find each one inside a system you use every day.
  2. Explain why MIS is the business management of technology rather than a technology skill, and how it differs from business analytics.
  3. Apply the weakest component rule: identify which component a failure lives in before assigning blame.
  4. Read a system as a loop of dependencies rather than a pile of parts, which this book calls systems thinking.
  5. Describe what a technology ethnographer does inside an organization, and why that is the job this degree prepares you for.

1Why a music app opens a business course

It is tempting to treat Spotify Wrapped as marketing. The campaign is real, and it works. But it only exists because of what sits underneath it: an information system, and that is the part worth your attention.

For Wrapped to work, people who work at Spotify have to figure out how to:

  • capture a year of listening data, play by play;
  • decide how to store that data so that every listen can be retrieved and sent to each user;
  • reduce hundreds of millions of pieces of data down to a few facts worth telling each user;
  • and deliver all of this data as information to each user on the same morning without the whole system failing.

An information system underlies how all of this works. It is impossible to write 1.4 billion Wrapped stories by hand. No creative team, no agency, no amount of budget can do that. This is a technology story.

Every information system is built from the same five components: hardware, software, data, networks, and people. This chapter uses Spotify Wrapped to illuminate all five. I hope you begin to look at technology this way: as five complex technological components that must all work together to give you information.

2So, what is MIS?

You are reading this because you signed up for a class called Principles of Management Information Systems. But, you may not be entirely sure what MIS actually is. That’s okay. Let’s start by saying what MIS is not.

MIS does not mean Excel. Over the last decade, students often come into MIS 320 thinking that MIS means Excel. Several students say something like “Oh, MIS is an Excel class.” That’s okay. Please note that MIS is much more than Excel.

The Excel misconception, I believe, comes from the fact that, in a business school, MIS is the “technology major.” For years, business schools have decided that Excel is important for all business students to learn (which is true) but the “intro to MIS classes” have been where all business students go to learn Excel. Just know, for now, that MIS is much more than Excel.

Yes, you will do a month of Excel in this course, and you should, because you will likely use it for the rest of your career. So will the accounting, finance, marketing, operations, and even management majors. In today’s business world, working with data is not optional anymore. And Excel is often where that occurs.

So if MIS is not Excel, then what the heck is MIS? Here is the short version, and then we will spend a quarter unpacking it.

Key conceptManagement information system

To think of MIS, think of the three words separately. Management. Information. System. Management means to oversee something. Information is processed, organized, and structured data that provides meaning, context, and value to a recipient (we will discuss that in Ch. 2). And a system is a group of interacting or connected parts that work together to form a complex, unified whole.

So, to manage an information system, means to have technical expertise in digital systems that provide a recipient with structured data that provides meaning, context, and value. The recipient could be anyone. The data could be anything. Consider your text messages. Or TikToks. That is data present in a meaningful form for you (the recipient). Delivering data this way in a business or organizational setting takes a lot of time and knowledge.

This class is about reviewing companies who manage information systems well (and not so well).

So, let’s start by thinking about what an information system is. A good way to think about an information system is five components: hardware, software, data, networks, and people. This combination of these components together is the information system. So you can define an information system as a combination of hardware, software, data, networks, and people that an organization uses to collect data and deliver the data to a recipient in a meaningful way.

It’s important to note that none of the five components is a system by itself, and a failure in any one component is a failure of the whole information system. This course will encourage you to think about technology in terms of all five components working together at once to do something.

Now, on to the actual management part. Somebody has to manage one or more of the five components of the information system. This is a valuable and in-demand skill (yes, even in an AI world, maybe even more so). To manage an information system, someone needs to decide what a system is for, what it is worth spending, who is accountable for it, and whether the organization is going to change how it works to accommodate it. Those are management decisions, made by people who are not writing the code, and they determine the outcome more often than the technology does. That is what MIS is: the business management of technology.

Also, you may find it funny that people is one of the components of an information system. That is not a typo. An information system with no people in it is not an information system, it is equipment. Look at your phone for a minute. It would be a brick without people. Technology is still built for a world of humans (we can chat about the potential of this changing one day). Often the component that breaks the system is people.

Four of the five components are more technology-related. Hardware, software, data, networks: you can buy them, install them, point at them, and put a number on what they cost. The people component is why this class lives in a business school rather than only in a computer science department. You need to know how to manage both the technology and the people together. Collecting data without people that know how to analyze it is pointless. Building software for no one is not a smart business decision.

So what does an MIS graduate often do? The IS Job Index, a survey of information systems programs run out of Temple University, counts an estimated 5.1 million IS jobs in the United States and describes the people in them as experts in developing, applying, modifying, and strategizing technology to digitally transform organizations, working alongside computer scientists and engineers rather than as them. The four roles it names first are data analysts, digital transformation and AI consultants, application developers, and chief information and technology officers.

Closer to home, WWU's graduate outcomes data, which follows WWU graduates through Washington state employment records, says where those roles actually lead. About nine in ten WWU MIS graduates found in those records are working within a year of finishing. Starting pay averages around $56,000, and five years out the median is $95,000. Most go to technology and consulting firms or to the information sector. The five-year number is the one to notice: typically MIS graduates do not just start well, they keep climbing, because the work is about deciding what gets built and measured, and that is worth more the longer you do it.

Overall, think of the computer scientist as asking whether the system works. An MIS major asks whether an information system is worth building, whether the organization around it will actually use it, and whether the decisions it feeds are better than the decisions it replaced.

The simple version of what MIS actually is, and the simple explanation Prof. Califf has been giving students for 10+ years is: MIS is about translating the language of technology from human to “geek” and “geek” back to human again. Most people don’t understand how technology works. An MIS person does. And most people don’t know how to translate how technology works into business value. An MIS person does. This leads to a concept called translation.

Key conceptTranslation

The work of standing between the technical side of a system and the human side of it is called translation. It may sound odd, but know what (and how) technology works and translating that into what it means for a specific organization, and how technology may support needs of a business and the users of the technology is a skill. It requires understanding technology as a whole (a system). Translation is the clearest one-word answer to what an MIS graduate does for a living.

Recently, Sam Illingworth, an AI critic, made an argument about the critical nature and value of translation, particularly in an AI era. He calls translation the key skill of the AI era, and notes that it draws on a variety of technical and human knowledge around topics like data literacy, technology ethics, contextual reasoning, and the judgment to know when to trust an output and when to overrule it.

Ok, now we know a bit more about what MIS is and what MIS majors do, so let’s get more into the five components.

3The five components of MIS

Every information system, whether it is Spotify Wrapped, a hospital records system, or the register at your coffee shop, is built from the same five overarching components. Each component is critical, and each is associated with a specific job. Every case in this digital textbook gets mapped onto them. The table below gives a definition of each component, an everyday example, and an example of a job associated with each.

The Five Components of MIS
ComponentWhat it isAn everyday exampleWho works on it
HardwareThe physical machines: phones, laptops, servers, sensors, registers. The register at a coffee shop, the phone in your pocket, the servers that never sit in the same building as you. IT support, infrastructure and cloud teams.
SoftwareThe instructions the hardware runs, from the operating system on your phone to the programs a company runs on its servers. The app you order from, and the program on the company's side that decides which store gets the order. Developers, but also the analysts who write down what the software must do.
DataThe raw facts captured about the world: plays, sales, clicks, temperatures. Every sale, every click, every scan of a loyalty card, each with a time and a place attached. Data analysts, database administrators, and the manager who decides what gets recorded.
NetworksThe connections underneath everything else, moving data between machines and between organizations. Nothing above reaches anything else without them. The Wi-Fi in the store, the connection to the payment processor, the link between headquarters and every branch. Network and security teams.
PeopleEveryone who builds, operates, interprets, ignores, or works around the system. The barista, the customer, the manager reading the report, and the person who decided what the report should show. Everyone in this table, and this is the component MIS graduates are trained to manage.

It is important to remember that while each component is important, an information system cannot function unless all five components are working. This leads to the idea of the weakest component rule.

Key conceptThe weakest component rule

A system is only as good as its weakest component. Four can be excellent and the fifth can still ruin the whole system, quietly, while the output still looks convincing. When a system fails, check all five before assigning blame. You will do exactly that on a real failure in every chapter of this book.

Another important concept related to information systems is about the dependencies among all five components, and the behavior of the whole system rather than any piece in isolation. This idea to think of the system in terms of an interrelated whole is called systems thinking.

Key conceptSystems thinking

The ability to break a desired outcome into clear, logical steps, and to see how the components that produce it depend on one another, so that you judge the behavior of the whole system rather than any one piece in isolation.

Systems thinking has been called one of the most important skills in an AI era. As work begins to shift more to managerial thinking rather than purely technical, thinking of technology in terms of interrelated components is becoming very important. For example, Netflix Chief Product and Technology Officer Elizabeth Stone told Lenny Rachitsky on July 19, 2026, that systems thinking is the most important skill she now hires for. The concept of a systems architect is becoming more and more important in an AI era, and systems thinking lives at the heart of that.

Let’s further break down an information system and how to manage one using the case of Spotify Wrapped. Spotify Wrapped is a good first information system to take apart because you have likely seen what it produces and likely haven’t thought much about how it was produced, which is true of nearly every system you will ever meet.

Before reading the case of Spotify Wrapped as an information system, try taking the quiz about the five components.

4Spotify Wrapped as an information system

Before we begin, think about these three numbers that set the scale of Spotify Wrapped:

~1.4BWrapped stories written before launch day
350Mlisteners who received up to 5 stories each
165Kstories sampled and graded before any were released to users

Let’s begin. The 2025 edition of Spotify Wrapped, called Wrapped Archive, gave you up to five remarkable days out of your listening year. In essence, Spotify wrote you a short story about each remarkable day: the single day with the highest total stream time, a day featuring an unusually high volume of new artist discovery, a day where one specific music genre dominated the listening queue, a day dedicated heavily to older track releases, and a day with habits that completely deviated from the user’s established taste profile.

In March 2026, Spotify’s engineering team published an account of how they actually built it. Here is a picture of the information system they described. Notice that it is very specific. Technical knowledge is important, but managing all of these technical components together is key.

Now let’s take the 2025 Wrapped apart one component at a time so we can better understand the components of an information system. We’ll start with the data first.

4.1 Data: finding five days in a year of listening

Data, data, data! That’s all we hear about today, right? What does “data” actually mean? Well, it depends on the context. For Spotify, data means a play event (i.e., when you play a song, Spotify collects a lot of data about that song and stores it). Here is an example of data that Spotify collects when you play a song. That table represents data: which are recorded facts, one row at a time, with no opinion attached. Just numbers and letters. No context attached. Data is collected without context. Humans shape what that means. Data with context (i.e., what Wrapped actually shows you) is information, which is essentially raw data that has been processed into something worth telling a user. We’ll explore more about the differences between data and information in Chapter 2.

Let’s revisit the table. There are three plays of the same Moana song in seven minutes on a car speaker on a Monday morning (thanks, five-year-old daughter!), then two Sum 41 tracks on a phone an hour and a half later. Any parent can tell you what happened: a school run, then the commute to work. That is context and it is really up to a human to make that meaningful.

By itself, the data shows one listener, u_4471, and by December it will confidently report that Prof. Califf’s top song of the year was “Beyond” from the Moana 2 soundtrack. That is not wrong. But is this valuable information to give to a user? We can discuss that later.

It is also important to note that getting to a Wrapped story like “your top artist was Sum 41” means matching billions of data events against a separate catalog of song information and translated into something fun and meaningful. That is complex and requires many people and other components of an information system working together. Collecting data is easy, especially in 2026. The problem is that a year of listening for hundreds of millions of people is far too much to hand to anything and say “find what matters.” So somebody had to decide what “remarkable” means, precisely enough that a computer could check it, and that is not a technical question. No amount of engineering skill tells you which day of someone’s year mattered to them.

The data component has now done its job. Out of a year of raw rows, each listener has a handful of candidate days and the numbers that describe them. Nothing has been written yet. Next, let’s discuss software.

4.2 Software: teaching a model to write 1.4 billion stories

The data component is essentially about collecting lots of numbers; Software is what turns that into something a person would actually want to read.

Software is complex here. When most people discuss software and Spotify, they will picture the Spotify app: the green icon, the play button, the thing on your phone. Yes, that is software. But there are different types of software in an information system.

The Spotify app is the user part with a screen on it. Behind that app sits code that records every play, rules that pick the remarkable days, a pipeline that assembles a packet for each one, something that talks to the model, and a system that files the finished stories where they can be found again. None of that has a screen. It’s just digital code. Most software does not have a screen. It runs on a server somewhere, checking rules, making decisions, and moving data between systems, with nobody watching. The app on your phone is the small part of it that you can see.

For Spotify Wrapped, the scale of actually writing stories with data is the first thing worth pausing on: there are around 350 million eligible Spotify listeners, who can get up to five stories each. That comes to roughly 1.4 billion Wrapped Stories. Every one of those stories had to be written before launch day.

To do this, Spotify used a large language model (LLM): the same kind of model that is behind ChatGPT, a program trained on enormous amounts of text that can produce fluent sentences on request. Spotify did not build the LLM from scratch. Nobody does. It started with an existing LLM, then trained a smaller copy of it on its own examples until the writing sounded like Spotify, a process the engineers call fine-tuning. Doing that effectively took two kinds of knowledge at once: an understanding of what people actually want to hear about their own year, and an understanding of how the data and the technology work. Neither one alone would have produced a Wrapped story anybody wanted to share.

So what Spotify did was note the limitations of the technology: LLMs are bad at math. The data analysts calculated a bunch of statistics and asked the LLM to write lovely stories about users. A team of people actually spent several months teaching the LLM what to create based on the pre-calculated data as related to various scenarios for various story types.

Now, remember: Spotify Wrapped produced 1.4 billion stories for 350 million users. This could not happen without the LLM and the people supervising it. The team read enough stories, every day for three months, to know what a good one sounded like, and they wrote that general standard down as rules. A team of people can indeed write the rules for a good Wrapped story. But how can a team of people possibly review 1.4 billion stories?

They cannot, and Spotify says so: human review at that scale is impossible. So, once the story-writing LLM was ready, the team took a sample of about 165,000 finished stories and had larger, more capable LLMs grade them. Those LLMs were used as judges, and essentially graded the stories against the rules the people had written. Stories were graded on four things: accuracy, safety, tone, and formatting.

Overall, the people set the standard for the Spotify stories. A second AI checked whether the first AI met it. That is the arrangement to remember, because it is how most AI is supervised in business now: humans decide what good means, and machines do the checking at a scale humans never could. Humans are in the loop. The nature of the work is indeed changing, but humans are still there.

Next, let’s discuss hardware.

4.3 Hardware: everyone all at once

Spotify has 1.4 billion Wrapped stories, written, approved, and sitting in servers somewhere. Now, every one of those stories has to appear on a different person’s phone, and they all have to appear on the same morning at the same time. That is a very hard thing to ask of a set of machines.

The usual answer to this problem is to get a bunch of servers in a data center and increase computing capacity, meaning more computers doing the work, as demand climbs through the day. That would have failed here, because demand for Wrapped does not climb slowly. It spikes!

The network teams at Spotify agreed to pay for far more computing capacity than they would ever normally need, hours before launch. They told their cloud suppliers (i.e., Google Cloud) what was coming. And they ran fake traffic through every region on purpose, timed to finish just before real listeners showed up. The fake traffic was there to warm everything up. In their words: when real traffic arrived, nothing was cold. In essence, servers were running in a data center just on the business side. When it was time for Wrapped to launch, they were already running and that alleviated potential issues.

It’s important to note the scale of the network component here. Getting 1.4 billion stories worth of data to individual users is not possible on your own servers. This is why most businesses today operate on cloud infrastructure, which is essentially renting computing space by the hour. The computing space exists in thousands of servers that sit in data centers somebody else owns, which is the only reason capacity can be turned up for one morning in December and turned back down afterwards. Nobody buys and installs servers for a single morning. There is of course a cost to cloud infrastructure, which we will discuss in Chapter 10.

Overall, in the network layer, a team of people at Spotify had to approve all of server capacity, had to work with Google Cloud to prepare, weeks or months ahead, in several meetings. You don’t just turn that much computing on with the click of a button. That work is an MIS job long before it is an engineering one.

Now, let’s discuss networks.

4.4 Networks: getting it from there to here

Of the five components, networks is likely the easiest to say in a sentence. Networks essentially represent how data gets from one place to another. Bellive it or not, the world is not wireless. It is wired.

Every song you played this year traveled from your phone to Spotify servers somewhere. When Wrapped launched in December, 1.4 billion finished stories traveled to a few hundred million phones, more or less at once. And in between, the computers doing the work had to talk to each other because they are not one computer and they are not in one building.

You likely haven’t seen a network, but likely have set one up in your house, apartment, etc. Once it is set up, nobody really pays attention to it, unless of course it goes down.

One thing to note about the network layer is that it tends to be the one nobody makes contingency plans for. Deciding how much traffic to expect, and what should happen to the requests that fail anyway, is a decision somebody makes in advance with incomplete information. The same kind of decision as the capacity one above, made by the same kind of person.

Now, let’s discuss likely the most important part of the five components of an information system: the people.

4.5 People: the ones who use it, and the ones who built it

There are two groups of people in this component, and the system does not work without either one.

First, start with the people who use Wrapped. You supplied every row of the data to Spotify, one play at a time, over a year, likely without once thinking of it as data. You are also the distribution: Spotify does advertise Wrapped, but the campaign that matters is unpaid: the one run by users posting their Wrapped to social media, sharing it with their friends, and so on. That means Wrapped has to function in a human world. It is made for people. People want to see it and don’t want to think about it too much. In that sense, you are the thing the system is trying to describe, which is the hard part.

Second, think about the people who built the information system that powers Wrapped. Look back at what the four components above were actually about. Somebody decided what counts as a remarkable day. Somebody weighed a better LLM against what it would cost to run 1.4 billion times. Somebody took months to negotiate how to pay for a morning’s worth of computing weeks before anyone knew it would be needed. Somebody decided the finished stories should be reviewed before they went anywhere. People matter.

Note that none of the above decisions is a coding decision. Every one of them is a management decision: what data to collect, how to collect it, what to build, what to spend, what models to use, how to make the rules, what standard the output has to meet, and who signs off before it reaches 350 million people. Every one of those decisions had to be made in advance, with incomplete information, and every one could have gone the other way. That is the management half of management information systems, and it is the half that decided whether Wrapped worked.

What about AI tho? It’s a good thing to be concerned about. If AI is so good, and powers a lot of what happened in the 2025 Wrapped, does the people component just go away? Spotify has been asking that question about its own work, and publishing the answers, which is more useful than guessing.

On the user side, yes, AI made the product and the user experience better. You can now prompt a playlist in a sentence and have one built for you, which beats any menu of buttons, and it only works because the system already knows a great deal about what you listen to. It’s great for copy and pasting setlists and building a playlist from a live show. The AI DJ is questionable, however.

On the business side, the answer Spotify gives is a bit more careful. Spotify has tested whether a language model can predict which version of a feature would win, instead of trying both on real users. For example, suppose the Wrapped team cannot decide between two versions of the button that invites you to share your results: "Share your Wrapped" or "Show your friends." The normal way to settle it is an A/B test: show version A to a few million users and version B to a few million others, and count who taps. That takes weeks and uses real people as the test. The tempting shortcut is to describe both buttons to a language model, ask it which one people would tap, and get an answer in an hour.

Spotify's researchers have discussed how they tested this shortcut on a real dataset: thousands of old A/B tests of news headlines, where the human results were already known, so the model's guesses could be checked. The result was basically "the LLM is fine on the familiar, but useless on novelty."

That is a bigger problem than it sounds, because businesses run on novelty. Nobody needs a test to confirm that last year's feature still works. The questions worth asking are about the thing nobody has tried yet, and that is exactly where the model has nothing to go on. For Spotify, whose whole business is guessing what you will want to hear next, a tool that only works on the familiar is not a shortcut at all.

One thing to note about AI here. The same company reached two different conclusions about the same technology. A model could write 1.4 billion stories, because that job was handing it facts and asking for good sentences. A model could not replace an A/B test, because that job was asking it what people would do.

AI is good at some things and not at others, and the two look surprisingly alike from the outside. Knowing which is which, the value AI can bring for specific tasks, before the money is spent, is a people job, and technology decisions will always require people.

4.6 What to take away

You have just been through a case on how Spotify Wrapped is an information system. Note that Wrapped is just an example in the context of music streaming on your phone. The same five components sit in many contexts: underneath a hospital deciding which patients get seen first, a factory deciding a machine is about to fail before it fails, a bank deciding whether to approve a loan, a grocery chain deciding what goes on a shelf in Bellingham next Tuesday. They are underneath a music festival moving forty thousand people through a gate, a baseball team deciding who to draft and what to pay them, and a state deciding who qualifies for a benefit. They are underneath your university deciding which classes to offer, which ones you are allowed to register for, and whether you have met the requirements to graduate.

So, here is the takeaway. An information system is not just a single piece of technology. It is five components that all have to work together, in an order, at the same time. The number of ways that can go wrong is exactly why a team of people get paid to build, monitor, and manage systems and components of systems. That is the job this field is named after: not building the technology, but managing it. And that involves deciding what it should do, whether it is worth what it costs, and who takes the blame when it goes wrong.

And please be aware that people sit at the center of an information system three times over: Wrapped was built by people, it was built for people, and it works because people share it with other people. Take the people out at any of those three points and there is nothing left to manage. Only equipment.

The engineering in this chapter is impressive, but none of it is the reason the case is here. The reason is the decisions, and four of them apply to any company you will ever work for.

Decide what data to collect and store before you need it. Wrapped only worked in 2025 because, years earlier, somebody at Spotify decided to save every song play with the time it happened. Nobody was planning to write stories with that data back then. They just kept it. A lot of a company's most valuable data is like that: collected for one reason and useful later for another. Part of a technology manager's job is making sure it gets kept.

Start with what the business needs, not with what the technology can do. Wrapped exists because Spotify wanted people to feel something about their year and post it, which keeps them subscribed and brings in new listeners for free. Every technical decision in this chapter, from what data to keep to how many stories to write, was made to serve that one business goal. The companies that get this backwards, building impressive technology and then looking for a reason, are the ones you will read about in the failure chapters.

The decisions that mattered were not made by programmers. Someone decided what a good story sounds like. Someone decided the model should not do math. Someone decided a sample of 165,000 was enough to trust the rest. Someone decided this was worth three months of a team's time. Those are management decisions about technology, made by people who understood both the business and the system well enough to say what should be built. That is the work MIS graduates do, and it is why this course lives in a business school.

Key takeaways
  • An information system is hardware, software, data, networks, and people working together. Remove any one and the rest is equipment.
  • Most of what decides whether a system works is managerial: what somebody chose to build, what they were willing to spend, and who was made responsible for checking it.
  • A system is only as good as its weakest component, and the weakest one is rarely the one you can buy.
  • Spotify Wrapped looks like marketing. Underneath it is a year of captured data, a model that had to be judged before it was trusted, hardware warmed up in advance, and people at both ends.
  • Your job in this field is to watch how people and technology actually work together, and to decide what fits.

4.7 What a business student may take from Spotify Wrapped

The engineering in this chapter is impressive, but none of it is the reason the case is here. The reason is the decisions, and four of them apply to any company you will ever work for.

Decide what data to collect and store before you need it. Wrapped only worked in 2025 because, years earlier, somebody at Spotify decided to save every song play with the time it happened. Nobody was planning to write stories with that data back then. They just kept it. A lot of a company’s most valuable data is like that: collected for one reason and useful later for another. Part of a technology manager’s job is making sure it gets kept.

AI is not good at everything, so give it only the jobs it is good at. Spotify did not hand the whole task to the AI model. The LLM got the already computed data statistics handed to it and was asked to do the one thing it does well: write. When Spotify’s researchers tried a model on a job it is not good at, predicting what customers would do, it fell short, and they said so. As a business student, this is a key thing to know about AI: it is a very good tool for some jobs and a bad one for others, and the two can look the same from the outside. Good design is knowing which is which and arranging the rest of the system around it.

Start with what the business needs, not with what the technology can do. Wrapped exists because Spotify wanted people to feel something about their year and post it, which keeps them subscribed and brings in new listeners for free. Every technical decision in this chapter, from what data to keep to how many stories to write, was made to serve that one business goal. The companies that get this backwards, building impressive technology and then looking for a reason, are the ones you will read about in the failure chapters.

The decisions that mattered were not made by programmers. Someone decided what a good story sounds like. Someone decided the model should not do math. Someone decided a sample of 165,000 was enough to trust the rest. Someone decided this was worth three months of a team’s time. Those are management decisions about technology, made by people who understood both the business and the system well enough to say what should be built. That is the work MIS graduates do, and it is why this course lives in a business school.

5How this course works

5.1 The road through the quarter

Thirteen chapters, one company at the center of each, in an order that is deliberate and not the one a conventional textbook would use. The first three chapters are about why information matters, the middle seven are about how systems get built and used, and the last three are about who decides. Data gets its own chapter in Week 7 rather than Week 2, on purpose: by then you will have spent four labs cleaning and joining real data, so the theory arrives after the experience. The cloud comes last because it is where the five components come back together. The chapter-by-chapter map, with each company and each chapter’s claim, is in About this book.

5.2 Lecture: Mondays and Wednesdays

Mondays introduce the concepts. Wednesdays put them to work on a real company at a specific moment, usually one where something went right or went badly wrong. Some weeks the class works through the company’s numbers or maps its process together, and some weeks Prof. Califf covers new material the case brings up, but the center of every Wednesday is the same routine, and here is how it goes.

  1. Before class. Read the chapter and think about the five discussion questions at the end of it. They are never submitted and never graded, but the session is only as good as what walks in the door.
  2. Think. Prof. Califf puts the discussion questions on the board. Take a few minutes alone and write down your own answers before anyone else can influence them.
  3. Pair. Work the questions in a small group. Argue, and settle on positions the group is willing to defend out loud.
  4. Share. Groups take the questions in front of the whole class, and Prof. Califf presses on the claims. Expect this to get more formal as the quarter goes on, from talking from your seat in the early weeks to short prepared presentations later.
  5. Bridge. Every Wednesday ends by connecting the case to that week’s lab, so you know why you are about to spend eighty minutes in Excel or SQL.

This first week is the exception to the whole rhythm: the quarter starts on a Wednesday, so today is both sessions at once.

5.3 Lab: Thursdays and Fridays

Labs are hands-on and eighty minutes long: Excel, Tableau, SQL, security, UX design, and information architecture. Most of them serve one fictional client, Cascadia Outfitters, a member-owned outdoor gear co-op with eight stores in the Pacific Northwest, whose data you will come to know better than they do. The labs run as a single engagement rather than as disconnected exercises, so what you find in one lab tends to matter in the next.

Every chapter ends with discussion questions and a bridge to that week’s lab, so you always know why you are learning what you are learning. Exams fall in Weeks 5, 9, and 11.

About AI in this course

You are allowed to use generative AI throughout, deliberately, and under a policy you learn in Lab 1 this week. The short version: AI as tutor is always fine; AI as ghostwriter needs disclosure; AI as substitute for your own thinking, or during exams, is never fine.

6Vocabulary

Information system
Hardware, software, data, networks and people, working together to collect information and act on it.
MIS
The business discipline studying how organizations use information systems to create value.
Data vs. information
Data is captured facts; information is data processed into something that changes a decision.
Five components of MIS
Hardware, software, data, networks, and people, this book’s analytical thread.
Efficiency
Doing the thing with less.
Effectiveness
Doing the right thing at all; the harder test.
Systems thinking
Seeing the whole system rather than its parts in isolation.
Technology ethnographer
What an MIS graduate does: observe how people in an organization actually use its technology, then decide where technology fits and what would improve the business.
Weakest component rule
System value is capped by the weakest of the five components, not the strongest.
Translation
Standing between the technical side of a system and the human side: saying what an output means for a particular decision, and knowing when not to trust it.

Where this usually landsOn question one, somebody in your group almost certainly has a sibling, a roommate, or a partner in their listening history, and most groups blame the data. 7Discussion questions

None of these get handed in. On Wednesday you run the discussion and Prof. Califf steers it, which means the class is only as good as what you walk in with. Read the chapter, pick the question you have the strongest opinion about, and be ready to say it first.

  1. Compare your Wrapped with three or four people around you. Find one that is wrong about the person it belongs to, or at least a bit off, and work out which of the five components explains it. Somebody in the group will have a sibling, a roommate, or a partner in their listening. Apply
  2. Invent a Wrapped for something that does not have one. Your gym, your degree, Western, the grocery store, your group chat. What data does that place already collect without anyone thinking about it, and what would it be able to tell you in December that you do not currently know?
  3. Now find the line. What is a Wrapped that nobody would want, that would take exactly the same five components to build? Where is the difference between a delightful one and a creepy one, and is it about the data, the people, or something else entirely? Ethics
  4. Pick a system you used today that has nothing to do with music: registration, a self-checkout, your bank app, a food delivery order, the parking meters on campus. Name all five components in it, in your own words. Then, if one of them quietly failed tonight, which would you notice first, and which one could be broken for months without you ever finding out?
