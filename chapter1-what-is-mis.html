Every December, a music app produces a slideshow about you: your top artist, your minutes, your “listening personality”. Within hours it is everywhere, posted voluntarily by hundreds of millions of people.

It is tempting to file this under marketing. Do not. The outward appearance is a brilliant viral campaign; the engine underneath is an information system, and that is the part worth your attention. Somebody had to capture a year of listening play by play, store it so that any one listener could be found again, reduce hundreds of millions of listening years down to the few facts worth telling each person, and land all of it on the same morning without the whole thing falling over.

None of that is a campaign. That is a system, and it is one nobody outside the company ever sees. Take it away and the best idea in the room is an empty template with nothing to put in it.

Which cuts the other way too. Nobody writes 1.4 billion stories. No creative team, no agency, no amount of budget. Personal at that scale stopped being a copywriting problem some time ago, and the campaigns people envy now are all built on the far side of a system somebody had to design first.

That is the situation almost every good business idea runs into now, including the ones that begin with “we will use AI.” A model can write a paragraph about your year in music, but only if something else already knows what your year in music was. Somebody has to have the data, and somebody has to have built the machine that turns it into something worth showing you.

And somebody has to be able to hold the whole of it in their head at once: the data, the software, the network, the cost, and the people at both ends. Well enough to know where it would break, and to notice when one piece has gone quietly wrong while everything still looks fine. That person is usually not the one who wrote the code.

This course is about information systems: what they are made of, how the parts depend on each other, and what it takes to manage all of it at once. Every company already has people who can use the technology. Far fewer can see the whole system at once, and those are the ones who get asked what the company should do next.

01So, what is MIS?

You signed up for a class called Principles of Information Systems, and you may not be entirely sure what you signed up for. Fair enough. Let us start by clearing away the most common wrong answer.

MIS does not mean Excel. Most students walk in thinking it does, and the mistake is an understandable one. MIS is the “technology major” in a business school, so for years the MIS classes have been where everyone goes to learn Excel. Being the place a skill gets taught is not the same as being the skill.

You will do a month of Excel in this course, and you should, because you will use it for the rest of your career. So will the accountants, the finance majors, the marketers, and everyone going into operations. Working with data is the floor in business now, not a specialization, roughly where writing a professional email sits.

Which raises a fair question, since this college also offers business analytics: what is the difference between MIS and Business Analytics? Roughly this. Analytics asks what the data says. MIS asks where the data came from, whether the system that produced it can be trusted, whether the organization will actually act on the answer, and what happens when one piece of it breaks. The two overlap constantly and you will use both. But the analyst is handed a dataset. You are going to spend this quarter asking who built the system that handed the data over, what it was built to do, and where it can break.

So if the spreadsheet is not the subject, what is? Here is the short version, and then we will spend a quarter unpacking it.

Key conceptManagement information system

A management information system is the combination of hardware, software, data, networks, and people that an organization uses to collect information and act on it. The definition’s point is the word combination: none of the five is a system by itself, and a failure in any one is a failure of the whole. This course is training in seeing all five at once, in systems that mostly show you only their surface.

Managing one of these systems means understanding all five of its parts: the hardware it runs on, the software that tells the hardware what to do, the data it captures, the networks that move that data around, and the people who use it.

People are not the last item on that list by accident, and they are not there as a courtesy. A system with no people in it is not a system, it is equipment. People have to want the answer, people have to act on it, and people have to notice when it is wrong. Every failure you will read about this quarter had working equipment in it somewhere.

Four of those five are technology. Hardware, software, data, networks: you can buy them, install them, point at them, and put a number on what they cost. The fifth is not technology, and the fifth is why this class lives in a business school rather than only in a computer science department. Technology nobody adopts goes nowhere. A system nobody trusts gets worked around. A report nobody reads changes no decision at all.

So the computer scientist asks whether the system works. You are going to ask whether it is worth building, whether the organization around it will actually use it, and whether the decisions it feeds are better than the decisions it replaced. Most of the famous technology failures you will read about were systems that worked perfectly, inside organizations that did not.

This is true of AI too, and it is worth saying plainly in a year when everyone is being told otherwise. A model is software. It sits inside the other four components like any other piece of software: it needs hardware to run on, data to work from, a network to reach anyone, and people to decide what question it is answering and whether the answer is any good. Adding AI to a system does not remove the people from it. It changes what they are responsible for.

There is a name for what they become responsible for, and it is the clearest short answer to what an MIS graduate does for a living.

Key conceptTranslation

The work of standing between the technical side of a system and the human side of it: taking what the technology produced and saying what it means for this organization, this decision, and these stakes. It requires understanding the output well enough to know when it is wrong, and being willing to say so. Translation is the clearest one-word answer to what an MIS graduate does for a living.

The blunter version, and the one Prof. Califf has been giving students for years: MIS is about translating the language of technology from human to “geek” and “geek” back to human again. He took it from a boss twenty years ago, while working as a project manager at a web design company, sitting between clients who knew what they wanted and developers who knew what was possible and translating in both directions all day. That is not a metaphor for the job. For a great many MIS graduates, that is the job.

These days a good deal of the translating runs from human to machine and back instead. The work did not shrink when the machines got better at talking. It got larger, because more output arrives faster and fewer people are equipped to check it.

The educator Sam Illingworth makes the same argument about the workforce generally, calling translation the key skill of the AI era and noting that it draws on data literacy, ethics, contextual reasoning, and the judgment to know when to trust an output and when to overrule it. Read his list again and notice how little of it is technical. That is the job.

02The five components of MIS

Key conceptThe weakest component rule

A system is only as good as its weakest component. Four components can perform perfectly and the fifth can still sink the whole thing, quietly, while the output still looks convincing. The rule’s practical form is a habit of investigation: when a system fails, resist the first explanation and walk all five components before assigning blame. You will run that walk on a real failure in every chapter of this book.

Every information system, Spotify Wrapped, a hospital records system, the register at your coffee shop, is built from the same five components. Every case in this book gets mapped onto them. Learn to see the five and you can decompose any system you ever meet, including the ones you will be hired to fix.

The Five Components of MIS
Hardware

The physical machines: phones, laptops, servers, sensors, registers.

Software

The instructions the hardware runs, from operating systems to Wrapped's pipeline.

Data

The raw facts captured about the world: plays, sales, clicks, temperatures.

Networks

The connections that move data between machines, and between organizations.

People

Everyone who builds, operates, interprets, ignores, or games the system. The four above are bought or built. This one decides whether any of it matters, and without it there is no system, only equipment.

One rule governs the whole course: a system is only as strong as its weakest component, no matter how strong the others are. That includes this one.

The rest of this chapter is one worked example. Spotify Wrapped is a good first system to take apart because you have seen what it produces and never once seen the thing that produces it, which is true of nearly every system you will ever meet. You are about to locate all five components inside it and watch what each one had to do for the thing to arrive on your phone in December.

Next week you meet a company running the same five on coffee. By Week 4 you meet companies where a component is broken, and you practice locating which one.

03Spotify Wrapped as an information system

From the outside

So let us take one apart, starting with the card at the top of this chapter. Strip the confetti off and ask what has to be true for Spotify Wrapped to exist at all. Every play, skip, and replay you produced all year was captured, timestamped, attached to your account, stored. That raw data was processed: aggregated, ranked, compared against everyone else’s. The results were packaged into a form a human wants to look at. And the package was delivered at the exact moment of the year you’d most want to share it, turning you, unpaid, into Spotify’s distribution channel during the season people choose next year’s subscriptions.

Capture, process, package, deliver, and a business result at the end. That pipeline, not the app, not the servers, is the information system. The technology is just what the pipeline is currently made of.

Here is a small thing that gives the game away. The cards you swipe through look like little videos, and they are not. Rendering a few hundred million personalized videos and pushing them down to phones would be impossible on any budget, so what actually arrives is a set of instructions for drawing the animation, which your phone assembles on the spot. What you experience as a finished piece of media was never a piece of media. It was a decision somebody made about what could be sent.

Inside the 2025 build

Everything above is the shape of Wrapped from outside. In March 2026, Spotify's engineering team published an account of how they actually built the 2025 edition, and it is worth walking through carefully, because it turns the five components from a definition you memorize into five sets of decisions you can see people making.

The 2025 feature was called Wrapped Archive. Rather than just handing you your top artist, it picked up to five remarkable days out of your listening year and wrote you a short story about each one. The day you discovered the artist that changed everything. The day your taste took a hard left turn. Here is what had to be true for that to reach you.

Data: finding five days in a year of listening

Before anything else, it is worth being concrete about what “data” means here, because the word gets used loosely enough to mean nothing. A play event is a row. Here are five of them, roughly as the system records them. Length is recorded in milliseconds, which looks like overkill until you realize the system has to tell listening apart from skipping, and that is a question of seconds.

That is data: recorded facts, one row at a time, with no opinion attached. Notice how little any single row tells you. The third row is the only one with anything in it: 21,880 milliseconds is twenty-two seconds, so that play was a skip. Even that is an inference, not a fact the system recorded.

Now read the block. Three plays of the same Moana song in seven minutes on a car speaker on a Monday morning, then two Sum 41 tracks on a phone an hour and a half later. Any parent can tell you what happened there: a school run, then the drive to work. The system cannot. The system has one listener, u_4471, and by December it will confidently report that his top song of the year was “Beyond”. It is not wrong about the data. It is wrong about the person, and it has no way to know the difference.

One more thing the table hides. Look at the artist column. That name is not in the play event. The system recorded that account u_4471 played a track with a particular ID, and everything else you would want to know about that track, the artist, the album, the genre, the release year, lives somewhere else entirely. Getting from a year of play events to “your top artist was Sum 41” means matching hundreds of billions of events against a separate catalogue of song information, for every listener at once. That matching problem is most of the work, it is called a join, and you will spend Week 7 doing it by hand on a much smaller database.

Hold on to that column marked started at (UTC), too. Every one of those timestamps is recorded in one shared world clock, and every one has to be converted into the listener’s own local day before anybody can say which day was “biggest”. Small step. Easy to get wrong. Nobody would ever see it if you did.

So that is the raw material: rows like those, hundreds of billions of them a day, for every listener. The problem was never collecting it. The problem is that a year of listening for hundreds of millions of people is far too much to hand to anything and say "find what matters."

So somebody had to decide what "remarkable" means, precisely enough that a computer could check it. Notice that this is not a technical question. No amount of engineering skill tells you which day of someone's year mattered to them. That is a judgment about people, and it has to be made before a single line of code is useful.

The rules they landed on are called heuristics. Biggest listening day is just the highest total minutes. Biggest discovery day is the day you heard the most first-time artists. Most unusual day is the day you strayed furthest from your normal taste. Then they ranked the candidates and kept the best few per person.

Look at what happened there. A fuzzy human idea, what was memorable about your year, got turned into arithmetic a machine can run. That is translation, the thing named a few pages back, doing its actual day job. It is most of what data work is, and you will do a smaller version of it in every lab this quarter: someone hands you a question in English and you have to decide what it means in columns and formulas before you can answer it.

At the end of all that, the data component has done its job. Out of a year of raw rows, each listener now has a handful of candidate days and the numbers that describe them. Nothing has been written yet. Nobody has seen anything. There is just a shortlist.

Software: teaching a model to write 1.4 billion stories

So the data component hands over a shortlist of days and a pile of numbers, and software is what turns that into something a person would actually want to read. But before we get to how, a word about what software even means here.

Say “software” and most people picture the Spotify app: the green icon, the play button, the thing on your phone. That is software, and it is the smallest part of the software in this story. The app is the bit with a screen on it. Behind it sits code that records every play, rules that pick the remarkable days, a pipeline that assembles a packet for each one, something that talks to the model, and a system that files the finished stories where they can be found again. None of that has a screen. Neither does most software in most companies.

The scale of the writing job is the first thing worth sitting with: around 350 million eligible listeners, up to five stories each, comes to roughly 1.4 billion reports. Every one had to be written before launch day.

Notice what the software was not asked to do. It was not asked to work out which days mattered; that was settled upstream. It was handed the answer and asked to say it well. When they sent each day to the model, they also sent a pre-computed block of statistics, because, in their words, language models are bad at math. The system was built around a known weakness of one of its own components, which is most of what good design is.

Getting the model to write well took more than three months of daily work. That is worth sitting with, because from the outside it looks like somebody asked a machine for a story and a story came out.

Then somebody had to do arithmetic. The best model available wrote lovely stories and would have cost an unthinkable amount to run 1.4 billion times, so they used it to teach a cheaper one to write the same way. Quality against cost, worked out by people, on a spreadsheet somewhere. That is not a technical decision. That is the job.

A system with no people in it is not a system. It is equipment.

Hardware: everyone at once

So: 1.4 billion stories, written and sitting there. Now every one of them has to appear on a different person’s phone, and they all have to appear on the same morning.

That is a brutal thing to ask of a set of machines. The usual answer is to let a system add capacity, meaning more computers doing the work, as demand climbs through the day. That would have failed here, because Wrapped does not climb. It spikes.

So the team switched on far more computing than they would ever normally need, hours before launch, told their suppliers what was coming, and then ran fake traffic through every region on purpose, timed to finish just before real listeners showed up. The fake traffic was there to warm everything up. In their words: when real traffic arrived, nothing was cold.

None of which is possible if you own your machines. Most of the computers in this story do not belong to Spotify. The work runs on cloud infrastructure, rented by the hour in data centers somebody else owns, which is the only reason capacity can be turned up for one morning in December and turned back down afterwards. Nobody buys and racks servers for a single morning. What that arrangement costs, and what a company gives up in exchange, is Week 10.

Somebody had to approve all of that, weeks ahead, in a meeting. Renting a large amount of computing for one morning is a real number on a real budget, requested by someone who had to explain why the cheaper option was worse and what it would cost to be wrong. That request is an MIS job long before it is an engineering one.

Remember that sentence. Later in the quarter you will read about a launch where nobody did any of this.

Networks: getting it from there to here

Of the five, this one is the easiest to say in a sentence. Networks are how data gets from one place to another.

Every song you played this year traveled from your phone to Spotify. In December, 1.4 billion finished stories traveled back the other way, to a few hundred million phones, more or less at once. And in between, the computers doing the work had to reach each other, because they are not one computer and they are not in one building.

You notice a network exactly once, and that is when it stops. The rest of the time it is the component everybody forgets, which is also why it tends to be the one nobody planned for. Deciding how much traffic to expect, and what should happen to the requests that fail anyway, is a call somebody makes in advance with incomplete information. Same kind of call as the capacity one above, made by the same kind of person.

People: the ones who use it, and the ones who built it

There are two groups in this component and the system does not work without either.

Start with you. You supplied every row of the data, one play at a time, over a year, without once thinking of it as data. You are also the distribution: Spotify does not have to buy advertising for Wrapped, because you post it. And you are the thing the system is trying to describe, which is the hard part. Look again at the card at the top of this chapter. The system did not have a person. It had an account, and it reported with total confidence on a year that was partly somebody else’s.

Then the people who built it. Look back at what the last four sections were actually about. Somebody decided what counts as a remarkable day. Somebody weighed a better model against what it would cost to run 1.4 billion times. Somebody approved paying for a morning’s worth of computing weeks before anyone knew it would be needed. Somebody decided the finished stories should be checked before they went anywhere, and had a sample of about 165,000 of them graded on accuracy, safety, tone and formatting.

Not one of those is a coding decision. Every one of them had to be made by a person, in advance, with incomplete information, and every one of them could have gone the other way.

Which brings up the thing everyone is actually worried about. If AI is this good, does the people component just go away? Spotify has been asking that question about its own work, and publishing the answers, which is more useful than guessing.

On the user side, AI made the product better. You can now describe a playlist in a sentence and have one built for you, which beats any menu of buttons, and it only works because the system already knows a great deal about what you listen to.

On the building side they ran a harder test. Before shipping anything, Spotify compares two versions of it on real users and sees which does better. The tempting shortcut is to skip the users and ask a model to predict the winner: faster, cheaper, no waiting. So they tried exactly that against thousands of real tests. The model recovered about 39 percent of the actual effect, and it was not randomly off. It was wrong in a consistent direction, making every change look less effective than it really was. A company running on those numbers would quietly underrate its own best work for years.

Their sharpest finding is the one worth keeping. The shortcut is least trustworthy exactly where it would be most valuable, because the further an idea is from anything you have tried before, the less reason there is to trust a model’s guess about it. What the model is good for is screening out weak ideas before anyone spends a real test on them. Useful. Not a replacement.

That is the pattern to watch for the rest of the quarter. AI did not remove the people from this system. It moved them: less time producing, more time deciding what good looks like and checking whether the machine got there. That is the component the other four run on.

What to take away

Go back to the top of this chapter. Your Wrapped shows up in December, you swipe through it, you post one of the cards, and it looks like a marketing campaign. In one sense it is. That is what most people see, and most people never see anything else.

You have just been through what is behind it. A year of plays recorded one row at a time. A decision about what “remarkable” even means, made by people before a line of code was any use. A model handed the answer and asked to say it nicely. Machines switched on hours early and warmed with fake traffic. A network carrying all of it out on a single morning. And a group of people who assumed the thing would break somewhere and went looking until they found where.

And Wrapped is just the example that happened to be on your phone. The same five components sit underneath a hospital deciding which patients get seen first, a factory deciding a machine is about to fail before it fails, a bank deciding whether to approve a loan, a grocery chain deciding what goes on a shelf in Bellingham next Tuesday. They are underneath a music festival moving forty thousand people through a gate, a baseball team deciding who to draft and what to pay them, a state deciding who qualifies for a benefit, and a university deciding which classes to offer, which ones you are allowed to register for, and whether you have met the requirements to graduate.

That last one is worth a second. Every interaction you have had with this institution has been an information system talking to you. When registration opens and the class you need is full, that is not bad luck. That is a system, built by people, working exactly as somebody designed it to.

Different industry, different stakes, same five parts, and always the same question about which one is the weakest. Wherever you end up working, it will be running on one of these.

So, the takeaway. An information system is not a piece of technology. It is five components that all have to work, in an order, at the same time, and the number of ways that can go wrong is exactly why somebody gets paid to manage one. From the outside it looks like a single clean thing. It never is.

And people sit at the center of it three times over. Wrapped was built by people, who had to decide what counts as remarkable, what to spend, and where to go looking for trouble. It was built for people, one story per listener, which is the only reason it is worth building at all. And it works because people share it with other people. Take the people out at any of those three points and there is nothing left to manage. Only equipment.

Take the campaign away and Spotify still has the system, and next year they can point it at a different question. Take the system away and there is no campaign at all, just a good idea and an empty template. Every December, several hundred million people look straight at the output of an information system and see a piece of marketing.

Which is where translation comes back. Every decision in this chapter sat between two languages. Somebody had to hear “make people feel something about their year” and turn it into a rule a computer could check. Somebody had to take “the better model costs more” and turn it into a number a finance team would sign off on. Somebody had to read a study full of statistics and then walk into a room and say, in plain English, that the fast shortcut would make good ideas look bad.

None of those people wrote the code. All of them were necessary, and no amount of technology could have told them what to do.

Most of you are not MIS majors, and that is fine, because this has stopped being a major-specific skill. Whatever you end up doing, a growing share of your work will arrive as output from a system somebody else built, with AI somewhere inside it, and it will look confident whether or not it is right. Somebody in the room has to be able to ask where it came from and whether to believe it. The more of the work the machines do, the more that question is worth, and the fewer people there are who know how to ask it.

You do not get to look at it the easy way anymore. That is the whole point of this course, and it starts now.

04How this course works

Mondays introduce concepts. Wednesdays apply them to a real company at a specific moment, with student groups presenting the discussion questions from that week's chapter. This first week is the exception: the quarter starts on a Wednesday, so today is both at once. Labs (Thursday/Friday) are hands-on: Excel, Tableau, SQL, and more, mostly in service of one fictional client, Cascadia Outfitters, a member-owned gear co-op whose data you will come to know better than they do. Exams fall in Weeks 5, 9, and 11. Every chapter ends with discussion questions and a bridge to that week’s lab, so you always know why you’re learning what you’re learning.

About AI in this course

You will use generative AI throughout, deliberately, and under a policy you learn in Lab 1 this week. The short version: AI as tutor is always fine; AI as ghostwriter needs disclosure; AI as substitute for your own thinking, or during exams, is never fine.

05Vocabulary

Information system
Hardware, software, data, networks and people, working together to collect information and act on it.
MIS
The business discipline studying how organizations use information systems to create value.
Data vs. information
Data is captured facts; information is data processed into something that changes a decision.
Five components of MIS
Hardware, software, data, networks, and people, this book’s analytical thread.
Efficiency
Doing the thing with less.
Effectiveness
Doing the right thing at all; the harder test.
Systems thinking
Seeing the whole system rather than its parts in isolation.
Weakest component rule
System value is capped by the weakest of the five components, not the strongest.
Translation
Standing between the technical side of a system and the human side: saying what an output means for a particular decision, and knowing when not to trust it.
Join
Matching records in one place against related records held somewhere else, so a play event can become the name of an artist.
Capacity
How much computing a system has available. Raised ahead of a known spike rather than in response to one.
Cloud infrastructure
Computing rented by the hour in data centers somebody else owns, which is what makes capacity adjustable at all.
Heuristic
A rule that turns a fuzzy human idea into something a computer can check.

Green: after drafting your own answer, ask an AI to argue against it and press on your weakest claim, sparring partner mode, before you present. Red: submitting or reading out an AI-written answer. These discussions grade your reasoning, and everyone in the room can tell the difference.

06Discussion questions

  1. Compare your Wrapped with three or four people around you. Find one that is wrong about the person it belongs to, or at least a bit off, and work out which of the five components explains it. Somebody in the group will have a sibling, a roommate, or a partner in their listening. Warm-up
  2. Invent a Wrapped for something that does not have one. Your gym, your degree, Western, the grocery store, your group chat. What data does that place already collect without anyone thinking about it, and what would it be able to tell you in December that you do not currently know?
  3. Now find the line. What is a Wrapped that nobody would want, that would take exactly the same five components to build? Where is the difference between a delightful one and a creepy one, and is it about the data, the people, or something else entirely? Open judgment
  4. Pick a system you used today that has nothing to do with music: registration, a self-checkout, your bank app, a food delivery order, the parking meters on campus. Name all five components in it, in your own words. Then, if one of them quietly failed tonight, which would you notice first, and which one could be broken for months without you ever finding out?
