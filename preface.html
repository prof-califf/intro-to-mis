Front matter

About this book

A free, open textbook for MIS 320, Principles of Management Information Systems, at Western Washington University. Thirteen chapters, ten labs, one fictional company, and no cost to the student.

Who it is for

Business students taking their first course in information systems, most of whom will never write production code and all of whom will spend their careers deciding what gets built, bought, recorded, and measured. It assumes no technical background. It does assume you are willing to be treated as a capable adult who can argue with a case.

Many readers are not native English speakers. The book is written in plain language on purpose: short sentences, no idioms, every technical term defined in the sentence where it first appears, and a glossary for the ones you half-remember from four weeks ago.

What it argues

Management information systems is the business management of technology, not a technology skill. Every chapter is built on the same five components, hardware, software, data, networks, and people, and on one rule: a system is only as good as its weakest component, and the weakest one is rarely the one you can buy. Every chapter has a company at its center and three shorter cases around it, chosen so that one is historical, one is from the period when the relevant technology changed, and one is from the last five years. The historical case proves the pattern is structural. The current one proves the course is about the world you are walking into.

The road through the quarter

Thirteen chapters, one company at the center of each, in an order that is deliberate. Each part depends on the one before it, and the order is not the one a conventional textbook would use, so here is the map and the reasoning.

Part 1, why information matters (Weeks 1 to 3). This chapter hands you the five components. Chapter 2 asks when information is worth collecting at all, with Starbucks, and answers: when it changes a decision. Chapter 3 asks whether technology can give a company a lasting advantage, with Zara, and answers: not the technology, the arrangement.

Part 2, building systems (Weeks 3 to 7). Chapter 4 is about business processes, because a system can only manage what a process records; Domino’s rebuilt its ordering process before it rebuilt anything else. Chapter 5 is how systems get built and why they fail, with Healthcare.gov. Chapter 6 is data, with Walmart, and Chapter 7 is the enterprise systems that force a whole company to record things the same way, with Nike. (Yes, data waits until Week 7. That is on purpose: by then you will have spent four labs cleaning, joining and pivoting real data, so the chapter explains problems you have already met rather than problems you have only been told about.)

Part 3, using data (Weeks 6 and 10). Chapter 8 is business intelligence, with the Oakland A’s: what to measure, and what goes wrong when the measure becomes the target. Chapter 9 is machine learning, with TikTok and Amazon: what happens when you hand the measuring to a machine that works out its own rules.

Part 4, what can go wrong (Week 8). Chapter 10 is security, with Change Healthcare, and argues a breach is an availability problem before it is anything else. Chapter 11 is governance, with Equifax: who is accountable when the controls exist on paper and not on the day.

Part 5, the decisions you will actually make (Weeks 9 and 10). Chapter 12 is software, with Robles v. Domino’s: how it is designed, how it is chosen, and why the interface decides who gets to be a customer. Chapter 13 is the cloud, with Netflix, and it comes last because it is where the five components come back together: what to rent, what to build, and who pays the bill.

If you take one thing from the map: the first three chapters are about why, the middle seven are about how, and the last three are about who decides. The five components run through all of it.

Optional: the same road, by component

A second way to read the sequence, useful once you are a few weeks in. Every chapter names all five components inside its case; this is the one each chapter is mostly about. Notice that data is the spine of the middle of the book, and that people carry as many chapters as any technology does.

How to use it

Each chapter follows the same shape. Learning objectives, then the concepts, then a short interactive quiz on the chapter's core distinction, then the three short cases, then the main case, then a section on who pays. After that: key takeaways, vocabulary, five discussion questions, a bridge to that week's lab, sources, and teaching notes with two session plans.

  • Read the concepts before Monday and the case before Wednesday. The session plans in the teaching notes tell you exactly what each class will do with them.
  • Do the quiz and the check-yourself boxes as you read. None of them is graded. All of them are the kind of question the exams ask.
  • Treat the discussion questions as arguments, not lookups. None can be answered from the text alone. That is deliberate.
  • The labs are where the ideas become skills. Every lab uses the same fictional company, Cascadia Outfitters, and the same database, so what you clean in Excel in Week 3 is what you query in SQL in Week 7.

The AI policy is in Lab 1 and repeated in every chapter: use it to multiply your work, not to outsource your judgment. The book was written with AI assistance under the same rule, and every checkable claim in it was verified against a source you could cite. Where a figure could not be confirmed, the chapter's sources section says so.

Who wrote it

Christopher B. Califf is a professor of Management Information Systems at Western Washington University. His research on technostress in healthcare, published in MIS Quarterly, appears in Chapters 7 and 12. Jennifer Long designed and tested the lab sequence. The five-component framework the book is built on is David Kroenke's; the case-anchored structure owes a debt to John Gallaugher's Information Systems: A Manager's Guide to Harnessing Technology, which the author taught from for years before writing this one.
