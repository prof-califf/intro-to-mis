Chapter 4 · Business Processes

Business Processes: Why Domino's Best Software Could Not Fix Its Pizza

In 2008 Domino's shipped a piece of software that business schools still teach and every industry has since copied. Over the following year its sales fell, its stores closed, and its stock collapsed. This chapter is about the thing underneath the software that nobody had looked at.

In 2008, Domino's launched a piece of software that is now taught in business schools worldwide and copied by every industry from banking to healthcare. Over the following year, the company's sales fell, its stores closed, and its stock collapsed. The Pizza Tracker did not save Domino's. Understanding why is the entire point of this chapter.

01What a business process is

Chapter 1 defined an information system as five components working together: hardware, software, data, networks and people. That definition answers what a system is made of. It does not answer what a system is for, and that turns out to be the harder question, because a system is never built for its own sake. It is built so that some work gets done.

The work is the process. Every organization you will ever join runs on them, most of them undocumented, and the reason this chapter comes before the ones on databases and analytics is that a process is what those systems exist to serve.

Key conceptBusiness process

A business process is a repeatable sequence of steps that turns inputs into something a customer values, crossing departments as it goes. Processes are where strategy becomes physical: Zara’s two-week loop from last chapter is a process, and so is getting one pizza from order to door. The discipline of this chapter is learning to see the process as an object you can map, measure, and redesign, rather than as “just how we do things.”

A business process is a structured set of activities that takes inputs and produces an output a customer values. Order-to-delivery. Hire-to-retire. Quote-to-cash. The definition sounds bland until you notice what it rules out: a process is defined by its output to a customer, not by the department that performs it.

That distinction is the source of most organizational dysfunction. Companies are organized vertically into functions (marketing, operations, finance, IT) but value flows horizontally across them. Every point where work crosses a functional boundary is a handoff, and handoffs are where information is lost, time is spent waiting, and accountability evaporates. Ask who owns "the ordering process" at most firms and you get either a blank look or four different names.

Functions versus processes

Functions are how a company is staffed. Processes are how a company delivers. A customer never experiences your org chart; they experience the seam between your website and your kitchen. Information systems live on exactly those seams, which is why process thinking is the foundation for everything else in this course.

Where a process lives in the system

It is worth being precise about how a process and an information system relate, because students often treat them as the same thing and they are not. A process is the work. An information system is the arrangement of five components that carries the work and makes it visible. Run the components over any process and each one has a specific job:

  • Data is the process's memory. Every step that matters leaves a record, and the steps that leave no record are the ones nobody can manage.
  • Software encodes the rules: what may happen next, what is not allowed, what triggers automatically.
  • Networks carry the work between the people and places that perform it. Every handoff on a map is a network crossing, whether that network is fiber or a person walking a docket to the next desk.
  • Hardware is where the process meets the physical world: the register, the oven, the scanner, the phone.
  • People perform the steps a machine cannot, decide the cases the rules do not cover, and are the only component that can notice the process itself is wrong.

Two consequences follow, and they run through the rest of this chapter. The first is that a system can only manage what the process records. Capturing a timestamped event at each step, so the process becomes measurable at all, is called process instrumentation. If nobody captures the moment a pizza leaves the oven, no software can tell you how long it sat. Deciding what a process records is therefore a design decision, made by people, usually years before anyone needs the answer.

The second is the one this chapter's case is built on. Because the five components are the machinery and the process is the work, you can replace every component and still have the same bad process. New hardware, new software, faster networks, better data, and a broken sequence of steps produces a broken sequence of steps performed faster and recorded more precisely. That sentence is the entire Domino's case in advance.

02Mapping, measuring, redesigning

You cannot improve a process you have not written down, and the written-down version is almost never what managers believe is happening. Process mapping produces two artifacts:

  • The as-is map, what actually happens today, including the workarounds, the re-keying, the phone call someone makes because the system doesn't support a case.
  • The to-be map, what should happen after redesign.

Skipping the as-is is the most common failure in process work. Teams jump to the to-be because it is more fun, and they design an improvement to a process that does not exist. The as-is is where you find the bottleneck, the single constraint that sets the pace of the whole process. Improving anything other than the bottleneck produces no improvement in output, only local statistics that look better.

Drawing the map: swimlanes

Process maps have a standard grammar, and learning it takes five minutes: a box is an activity, a diamond is a decision with labeled exits (yes/no, approved/rejected), an arrow is the flow, and rounded ends mark start and finish. The move that makes a flowchart genuinely useful is the swimlane: one horizontal lane per actor (customer, web system, store, kitchen, driver) with each activity drawn in the lane of whoever performs it. Now every handoff is visible as an arrow crossing a lane boundary, and the diagram answers the question managers argue about most: who owns this step? Count the lane-crossings on any process map and you have counted its most likely failure points. You will draw Domino's order-to-delivery in swimlanes for Wednesday: expect five lanes and be suspicious of any map with fewer than six crossings.

Measuring a process

Four measures cover most of what you need:

  • Cycle time, elapsed time from start to finish, including waiting.
  • Throughput, completed units per period.
  • Quality / defect rate, share of outputs that fail to meet the standard.
  • Cost per transaction, resources consumed per unit of output.

Domino's spent three decades optimizing exactly one of these. The famous thirty-minute delivery guarantee made delivery time the one number the entire company managed to, and the company got extremely good at it. Too good, in a sense. By its own later account, Domino's had been leading the industry in delivery for the wrong reasons. It met its speed promise with frozen and canned ingredients that left the crust tasting like cardboard, and at times at the cost of driver safety.

The measurement trap

Optimizing a process against a single metric will, given enough time, degrade every quality that metric does not capture. Domino's measured minutes and got minutes. Quality was not being managed badly. It was not being managed at all, because nothing in the process was watching it. You will see this again in Week 6 with Moneyball, where an entire industry measured the wrong thing for a century.

03Three short cases, one old finding

Before the case, three short ones. Process work has a long history and a stubbornly consistent finding, which is that the fix almost never turns out to be the equipment. Each case below fixed a process that was genuinely broken, and in each one the fix lived somewhere different: in the design, in the handoffs, or in who was allowed to stop the work. Notice what none of them is. None is a story about buying better technology, and two of the three happened before the computer arrived in business at all.

Mini-case 1 · 1948: the kitchen drawn on a tennis court

Richard and Maurice McDonald ran a successful drive-in restaurant in San Bernardino, California. Carhops took orders at the car, food came out on real plates with real cutlery, and the menu ran to dozens of items. It made money. In 1948 they closed it for three months anyway.

What they did during those three months is the reason this case opens the chapter. They went to the tennis court behind the house at night and drew the kitchen on it in chalk, full size, every piece of equipment measured out where they thought it should stand. Then they walked their staff through it, and had them mime making a hamburger, over and over, while the brothers watched where people collided, waited, or crossed each other's path. Then they rubbed out the chalk and drew it again. One night it rained before they had copied the layout onto paper and washed the whole thing away, so they drew it again.

Only after the process worked on the tennis court did they build the kitchen. The result was the Speedee Service System: no carhops and no plates, a menu cut to a handful of high-volume items, and a kitchen laid out as an assembly line where each person did one step. Hamburgers at fifteen cents.

It did not go well at first. Sales fell after the reopening, the carhops they had let go came back to laugh at them, and regular customers asked when they were going back to the old way. Then families started arriving, and the thing they had designed on a tennis court became the way most of the world eats lunch.

Where the fix lived: in the design. The brothers built a to-be map, tested it with people walking through it, and revised it several times before spending a dollar on equipment. Almost no company does this. The usual sequence is to buy the equipment and then discover the process. Notice also what they were optimizing: not the product, which barely changed, but cycle time, the minutes between an order and a hamburger. And notice which component they changed. The hardware was a kitchen either way. What they redesigned was the sequence and the people's place in it.

Check yourselfThe chalk cost nothing and the rehearsal cost three months of revenue. Name one thing the rehearsal could reveal that a drawing on paper could not, and say why that thing is usually discovered only after the equipment is installed.

Mini-case 2 · 1988: five hundred clerks and a computer that would not have helped

Ford's accounts payable operation in North America employed more than five hundred people. Their job was matching: a purchase order from purchasing, a receiving document from the loading dock, and an invoice from the supplier. When all three agreed, the supplier got paid. When they disagreed, which was often, a clerk investigated until they agreed. Under the old procedure the accounting department had to match fourteen separate data items before it could release a payment.

Ford did what most companies do. It planned to install a new computer system, tighten the procedures, and cut the department by about twenty percent, from five hundred people to four hundred. That is a respectable target and the project would have been counted a success.

Then somebody looked at Mazda, in which Ford held a stake. Mazda's entire accounts payable organization was five people. Even adjusting for the fact that Mazda was a much smaller company, Ford concluded its own department was roughly five times the size it should be. The twenty percent target had not come from any analysis of what the work required. It had come from the shape of the process Ford already had.

So Ford redesigned the shape. Under what it called invoiceless processing, purchasing enters an order into a shared database and sends a copy to nobody. When the goods reach the receiving dock, the receiving clerk checks the database, and if the delivery matches an outstanding order, accepts it and records it. The system pays from that. There is no invoice to match, because there is no invoice. Headcount fell by about seventy-five percent.

This is the case that produced the most quoted line in process work. Michael Hammer, writing it up in Harvard Business Review in 1990, argued that companies were using computers to speed up work that should not have been happening at all, and told them: don't automate, obliterate.

Where the fix lived: in the handoffs. This is business process redesign in its purest form. Nothing was wrong with any individual clerk or any individual step. What was wrong was that three departments each produced a document about the same delivery, and reconciling their disagreements had become a job for five hundred people. Draw it in swimlanes and the problem is visible immediately: three lanes, three documents, and a bottleneck where they meet. This is also the cleanest example in the chapter of reengineering against improvement. The twenty percent plan was improvement. Deleting the invoice was reengineering.

Check yourselfFord's original plan, a new system and a twenty percent cut, would have been reported internally as a win. Explain precisely why that is the dangerous outcome, and say what question the team asked that turned a twenty percent target into a seventy-five percent one.

Mini-case 3 · Toyota: the cord that stops the line

On a Toyota assembly line, any worker who sees something wrong can pull a cord and stop the line. Not report it to a supervisor, not write it down at the end of the shift. Stop the line, on their own authority, while everybody waits.

To most manufacturers for most of the twentieth century this was close to unthinkable. The line is the most expensive thing a factory owns and stopping it is the most costly thing that can happen, so the authority to stop it belongs high up, if it belongs to anyone. The reasoning at Toyota ran the other way. A defect that passes down the line gets built into something bigger, then something bigger than that, and by the time anyone notices, the cost of fixing it has multiplied and the evidence of what caused it is gone. Stopping now is cheap. Stopping later is expensive. Not stopping is the most expensive of all.

The person who can see the defect first is the person standing in front of it, so that is where the authority goes.

Where the fix lived: in who was allowed to stop the work. No new equipment, no new software, and nothing that would appear on a process map as a step. What changed was a permission. In the five-component terms of section 01, this is a people change with no hardware, software, data or network change at all, and it is the kind that never shows up in a technology budget. Permissions are invisible on an org chart and invisible on a flowchart, which is why they are the last place anybody looks.

Check yourselfDomino's spent 2008 and 2009 watching sales fall while shipping a product its own customers described as cardboard. Who inside that company had both the information to know and the authority to stop the line? If your answer is "nobody," say what that tells you about the process rather than about the people.

What the three have in common

Put them side by side and the pattern is clear. A kitchen layout, a set of matching rules, and a piece of rope. Not one of the three fixes was a technology purchase, and the two that could have been solved by buying something, Ford's and Domino's, are the two where buying something was exactly the plan that would have failed.

The other thing they share is harder to see and more useful. In every case the improvement was invisible until somebody described the process as a process, out loud, in a form other people could argue with: chalk on a court, a comparison with Mazda, a rule about who may pull a cord. That description is what the next two sections teach you to produce, and it is what the case at the end of this chapter went five years without.

04Domino's: the tracker on a broken pizza

The Pizza Tracker showed customers a live bar: your order has been placed, it's being prepared, it's in the oven, it's out for delivery. It was genuinely novel, it delighted people, and it worked exactly as designed. It also arrived at a company whose product customers actively disliked.

The verdicts were not subtle. Focus groups described the crust as cardboard, the sauce as ketchup, and the cheese as processed-feeling. In industry taste tests, Domino's pizza tied for last place with Chuck E. Cheese. A video of employees contaminating food went viral. The market rendered its own verdict: a stock that had traded above $30 in 2007 was under $7 by late 2008, while Papa John's ran "Better Ingredients. Better Pizza." directly at the quality gap. The operating numbers matched, U.S. same-store sales fell 4.9% system-wide in 2008, roughly seven times worse than the industry average, and the company closed 120 domestic units between 2008 and 2009 while the pizza category itself was growing.

A pizza tracker is a system for watching a process. It cannot improve the process it is watching.

Domino's had built excellent information technology on top of a broken business process, and the technology faithfully reported the brokenness in real time. This is the most important lesson in the chapter and one of the most important in the course: technology applied to a bad process produces a faster, better-documented bad process.

Redesigning the product, in public

Key conceptImprovement vs. reengineering

There are two ways to change a process. Incremental improvement keeps the process shape and tunes the steps: faster ovens, better scripts, one fewer handoff. Business process reengineering (BPR) throws the shape away and asks what the process would look like if you designed it today, from zero, around the outcome. Michael Hammer’s 1990 formulation was blunt: don’t automate, obliterate. Domino’s did both, in order: the 2009 recipe-and-honesty campaign reengineered how the product and its process faced the public, and the years of tracker-driven tuning since have been incremental improvement on the new shape. Most companies only ever do the second, because the first requires admitting the current shape is wrong.

The turnaround inverted the usual order of operations. Most companies fix quietly and announce selectively. Domino's announced the failure first.

  • 2008Pizza Tracker launches. Sales continue to fall.
  • 2009Taste tests, viral video, store closures, stock in single digits.
  • Dec 2009The "Pizza Turnaround" campaign airs: executives and chefs read real customer criticism aloud on camera, including the cardboard-crust comparison, in a documentary-style spot.
  • Dec 2009The reformulated recipe (reworked crust, rebuilt sauce, upgraded cheese) launches simultaneously with the ad, so the admission and the fix arrive together rather than in sequence.
  • 2010Patrick Doyle becomes CEO; new recipe rolls out to all U.S. stores.
  • 2011Mobile apps relaunched for iPhone and Android.
  • 2013Pizza Profiles: saved preferences and payment, collapsing reorder to a few taps.

Two aspects of the sequencing matter analytically. First, the product was fixed before the digital ordering push accelerated. The 2011–2013 technology investments landed on a process that now produced something worth ordering. Second, the public admission was itself a process change: it created a feedback loop. Customer criticism, previously an externality the company absorbed, became an input the company was contractually committed in public to respond to.

Operational transparency

Key conceptOperational transparency

Showing customers the work being done on their behalf, rather than hiding it behind a spinner or a counter. Research by Ryan Buell and colleagues found that people value a service more when they can see the effort inside it. The Pizza Tracker is the canonical business example, and it carries a system truth: to show a customer each stage, the company must first timestamp each stage, and once every step is timestamped, every step can be measured and fixed.

Return to the tracker, now that the process underneath it works. What the Pizza Tracker actually does is make an internal process visible to the customer: what researchers call operational transparency. It changes nothing about how fast the pizza is made. It changes what waiting feels like, by converting an opaque interval into an observable sequence of stages.

It also does something quieter and more valuable to the firm. To display a stage, the system must capture that stage. A tracker forces every step of the fulfillment process to emit a timestamped event, which means the company now has data on where time actually goes, per store, per shift, per driver. The customer-facing feature is a by-product of instrumenting the process. That instrumentation is what let Domino's later reason about its operations at all.

Why this generalizes

Package tracking, ride-share maps, bank transfer status bars, and the "your claim is being reviewed" screen are all the same design: expose the stages, capture the events. The business case is always double: it reassures the customer, and it forces the company to record each step as it happens. When you see a status tracker, ask what data it must be collecting to render itself.

Domino's posted a 14.3% same-store sales increase in the first quarter of 2010, and the stock rose 130% between December 2009 and December 2010. By September 2010 the company ranked as the fourth-largest e-retailer in the United States, behind only Amazon, Staples, and Office Depot.

Read that last statistic carefully, because it is the chapter's thesis in one line. A pizza chain became a top-five e-commerce company, not by building better e-commerce software than Amazon, but by fixing the product its e-commerce software was selling. The digital ordering capability had existed, largely unchanged, throughout the collapse.

What they built after the process worked

The turnaround gets the headlines, but the decade after it is the part with the transferable lesson. Once the product was worth ordering, Domino's spent ten years making ordering the thing it was best at, and the results show what happens when technology is applied to a process that is finally sound.

AnyWare: ordering as an interface problem

Beginning around 2015, the company built what it calls AnyWare, a set of ordering paths across platforms that had never been ordering platforms. A Ford dashboard. A Samsung smart TV. An Apple Watch. A pizza emoji sent as a tweet or a text. Amazon's voice assistant. Facebook Messenger and Slack. The stated goal was to let people order using whatever they already had in their hand, and the company now supports well over a dozen such paths.

Look past the novelty at the architectural claim underneath, which has a name: this is omnichannel, many customer-facing surfaces reaching one underlying process and one record of the order. Every one of those surfaces reaches the same ordering system, which reaches the same store, which triggers the same tracked fulfillment process from the transparency subsection above. Adding a new ordering surface is a small piece of work rather than a new company, because the process beneath is one process. That property has a name you will meet in Week 10, and it is what people mean when they say a system is well architected.

The data underneath

Every one of those orders emits data, and the volume is the point. Domino's has described integrating data from thousands of stores along with supply chain, staffing, and third-party sources, running through thousands of separate data pipelines into a central warehouse, to assemble one view of the customer and the operation. A loyalty program launched in 2015 attaches a large share of those orders to identified individuals rather than anonymous transactions.

Recognize that last mechanism, because you met it in Chapter 2 with Starbucks and you are about to spend a month inside it. An order attached to a known customer can be analyzed, predicted, and served. An anonymous order is a single event that teaches the company nothing. Your lab client is a co-op whose entire business model depends on making that attachment happen, and one of whose stores has quietly stopped.

Pizza theater: the tracker, rebuilt in a room

In 2012 Domino's introduced a store format it calls pizza theater: an open view of food preparation from the lobby, seating, and a screen showing the status of carryout orders. Read that as a design decision rather than a decor decision. It is the Pizza Tracker implemented in architecture. The same principle from the transparency subsection, expose the stages so waiting becomes legible, applied to a physical room instead of a screen.

By the late 2010s the company was reporting that a majority of United States sales arrived through digital channels, alongside a long unbroken run of same-store sales growth. Patrick Doyle's line, that Domino's is a technology company that sells pizza, stopped being a provocation and started being a description of the org chart.

The sequence is the lesson

Everything in this section was possible in 2008. The company had online ordering, a tracker, and capable engineers, and sales fell anyway, because the pizza was bad. The technology only compounded once the underlying process produced something worth ordering. Get the order of operations wrong and you build a very efficient way to sell a product people do not want.

From automating steps to software that runs the process

AnyWare multiplied the ways an order could enter the process. The decade's other change is who, or what, executes the process once the order is in, and it is worth naming the ladder, because your career will be spent somewhere on it.

The first rung is workflow automation: software routes the work, humans do the steps. The tracker era Domino's built is exactly this; the system sequences and timestamps, people make the pizza. The second rung is robotic process automation (RPA): software performs the clerical steps itself, clicking through screens the way a person would, ideal for high-volume, rule-following work like re-keying orders between systems. Domino's own early move up this ladder was Dom, a voice-ordering assistant launched back in 2014, which took the order-entry step away from human typing entirely.

The rung businesses are climbing now is the AI agent: software that is given a goal rather than a script, plans the steps itself, executes across multiple systems, and handles the exceptions in between. An agent handling a refund does not follow a flowchart someone drew; it reads the complaint, checks the order history, applies the policy, issues the credit, and writes the customer, deciding the sequence as it goes. Week 10 covers how such systems work. What belongs in this chapter is what they require: an agent can only be trusted with a process that is mapped, measured, and instrumented, which is to say a process that has been through everything this chapter teaches. Nobody should hand a goal to an agent inside a process they cannot draw.

Hammer's warning, at machine speed

The oldest finding in process work is that automating a bad process gives you the same bad process, faster. Hammer's 1990 line, don't automate, obliterate, was aimed at companies that pave the cowpath. A cowpath is the winding track cattle wear into a field, which everyone then follows only because it is already there. Paving it means pouring technology onto a process shape that should have been thrown away. AI agents make this temptation stronger than it has ever been, because automation is now cheap enough to skip the redesign entirely. An agent will execute a broken process with perfect diligence at machine speed, and the bullwhip chapter in Week 7 shows what automated diligence does to a bad number. The order of operations this chapter argues for does not change with the technology: map it, fix it, then automate it. Domino's fixed the pizza before it built the empire of ordering surfaces, and the sequence is the lesson.

What Domino's is doing now, and why it is the same argument

The turnaround is a decade old, so it is fair to ask whether the lesson survived. Two developments since are worth following, and both are process decisions dressed as technology decisions.

The aggregators. For years Domino's refused to appear on DoorDash or Uber Eats, and was almost alone among large chains in that. The stated reason was that delivery was the process it had spent forty years building, and handing it to a third party meant handing over the last mile, the timing, and the data. Then it joined Uber Eats in 2023 and DoorDash in 2024, with DoorDash fully rolled out across the system by the middle of 2025.

Look closely at what they agreed to, because it is unusual. Domino's takes orders from the aggregators and fulfills them with its own drivers. The platform supplies demand; Domino's keeps the process. In the terms of section 01, they bought a new front door and refused to give up the machinery behind it. By the end of 2025 aggregator orders were around five percent of United States sales, which management describes as customers it would not otherwise have reached.

The agent. On its second-quarter 2026 earnings call, the company described an orchestration agent that optimizes delivery routing and timing. Put that beside the ladder earlier in this section. It is not deciding what a customer wants or changing the recipe. It is sequencing deliveries inside a process whose steps are already defined, already instrumented, and already understood, which is exactly the rung the chapter argued was safe.

The results are ordinary in an instructive way. United States same-store sales grew about three percent in fiscal 2025, an eleventh consecutive year of market share gain, with the company at roughly 23 percent of the United States pizza market. In the second quarter of 2026, order counts rose while the average ticket fell, because a premium product line underperformed. That is a product miss inside a process that is working, and it is worth naming the difference for students: 2008 was a product failure that no process could rescue, and 2026 is a product miss that the process absorbs.

One more thing, and it is the part most likely to matter in ten years. More than sixty percent of the company's revenue comes from selling supplies to its own franchisees rather than from pizza. The dough and the trucks are a process nobody outside can duplicate, and no delivery platform can buy its way into it. That is the Chapter 3 argument arriving in a Chapter 4 case: the parts are for sale and the arrangement is not.

05Transparency for whom

Before mapping the components, turn the tracker around. Operational transparency runs one direction: the customer sees the store’s work, and the store, in turn, is seen. Every timestamp that delights a customer is also a performance record of the worker who produced it. A system that measures every stage of every order is a management instrument pointed at the kitchen, and the pressure of being continuously measured is part of what “the process improved” means from the inside. Chapter 2 raised the same point about algorithmic scheduling, and it lands here in a second form.

There is also a customer the system did not see. Domino’s built ordering into phones, watches, TVs, and cars, an ordering surface for practically everyone, and in 2016 a blind customer named Guillermo Robles could not order a pizza from the website or the app with his screen reader. The lawsuit that followed reached the Ninth Circuit and, in 2019, the Supreme Court declined to disturb the ruling against Domino’s. You will study the case properly in Week 9’s software design session; for now, hold the shape of it: the company that made ordering effortless from a Samsung TV had not made it possible from a screen reader. Reach and access are not the same achievement.

Deliberately exposing internal process stages to the customer.
Process instrumentation
Capturing timestamped events at each step so the process becomes measurable.
Feedback loop
A path by which process output re-enters as input, enabling correction.
Omnichannel
Many customer-facing surfaces reaching one underlying process and one record of the order.
Data pipeline
An automated route that moves data from where it is produced to where it is analyzed.
Business process redesign
Rethinking a process end to end rather than optimizing steps in place.
Monday activity · Where does the fix live?12 minutes

Three processes, three fixes. McDonald's redesigned the kitchen before building it. Ford deleted the invoice instead of hiring a computer to match it faster. Toyota gave the person standing in front of the defect the authority to stop the line. Not one of the three was a technology purchase.

Take a process you have worked inside, or been a customer of, that is visibly broken. Which of the three fixes does it need, and what is stopping that fix from happening?

Think 3 min

Name the process in one line, then pick one of the three. Be specific about the fix; "better communication" is not a fix, it is a complaint.

Pair 4 min

Swap. Your partner's job is to argue that a different one of the three is the real fix for your process.

Share 5 min

We collect a few on the board and sort them by which of the three they need.

Where this landsMost rooms pile up on the handoffs, because handoffs are the easiest of the three to see once you have a swimlane. The authority answer is the one worth digging for, since it never appears on a process map: somebody could see the problem and could not stop the work, or could stop the work and never saw the problem. Push on the second half of the question too. The reason the fix has not happened is usually that the process crosses departments and no single person owns it, which is the definition from section 01 arriving as a practical obstacle. Carry the best example into Wednesday and ask who at Domino's could have pulled the cord in 2008.
AI and these questions

Green: after drafting your own answer, ask an AI to argue against it and press on your weakest claim, sparring partner mode, before you present. Red: submitting or reading out an AI-written answer. The Wednesday discussion grades your reasoning, and everyone in the room can tell the difference.

0:00OpenPut one question to the room and hold the answers: name a company whose app or website is better than the thing it actually sells. Take four or five, write them on the board, do not comment. You are coming back to the list at the end.
0:05LectureWhat a business process is. Push on the definition: a process is defined by its output to a customer, not by the department that performs it. Get them to say out loud who owns "the ordering process" at a company they have worked for, and let the silence do the teaching.
0:12LectureWhere a process lives in the five components. Take one process the room knows and assign each component a job out loud: what does it record, what rules does the software hold, where are the network crossings. Land the two consequences, that a system can only manage what the process records, and that you can replace every component and still have the same bad process. That second sentence is Wednesday's case stated in advance, so say it slowly.
0:20LectureFunctions versus processes. Draw a plain org chart on the board, then draw one customer's experience as a horizontal line straight across it. Every crossing is a handoff. This is the single image the rest of the session hangs on.
0:26LectureMapping: as-is before to-be, and why teams skip the as-is. Then swimlane grammar, which takes five minutes: box, diamond, arrow, one lane per actor. Draw a three-lane example live and make them count the crossings.
