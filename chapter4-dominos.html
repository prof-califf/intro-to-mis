Chapter 4 · Business Processes

Business Processes: Why Domino's Best Software Could Not Fix Its Pizza

In 2008 Domino's shipped a piece of software that business schools still teach and every industry has since copied. Over the following year its sales fell, its stores closed, and its stock collapsed. This chapter is about the thing underneath the software that nobody had looked at.

In 2008, Domino's launched a piece of software that is now taught in business schools worldwide and copied by every industry from banking to healthcare. Over the following year, the company's sales fell, its stores closed, and its stock collapsed. The Pizza Tracker did not save Domino's. Understanding why is the entire point of this chapter.

Learning objectives

By the end of this chapter you should be able to:

  1. Define a business process by its output to a customer, and explain why that definition cuts across departments.
  2. Read and draw a swimlane diagram, and locate the handoffs and bottlenecks on it.
  3. Distinguish process improvement from reengineering, and say when each is the right choice.
  4. Explain the rule that an information system can only manage what the process records, and apply it to a failure.
  5. Decide which steps of a process can be handed to software or an agent, and which must stay with a person.

1What a business process is

Chapter 1 defined an information system as five components working together: hardware, software, data, networks and people. That definition answers what an information system is made of. It does not answer what an information system is for, and that turns out to be the harder question, because a system is never built for its own sake. It is built so that some work gets done.

The work is the process. Every organization you will ever join runs on processes, most of them undocumented, and a process is what every database, dashboard, and app in this book exists to serve.

1.1 What a business process is, and who owns it

A business process is a structured set of activities that takes inputs and produces an output a customer values. Order-to-delivery. Hire-to-retire. Quote-to-cash. The definition sounds bland until you notice what it rules out: a process is defined by its output to a customer, not by the department that performs it.

Key conceptBusiness process

A business process is a repeatable sequence of steps that turns inputs into something a customer values, crossing departments as it goes. Processes are where strategy becomes physical: Zara's two-week loop from the last chapter is a process, and so is getting one pizza from order to door. The discipline of this chapter is learning to see the process as an object you can map, measure, and redesign, rather than as "just how we do things."

That distinction, between the department that does the work and the customer who receives it, is the source of most organizational dysfunction. Companies are organized vertically into functions (marketing, operations, finance, IT) but value flows horizontally across them. Every point where work crosses a functional boundary is a handoff, and handoffs are where information is lost, time is spent waiting, and accountability evaporates. Ask who owns "the ordering process" at most firms and you get either a blank look or four different names.

Functions versus processes

Functions are how a company is staffed. Processes are how a company delivers. A customer never experiences your org chart; they experience the hand-off between your website and your kitchen. Information systems live on exactly those seams, which is why process thinking is the foundation for everything else in this course.

1.2 Where a process lives in the information system

A process and an information system are not the same thing, and it is worth being precise about the difference. A process is the work. An information system is the arrangement of five components that carries the work and makes it visible. Run the components over any process and each one has a specific job:

  • Data is the process's memory. Every step that matters leaves a record, and the steps that leave no record are the ones nobody can manage.
  • Software encodes the rules: what may happen next, what is not allowed, what triggers automatically.
  • Networks carry the work between the people and places that perform it. Every handoff on a map is a network crossing, whether that network is a cable or a person walking a folder to the next desk.
  • Hardware is where the process meets the physical world: the register, the oven, the scanner, the phone.
  • People perform the steps a machine cannot, decide the cases the rules do not cover, and are the only component that can notice the process itself is wrong.

Two consequences follow from that, and they run through the rest of this chapter. The first is that an information system can only manage what the process records. Capturing a timestamped event at each step, so the process becomes measurable at all, is called process instrumentation. If nobody captures the moment a pizza leaves the oven, no software can tell you how long it sat. Deciding what a process records is therefore a design decision, made by people, usually years before anyone needs the answer.

The second is the one this chapter's case is built on. Because the five components are the machinery and the process is the work, you can replace every component and still have the same bad process. New hardware, new software, faster networks, better data, and a broken sequence of steps produces a broken sequence of steps performed faster and recorded more precisely. That sentence is the entire Domino's case in advance.

2Mapping, measuring, redesigning

You cannot improve a process you have not written down, and the written-down version is almost never what managers believe is happening. Process mapping produces two documents, and the order you produce them in matters.

2.1 Two maps: as-is and to-be

  • The as-is map, what actually happens today, including the workarounds, the retyping of the same information into a second system, the phone call someone makes because the software does not support a case.
  • The to-be map, what should happen after redesign.

Skipping the as-is map is the most common failure in process work. Teams jump to the to-be because it is more fun, and they design an improvement to a process that does not exist. The as-is is where you find the bottleneck, the single constraint that sets the pace of the whole process. Improving anything other than the bottleneck produces no improvement in output, only local statistics that look better.

2.2 Drawing the map: swimlanes

Process maps have a standard grammar, and learning it takes five minutes: a box is an activity, a diamond is a decision with labeled exits (yes/no, approved/rejected), an arrow is the flow, and rounded ends mark start and finish. The move that makes a flowchart useful is the swimlane diagram: one horizontal lane per actor (customer, web system, store, kitchen, driver) with each activity drawn in the lane of whoever performs it.

Now every handoff is visible as an arrow crossing a lane boundary, and the diagram answers the question managers argue about most: who owns this step? Count the lane-crossings on any process map and you have counted its most likely failure points. The first discussion question asks you to draw Domino's order-to-delivery process in swimlanes: expect five lanes and be suspicious of any map with fewer than six crossings.

Build it yourself · A swimlane you can get wrong

There is no single right map, which is the uncomfortable part. Two people who work in the same company will draw this differently, and the argument about which one is correct is the useful part of process work. What is not a matter of opinion is the count: wherever your line changes lane, work is being handed to somebody else, and that is where it waits.

2.3 Measuring a process

Once a process is mapped, it can be measured. Four measures cover most of what a manager needs:

  • Cycle time, elapsed time from start to finish, including waiting. The twenty-two minutes between you tapping "order" and the pizza reaching your door. The six weeks between applying for a job and hearing back, most of which is your file sitting in somebody's queue.
  • Throughput, completed units per period. Pizzas out the door per hour on a Friday night. Patients seen per day in a clinic. Claims closed per week by an insurance team.
  • Quality / defect rate, share of outputs that fail to meet the standard. Orders remade or refunded. Prescriptions filled incorrectly. Parts returned by the customer who bought them.
  • Cost per transaction, resources consumed per unit of output. What it costs the company to process one order, one refund, or one new-hire's paperwork, once you count the staff time and not only the software licence.

Most companies pick one of the four and manage to it, because one number fits on a report and four do not. That choice is the measurement trap: optimize a process against a single metric for long enough and it will degrade every quality the metric does not capture. Nothing is being managed badly in those other places. Nothing is being managed there at all, because nothing in the process is watching. Pick a number below and see what it costs you, then carry the habit into the rest of the chapter. Whenever an organization manages to one number, ask what that number cannot see.

A process records what somebody decided to record, so the report can be honest, accurate, and green while the business gets worse everywhere the process was never asked to look.

2.4 Redesigning a process: improvement or reengineering?

Now you have a map and some numbers. The next question is what you are allowed to change, and there are only two answers. You can keep the process you have and make it work better, or you can decide the process itself is the wrong shape and build a different one. Almost every project you will ever sit in is one of these two, and confusing them is how companies spend a year making the wrong thing faster.

  • Incremental improvement keeps the shape of the process and tunes the steps: a faster oven, a better script, one fewer handoff. The process afterwards is recognizably the old one. The same people do the same jobs in the same order, with less friction.
  • Business process reengineering, usually shortened to BPR, throws the shape away and asks what the process would look like if you designed it today, from zero, around the outcome it is supposed to produce. Michael Hammer, who named it in 1990, put it bluntly: don't automate, obliterate. Steps disappear, jobs change, and sometimes a whole department has nothing left to do.

The test for which one you are looking at is not how big the change is, and it is not how much it cost. It is whether the steps and their order survive. Replacing paper forms with an app is improvement if the same approvals happen in the same sequence, however expensive the app was. Deleting the approval is reengineering, even if it took an afternoon. Most companies only ever improve, because reengineering requires somebody senior to say out loud that the current shape is wrong, which is a harder sentence than it sounds. Mini-case 1 is what it looks like when a company says it.

Quick quiz · Improvement or reengineering?

Five changes to a process. Decide whether each one improves the existing process or replaces it with a different one.

The test is not how big the change is. It is whether the steps and their order survive. Improvement keeps the process and makes it faster or cleaner; reengineering asks what the process is for and starts again.

3Two short cases, one old finding

Before the main case, here are two short ones. Process work has a long history and a stubbornly consistent finding, which is that the fix almost never turns out to be the equipment. Each case below fixed a process that was broken, and in each one the fix lived somewhere different: in the handoffs, or in what the process recorded. Notice what neither of them is. Neither is a story about buying better technology, and in the first one the computer was the thing that would not have helped.

3.1 Mini-case 1 · 1988: five hundred clerks and a computer that would not have helped

A clerk at Ford in 1988 has three pieces of paper on the desk. A purchase order from the purchasing department, saying what Ford asked a supplier to send. A receiving document from the loading dock, saying what actually turned up. An invoice from the supplier, saying what Ford is being billed for. The rule is that nobody gets paid until all three agree, and agreeing means every detail lines up: the same part number, the same quantity, the same price, the same terms. Fourteen separate data items had to match before the accounting department could release a payment.

They frequently did not match. The dock received 900 units of a part when the order said 1,000. The invoice priced an item at last quarter's rate. A part number was mistyped somewhere along the line. Every one of those disagreements became an investigation, and an investigation is a person on the phone to another department for however long it takes. Ford's accounts payable operation in North America employed more than five hundred people doing that work.

At first, Ford did what most companies do. It planned to install a new computer system, tighten the procedures, and cut the department by about twenty percent, from five hundred people to four hundred. That is a respectable target and the project would have been counted a success.

Then somebody looked at Mazda, in which Ford held a stake. Mazda's entire accounts payable organization was five people. Even adjusting for the fact that Mazda was a much smaller company, Ford concluded its own department was roughly five times the size it should be. The twenty percent target had not come from any analysis of what the work required. It had come from the shape of the process Ford already had.

So Ford redesigned the shape. Under what it called invoiceless processing, purchasing enters an order into a shared database and sends a copy to nobody. When the goods reach the receiving dock, the receiving clerk checks the database, and if the delivery matches an outstanding order, accepts it and records it. The system pays from that. There is no invoice to match, because there is no invoice. Headcount fell by about seventy-five percent.

Notice what Ford's original plan had been: buy a computer system to do the matching faster. That is an automation project, and it is the most natural project in the world to propose, because the work is obviously slow and computers are obviously fast. It would have worked, too. Four hundred clerks would have matched paper more quickly than five hundred did.

This is the case that produced the most quoted line in process work. Michael Hammer, writing it up in Harvard Business Review in 1990, used Ford to argue that companies were buying computers to speed up work that should not have been happening at all, and told them: don't automate, obliterate. The matching was not slow because the clerks were slow. It was slow because three departments were writing three documents about one delivery and then arguing about the differences. Automate that and you get the same argument, faster.

Where the fix lived: in the handoffs. The component: data. Take three things from this case.

  • Nothing was wrong with any clerk or any step. Every person was doing their job correctly. The problem was the shape: three departments each producing a document about the same delivery, and reconciling the differences had become a job for five hundred people. Draw it in swimlanes and it is visible at once, three lanes, three documents, and a bottleneck where they meet.
  • The fix was a data decision, not a software purchase. One shared record replaced three competing ones. Two documents did not need to agree with a third because the third no longer existed. This is business process redesign in its purest form: rethinking the process end to end rather than optimizing the steps in place.
  • It is the cleanest example in the chapter of reengineering against improvement. The twenty percent plan was improvement, and it kept every step. Deleting the invoice was reengineering, and it removed the reason the steps existed. Same department, same year, same problem, and a difference of fifty-five percentage points in the result.
Check yourselfFord's original plan, a new system and a twenty percent cut, would have been reported internally as a win. Explain precisely why that is the dangerous outcome, and say what question the team asked that turned a twenty percent target into a seventy-five percent one.
Show the answer

It is dangerous because it would have been measured. Twenty percent fewer clerks against a target of twenty percent is a success by every number the project tracked, and a success gets no second look. The four hundred people still matching paper would have stayed, the three-way mismatches would have stayed, and the idea that the invoice itself was the problem would never have come up, because nobody re-examines a project that hit its target. The question that changed it was the one Mazda forced: not "how do we match invoices faster?" but "why do we have invoices to match at all?" That is the difference between improving a process and redesigning it, and it is why the first thing to ask about any target is whether it measures the right thing.

3.2 Mini-case 2 · 2024: the door that was opened and never written down

On 5 January 2024, an Alaska Airlines 737 MAX 9 left Portland and, at about 16,000 feet, a panel covering an unused emergency exit tore away from the fuselage. The cabin lost pressure. Nobody was seriously hurt, and the crew landed the aircraft back in Portland.

Four bolts that hold that panel in place were missing. The panel had been opened at Boeing's factory in Renton, about ninety miles south of Bellingham, so that work could be done behind it, and then closed again without the bolts being put back.

Here is the part of the story that belongs in this chapter. Investigators could not establish who opened it, or when, or who closed it. Boeing's own process required a removal document to be created whenever that panel is opened. The document is not paperwork for its own sake: it instructs the workers and it triggers a quality inspection afterwards. No removal document was ever created. So there was no instruction, no inspection, and afterwards no record that anything had happened at all. Investigators asked Boeing for the documentation and were told it could not be found, which was true, because it did not exist.

Two further details show what the process was actually made of. Of roughly two dozen workers on the door teams at that factory, only one had opened that kind of panel before, and that person was not at work when this one was opened. And the panel was reported to have moved slightly upward on earlier flights before it finally departed the aircraft.

Where the fix lived: in what the process recorded. The component: data. The point of this case is the chapter's rule, arriving at 16,000 feet: an information system can only manage what the process records. No hardware failed here and no software was involved. A step happened and produced no record, and everything downstream that depended on that record simply did not happen.

Follow the consequences in order, because they are what "no record" actually costs. No document meant no instruction, so the people closing the panel had nothing telling them what had to go back in. No document meant no inspection was triggered, so the missing bolts were never caught. No document meant no evidence afterwards, so investigators could not establish who opened the panel, when, or who closed it. Boeing had quality systems, inspectors, and auditors. None of them could act, because to a system a step that leaves no record did not happen.

Check yourselfThe removal document does two jobs at once: it tells somebody what to do, and it leaves evidence that they did it. Name a process you have been part of where one step leaves no record, and say which of those two jobs is missing there.
Show the answer

Any answer with a real process in it is a good one. The pattern to look for: a step that people do by habit, with nothing written down when it is done, so the second job, evidence, is the one missing. Handing over a shift without a note. Checking a fridge temperature without logging it. Approving something verbally. In each one the work happens and the record does not, and the system built on top of that process, whatever it is, cannot see the step and therefore cannot manage it. That is the chapter's rule: an information system can only manage what the process records.

3.3 What the two have in common

Two very different stories: a clerk matching paper in 1988, and a panel leaving an aircraft in 2024. Three things connect them, and all three are about to reappear in the main case.

  • Neither fix was a technology purchase. Ford's problem was solved by deleting a document, Boeing's by a form that should have been filled in. In the one case where buying something was the plan, the purchase would have been counted a success and left most of the problem in place.
  • Both failures lived in the data component. Ford recorded the same delivery three times and had to reconcile the copies. Boeing recorded an entire step zero times. Too many records and no record are the same kind of design mistake, made in opposite directions.
  • Nothing improved until somebody described the process as a process. A comparison with Mazda. A form that should have been filled in. The improvement was invisible until the process was stated out loud, in a form other people could argue with.

That last one is what section 2 taught you to produce, and it is what the company in the main case went five years without.

4Main case · Domino's: the tracker on a broken pizza

In 2008 Domino's shipped the best piece of consumer software in its industry, and inside the company there was good reason to believe it would turn the business around. It did not. Sales kept falling for another year. This section walks the case in the order it happened, because the order is the argument. Start with the timeline, then we will go back and take it in pieces.

  • 1960Tom Monaghan and his brother buy one pizza store in Ypsilanti, Michigan. Delivery, not dining, is the business.
  • 1973Domino's promises delivery in thirty minutes or the pizza is free. Speed becomes the product.
  • 1993The guarantee is dropped after lawsuits over crashes involving drivers under time pressure. The habit of managing to the clock stays.
  • 2008Pizza Tracker launches, years ahead of the rest of the industry. Sales keep falling.
  • 2009Taste tests, a viral video, store closures, stock in single digits.
  • Dec 2009The "Pizza Turnaround" campaign airs: executives and chefs read real customer criticism aloud on camera, including the cardboard-crust comparison, in a documentary-style spot.
  • Dec 2009The reformulated recipe (reworked crust, rebuilt sauce, upgraded cheese) launches at the same time as the ad, so the admission and the fix arrive together rather than in sequence.
  • 2010Patrick Doyle becomes CEO; the new recipe rolls out to all U.S. stores. Sales turn sharply upward.
  • 2011Mobile apps relaunched for iPhone and Android.
  • 2013Pizza Profiles: saved preferences and payment, collapsing reorder to a few taps.
  • 2015AnyWare: ordering from a watch, a TV, a car dashboard, a text message.
  • 2023–25Uber Eats, then DoorDash, on terms that keep Domino's own drivers.

Look at the first four entries. Everything Domino's was good at in 2008 it had been building for forty-eight years, and none of it was the pizza.

4.1 Thirty years of measuring minutes

Domino's was a delivery company before it was a food company. The thirty-minute guarantee, introduced in 1973, was not a marketing line bolted onto the business. It was the business. Your pizza inside thirty minutes of the order, or you did not pay for it. The promise told the customer what to expect, told every store what to optimize, and told head office which number to look at on Monday morning. "Thirty minutes or it's free" was the company's identity for twenty years, and it worked. In the language of section 2, Domino's managed one measure, cycle time, and managed it superbly.

Here is the sentence to hold on to. By its own later account, Domino's had been leading the industry in delivery for the wrong reasons. It met the promise with frozen and canned ingredients that cooked fast and kept, which is where the cardboard crust came from, and at times it met the promise at the cost of driver safety.

The guarantee ended in December 1993. Drivers rushing to beat the clock had been in serious crashes for years, and after a jury in St. Louis returned a large punitive verdict against the company in a case brought by a woman a driver had struck, the promise was withdrawn. What did not end was the habit. The company kept measuring minutes, kept competing on minutes, and in December 2007 introduced the slogan "You Got 30 Minutes," which pointed at the old promise without making it. Nothing in the process measured whether the pizza was any good, so nothing in the company acted on it. The recipe went roughly thirty years without a serious change.

4.2 2008: the tracker, and what the company expected from it

The Pizza Tracker showed customers a live bar: your order has been placed, it is being prepared, it is in the oven, it is out for delivery. Online ordering had arrived shortly before, and the tracker sat on top of it. It was new, nobody else in food delivery had anything like it, and customers liked it immediately.

The expectation inside the company was reasonable. Domino's competed on the experience of waiting, the tracker made waiting better, so the tracker should sell more pizza. That reasoning contains an assumption nobody had tested: that the reason people were not ordering had something to do with the ordering.

4.3 The verdict customers had already reached

They had not stopped ordering because ordering was hard. The customers' verdicts on the product itself were not subtle. Focus groups described the crust as cardboard, the sauce as ketchup, and the cheese as processed-feeling. In industry taste tests, Domino's pizza tied for last place with Chuck E. Cheese. A video of employees contaminating food went viral. Papa John's ran "Better Ingredients. Better Pizza." directly at the quality gap.

The operating numbers matched the opinions. Same-store sales are the sales at stores open at least a year, the standard measure of whether existing stores are growing. Domino's U.S. same-store sales fell 4.9% system-wide in 2008, roughly seven times worse than the industry average, and the company closed 120 domestic units between 2008 and 2009 while the pizza category itself was growing. A stock that had traded above $30 in 2007 was under $7 by late 2008.

So the tracker launched into that. It worked exactly as designed, every day, on every order, and it made no difference to any of those numbers.

4.4 The diagnosis: good technology, bad process

A pizza tracker is a system for watching a process. It cannot improve the process it is watching.

Domino's had built excellent information technology on top of a broken business process, and between 2008 and late 2009 the technology faithfully reported the brokenness in real time. This is the most important lesson in the chapter and one of the most important in the course: technology applied to a bad process produces a faster, better-documented bad process.

You can see the shape of the failure by asking what the 2008 process recorded. Every step that moved the pizza produced a timestamped event, which is why the tracker could draw itself at all. The thing customers were actually complaining about produced no event anywhere.

Here is the same point in the five components. Look at which rows held the failure. It was not the software.

4.5 December 2009: fixing the product in public

The fix, when it came, was not a system. Domino's rebuilt the recipe: new crust, new sauce, new cheese. Then it did the part that still gets taught in marketing courses. It put the criticism on television. In the "Pizza Turnaround" spots, executives and chefs sat and read real customer comments aloud, including the cardboard line, and said the company agreed.

The sequence matters twice over. First, the ad and the new recipe launched together, in December 2009, so the admission and the fix arrived on the same day rather than as a promise followed by a wait. Second, in section 2's terms the company did both kinds of redesign, in order. Changing what the product was and how the company faced its customers was reengineering: the old shape, thirty years of speed at any cost to the food, was thrown away. Everything since, the tuning of stores and delivery driven by tracker data, has been incremental improvement on that new shape.

The public admission was itself a process change, and this is the part most readers skip. It created a feedback loop, a path by which what comes out of a process re-enters it as an input. Customer criticism, which the company had previously absorbed without recording, became something the company had publicly committed itself to respond to. Before December 2009 nobody's job depended on the complaint. Afterward, somebody's did.

Domino's posted a 14.3% same-store sales increase in the first quarter of 2010, and the stock rose 130% between December 2009 and December 2010. By September 2010, by a count the company and business-school cases repeated at the time, it ranked as the fourth-largest e-retailer in the United States, behind only Amazon, Staples, and Office Depot.

Read that last number carefully, because it is the chapter's main point in one line. A pizza chain became a top-five e-commerce company, not by building better e-commerce software than Amazon, but by fixing the product its e-commerce software was selling. The digital ordering capability had existed, largely unchanged, throughout the collapse.

4.7 Why the tracker mattered after all

None of this makes the Pizza Tracker a mistake. It makes it a second-order effect that could not pay off until the first-order problem was fixed. Once the pizza was worth ordering, the tracker turned out to be doing two useful jobs at once.

The first is the one customers see. Operational transparency means showing customers the work being done on their behalf instead of hiding it behind a loading spinner or a closed kitchen door. Research by Ryan Buell and colleagues found that people value a service more when they can see the effort inside it. The tracker changes nothing about how fast the pizza is made; it changes what waiting feels like, by turning a blank interval into an observable sequence of stages. You have met the same design many times this week: package tracking, the ride-share map, the bank transfer status bar, the "your claim is being reviewed" screen. Domino's built the version everyone else copied. In 2012 it built the same idea in architecture: a store format it calls pizza theater, with an open view of the kitchen from the lobby and a screen showing the status of carryout orders. Same principle, no screen required. Expose the stages and waiting becomes legible.

The second job is the one that matters to this course. To display a stage, the system must first capture that stage. A tracker forces every step of fulfillment to emit a timestamped event, which is the process instrumentation from section 1, and it means the company now knows where time actually goes, per store, per shift, per driver. The customer-facing feature is a by-product of instrumenting the process. When you see a status bar anywhere, ask what data it must be collecting in order to draw itself; the answer is usually worth more to the company than the bar is to you. That instrumentation is what let Domino's reason about its operations at all in the decade that followed.

4.8 Ordering from anywhere: what AnyWare actually is

Beginning around 2015, Domino's built what it calls AnyWare. The examples sound like a stunt: order a pizza from a Ford dashboard, a Samsung smart TV, an Apple Watch, Amazon's voice assistant, Facebook Messenger, Slack, or by sending a pizza emoji as a text or a tweet. The company now supports well over a dozen such paths.

What they actually built is easier to see if you ignore the gadgets and ask what happens after the customer taps. Each of those surfaces is a thin front door. None of them contains a copy of the ordering logic, the menu, the pricing, the store routing, or the customer's record. They all reach the same ordering system, which reaches the same store, which runs the same tracked fulfillment process from the subsection above. That is what omnichannel means: many customer-facing surfaces, one underlying process and one record of the order.

Adding a new ordering surface is therefore a small piece of work rather than a new company. That property is what people mean when they say an information system is well architected: the surfaces can multiply while the process underneath stays one process. It is also the reason the novelty is worth teaching. The Apple Watch app is a gimmick. The fact that it did not require its own copy of the business is not.

4.9 The data underneath

Every one of those orders produces data, and the volume is the point. A data pipeline is an automated route that moves data from where it is produced to where it is analyzed. Domino's has described running thousands of them, carrying data from thousands of stores along with supply chain, staffing, and third-party sources into a central warehouse. What comes out the other end is one view of the customer and the operation. A loyalty program launched in 2015 attaches a large share of those orders to identified individuals rather than anonymous transactions.

You should recognize that last mechanism, because you met it in Chapter 2 with Starbucks and you are about to spend a month inside it. An order attached to a known customer can be analyzed, predicted, and served. An anonymous order is a single event that teaches the company nothing. Your lab client is a co-op whose entire business model depends on making that attachment happen, and one of whose stores has quietly stopped.

By the late 2010s the company was reporting that a majority of United States sales arrived through digital channels, alongside a long run of same-store sales growth. Patrick Doyle's line, that Domino's is a technology company that sells pizza, stopped being a provocation and started being a description of the org chart.

4.10 Automating the steps

Ordering surfaces changed how work enters the process. The other change of that decade was who, or what, performs the steps once the order is in. Two levels of automation appear in the Domino's story, and both are ordinary corporate technology rather than anything futuristic.

Workflow automation is software that routes and sequences the work while humans still perform the steps. That is exactly what the tracker era was: the system sequences and timestamps, people make the pizza. Robotic process automation (RPA) is software that performs the clerical steps itself, clicking through screens the way a person would, which suits high-volume rule-following work such as retyping an order from one system into another. Domino's version arrived in 2014 as Dom, a voice-ordering assistant that took the order-entry step away from human typing entirely.

Notice what both of these have in common, because it is the condition for the next subsection. Each one was applied to a step that had already been mapped, defined, and recorded.

4.11 What comes next: agents, aggregators, and the same argument

The turnaround is more than fifteen years old, so it is fair to ask whether the lesson survived contact with newer technology. Three developments are worth following, and all three are process decisions dressed as technology decisions.

The agent. The level of automation businesses are reaching for now is the AI agent: software given a goal rather than a script, which plans the steps itself, executes across several systems, and handles the exceptions in between. An agent handling a refund does not follow a flowchart somebody drew. It reads the complaint, checks the order history, applies the policy, issues the credit, and writes to the customer, deciding the sequence as it goes. On its second-quarter 2026 earnings call, Domino's described an orchestration agent that decides when each pizza should be made so that it leaves the oven as the driver arrives, for its own orders and aggregator orders alike. That is a narrow job inside a process whose steps are already defined, already instrumented, and already understood, which is precisely the condition that makes it safe. The rule to carry out of this chapter is that an agent can only be trusted with a process that is mapped, measured, and instrumented. Nobody should hand a goal to an agent inside a process they cannot draw.

That rule is old. What is new is how cheap it has become to break it. Hammer's 1990 line, don't automate, obliterate, was aimed at companies paving the cowpath. A cowpath is the winding track cattle wear into a field, which everyone then follows only because it is already there; paving it means pouring technology onto a process shape that should have been thrown away. Ford could have paved its cowpath with a faster invoice-matching system and called it a win. An AI agent is the fastest paving machine ever built, and it will execute a broken process with perfect diligence, at machine speed, and do the same with a bad number. The order of operations does not change with the technology: map it, fix it, then automate it. Domino's fixed the pizza before it built the empire of ordering surfaces.

The aggregators. For years Domino's refused to appear on DoorDash or Uber Eats, and was almost alone among large chains in that. The stated reason was that delivery was the process it had spent forty years building, and handing it to a third party meant handing over the last mile (the trip from the store to the customer's door), the timing, and the data. Then it joined Uber Eats in 2023 and DoorDash in 2025, with the DoorDash rollout complete across the United States by the fall of 2025.

Look closely at what they agreed to, because it is unusual. Domino's takes orders from the aggregators and fulfills them with its own drivers. The platform supplies demand; Domino's keeps the process. In the terms of section 1, they bought a new front door and refused to give up the machinery behind it. Aggregator orders were about three percent of United States sales in 2024, before DoorDash. Management has said the channel could reach a billion dollars a year, roughly a tenth of its United States sales, made up of customers it would not otherwise have reached.

The results, which are ordinary in an instructive way. United States same-store sales grew 3.0 percent in fiscal 2025, and the company reported gaining another point of market share. The research firm Technomic put its share of fast-food pizza at about 30 percent in 2024, up from 26 percent in 2019. In the second quarter of 2026, order counts rose while the average ticket fell, because a premium product line underperformed. That is a product miss inside a process that is working, and the difference is worth naming: 2008 was a product failure that no process could rescue, and 2026 is a product miss that the process absorbs.

One more thing about Domino's, and it is the part most likely to matter in ten years. More than sixty percent of the company's revenue comes from selling supplies to its own franchisees rather than from pizza. The dough and the trucks are a process nobody outside can duplicate, and no delivery platform can buy its way into it. That is the argument from Chapter 3 arriving in this case: the parts are for sale and the arrangement is not.

4.12 What a business technology manager takes from this chapter

Strip the case down to what you can reuse in any company. Four decisions came up in it, and each one is a decision you will be in the room for.

  • Fix the process before you buy the technology. The Pizza Tracker worked perfectly on top of a product customers disliked, and it did nothing for sales until the recipe and the kitchen changed. When someone proposes a system, ask what process it will sit on and whether that process works today. A good system on a bad process is a faster way to disappoint people.
  • Map what actually happens before you design what should. Ford's team only saw the fix when they drew the invoice process as it really ran, with hundreds of clerks matching paper. Skipping the as-is map is the most common failure in process work, because everyone in the room believes they already know how it works, and nobody does.
  • Make sure every step leaves a record, because a system can only manage what the process records. Boeing's door plug had no removal record, so no system in the company could see it was missing. Domino's recorded minutes and not quality, so nothing in the company could see the pizza. Before you automate or measure anything, ask which steps produce no evidence. Those are the ones that will fail invisibly.
  • Hand software only the steps that are already defined and already recorded. Domino's orchestration agent sequences steps inside a process that has been mapped and timestamped for more than fifteen years, which is why it is safe. The order of work is fix, record, then automate, and it is the manager, not the software, who decides when a step is ready.

5The downside: transparency for whom

Section 4 is a story about an information system working. Before leaving it, turn the tracker around.

5.1 The worker inside the timestamp

Operational transparency runs one direction: the customer sees the store's work, and the store, in turn, is seen. Every timestamp that delights a customer is also a performance record of the worker who produced it. A system that measures every stage of every order is a management instrument pointed at the kitchen.

The pattern is everywhere once you look for it, and usually the customer-facing feature and the management instrument are the same system.

  • Warehouses. The reason a retailer can tell you your order is picked and packed is that every pick is timed. Amazon's warehouses measure the rate at which each worker picks items and how long they are idle between picks, a measure the company calls time off task, and that measurement has been used in decisions about who keeps the job.
  • Delivery driving. The map that shows you a driver approaching also reports that driver's speed, braking, route choice, and time per stop to a dispatcher. Delivery and freight companies have run telematics of this kind for years.
  • Any job with a queue. Call handling time, tickets closed per hour, chat response time. The customer sees responsiveness; the worker sees a number that has to keep going up.

None of these measurements is illegitimate. The question a manager should be able to answer is a narrower one: which of these numbers is here to run the process, and which is here to rank the people in it? The pressure of being continuously measured is part of what "the process improved" means from the inside. Chapter 2 raised the same point about algorithmic scheduling, and here it appears in a second form.

5.2 The customer the system did not see

There is also a customer the system did not see. Domino's built ordering into phones, watches, TVs, and cars, an ordering surface for practically everyone, and in 2016 a blind customer named Guillermo Robles could not order a pizza from the website or the app with his screen reader. The lawsuit that followed reached the Ninth Circuit and, in 2019, the Supreme Court declined to disturb the ruling against Domino's. Hold the shape of it: the company that made ordering effortless from a Samsung TV had not made it possible from a screen reader. Reach and access are not the same achievement.

Domino's was not unusual, which is the uncomfortable part. The National Federation of the Blind sued Target over a website its members could not use with a screen reader. It settled in 2008, with the company agreeing to fix the site and pay into a fund for the class. The same gap shows up in the physical world. A restaurant replaces its counter with a touchscreen kiosk that a blind customer cannot operate alone. A menu is posted as a photograph of a page, which a screen reader reads as nothing at all. In each case a company added a channel, measured how well it performed, and never measured who could not use it. Chapter 12 comes back to this and gives you the standard it is measured against.

Key takeaways
  • A process is defined by what it delivers to a customer, not by which department performs it. Value flows sideways through a company that is organized vertically.
  • You cannot improve a process you have not written down, and the written-down version is almost never what managers believe is happening.
  • The fix is never the equipment. In every case in this chapter it lived in the design, the handoffs, or what the process recorded.
  • Domino's did not win by buying technology. It rebuilt the ordering process so that the system could see every step, then made the system visible to the customer.
  • An information system can only manage what the process records. Quality at Domino's went unrecorded for years, so nothing in the system could act on it until a person did.

6Vocabulary

Business process
Structured activities converting inputs into an output a customer values.
As-is / to-be map
Documentation of the process as it currently runs, and as redesigned.
Handoff
A transfer of work across a boundary; the usual site of delay and lost information.
Bottleneck
The constraint that sets the pace of the entire process.
Swimlane diagram
A process map with one lane per actor; handoffs appear as arrows crossing lanes.
Cycle time
Elapsed time from process start to finish, waiting included.
Throughput
Completed units per unit of time.
Workflow automation
Software routes and tracks the work; humans perform the steps.
Robotic process automation (RPA)
Software performs rule-following clerical steps itself.
AI agent
Software given a goal rather than a script; plans and executes steps across systems. Requires a mapped, measured process to be trusted.
Business process reengineering
Redesigning a process from zero around the outcome, rather than tuning its current shape.
Operational transparency
Deliberately exposing internal process stages to the customer.
Process instrumentation
Capturing timestamped events at each step so the process becomes measurable.
Feedback loop
A path by which process output re-enters as input, enabling correction.
Omnichannel
Many customer-facing surfaces reaching one underlying process and one record of the order.
Data pipeline
An automated route that moves data from where it is produced to where it is analyzed.
Business process redesign
Rethinking a process end to end rather than optimizing steps in place.
