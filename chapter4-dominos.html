In 2008, Domino's launched a piece of software that is now taught in business schools worldwide and copied by every industry from banking to healthcare. Over the following year, the company's sales fell, its stores closed, and its stock collapsed. The Pizza Tracker did not save Domino's. Understanding why is the entire point of this chapter.

01A tracker on a broken pizza

The Pizza Tracker showed customers a live bar: your order has been placed, it's being prepared, it's in the oven, it's out for delivery. It was genuinely novel, it delighted people, and it worked exactly as designed. It also arrived at a company whose product customers actively disliked.

The verdicts were not subtle. Focus groups described the crust as cardboard, the sauce as ketchup, and the cheese as processed-feeling. In industry taste tests, Domino's pizza tied for last place with Chuck E. Cheese. A video of employees contaminating food went viral. The market rendered its own verdict: a stock that had traded above $30 in 2007 was under $7 by late 2008, while Papa John's ran "Better Ingredients. Better Pizza." directly at the quality gap. The operating numbers matched, U.S. same-store sales fell 4.9% system-wide in 2008, roughly seven times worse than the industry average, and the company closed 120 domestic units between 2008 and 2009 while the pizza category itself was growing.

A pizza tracker is a system for watching a process. It cannot improve the process it is watching.

Domino's had built excellent information technology on top of a broken business process, and the technology faithfully reported the brokenness in real time. This is the most important lesson in the chapter and one of the most important in the course: technology applied to a bad process produces a faster, better-documented bad process.

02What a business process is

Key conceptBusiness process

A business process is a repeatable sequence of steps that turns inputs into something a customer values, crossing departments as it goes. Processes are where strategy becomes physical: Zara’s two-week loop from last chapter is a process, and so is getting one pizza from order to door. The discipline of this chapter is learning to see the process as an object you can map, measure, and redesign, rather than as “just how we do things.”

A business process is a structured set of activities that takes inputs and produces an output a customer values. Order-to-delivery. Hire-to-retire. Quote-to-cash. The definition sounds bland until you notice what it rules out: a process is defined by its output to a customer, not by the department that performs it.

That distinction is the source of most organizational dysfunction. Companies are organized vertically into functions (marketing, operations, finance, IT) but value flows horizontally across them. Every point where work crosses a functional boundary is a handoff, and handoffs are where information is lost, time is spent waiting, and accountability evaporates. Ask who owns "the ordering process" at most firms and you get either a blank look or four different names.

Functions versus processes

Functions are how a company is staffed. Processes are how a company delivers. A customer never experiences your org chart; they experience the seam between your website and your kitchen. Information systems live on exactly those seams, which is why process thinking is the foundation for everything else in this course.

03Mapping the as-is

You cannot improve a process you have not written down, and the written-down version is almost never what managers believe is happening. Process mapping produces two artifacts:

  • The as-is map, what actually happens today, including the workarounds, the re-keying, the phone call someone makes because the system doesn't support a case.
  • The to-be map, what should happen after redesign.

Skipping the as-is is the most common failure in process work. Teams jump to the to-be because it is more fun, and they design an improvement to a process that does not exist. The as-is is where you find the bottleneck, the single constraint that sets the pace of the whole process. Improving anything other than the bottleneck produces no improvement in output, only local statistics that look better.

Drawing the map: swimlanes

Process maps have a standard grammar, and learning it takes five minutes: a box is an activity, a diamond is a decision with labeled exits (yes/no, approved/rejected), an arrow is the flow, and rounded ends mark start and finish. The move that makes a flowchart genuinely useful is the swimlane: one horizontal lane per actor (customer, web system, store, kitchen, driver) with each activity drawn in the lane of whoever performs it. Now every handoff is visible as an arrow crossing a lane boundary, and the diagram answers the question managers argue about most: who owns this step? Count the lane-crossings on any process map and you have counted its most likely failure points. You will draw Domino's order-to-delivery in swimlanes for Wednesday: expect five lanes and be suspicious of any map with fewer than six crossings.

Measuring a process

Four measures cover most of what you need:

  • Cycle time, elapsed time from start to finish, including waiting.
  • Throughput, completed units per period.
  • Quality / defect rate, share of outputs that fail to meet the standard.
  • Cost per transaction, resources consumed per unit of output.

Domino's spent three decades optimizing exactly one of these. The famous thirty-minute delivery guarantee made delivery time the one number the entire company managed to, and the company got extremely good at it. Too good, in a sense. By its own later account, Domino's had been leading the industry in delivery for the wrong reasons. It met its speed promise with frozen and canned ingredients that left the crust tasting like cardboard, and at times at the cost of driver safety.

The measurement trap

Optimizing a process against a single metric will, given enough time, degrade every quality that metric does not capture. Domino's measured minutes and got minutes. Quality was not being managed badly. It was not being managed at all, because nothing in the process was watching it. You will see this again in Week 6 with Moneyball, where an entire industry measured the wrong thing for a century.

04Redesigning the product, in public

Key conceptImprovement vs. reengineering

There are two ways to change a process. Incremental improvement keeps the process shape and tunes the steps: faster ovens, better scripts, one fewer handoff. Business process reengineering (BPR) throws the shape away and asks what the process would look like if you designed it today, from zero, around the outcome. Michael Hammer’s 1990 formulation was blunt: don’t automate, obliterate. Domino’s did both, in order: the 2009 recipe-and-honesty campaign reengineered how the product and its process faced the public, and the years of tracker-driven tuning since have been incremental improvement on the new shape. Most companies only ever do the second, because the first requires admitting the current shape is wrong.

The turnaround inverted the usual order of operations. Most companies fix quietly and announce selectively. Domino's announced the failure first.

  • 2008Pizza Tracker launches. Sales continue to fall.
  • 2009Taste tests, viral video, store closures, stock in single digits.
  • Dec 2009The "Pizza Turnaround" campaign airs: executives and chefs read real customer criticism aloud on camera, including the cardboard-crust comparison, in a documentary-style spot.
  • Dec 2009The reformulated recipe (reworked crust, rebuilt sauce, upgraded cheese) launches simultaneously with the ad, so the admission and the fix arrive together rather than in sequence.
  • 2010Patrick Doyle becomes CEO; new recipe rolls out to all U.S. stores.
  • 2011Mobile apps relaunched for iPhone and Android.
  • 2013Pizza Profiles: saved preferences and payment, collapsing reorder to a few taps.

Two aspects of the sequencing matter analytically. First, the product was fixed before the digital ordering push accelerated. The 2011–2013 technology investments landed on a process that now produced something worth ordering. Second, the public admission was itself a process change: it created a feedback loop. Customer criticism, previously an externality the company absorbed, became an input the company was contractually committed in public to respond to.

05Operational transparency

Key conceptOperational transparency

Showing customers the work being done on their behalf, rather than hiding it behind a spinner or a counter. Research by Ryan Buell and colleagues found that people value a service more when they can see the effort inside it. The Pizza Tracker is the canonical business example, and it carries a system truth: to show a customer each stage, the company must first timestamp each stage, and once every step is timestamped, every step can be measured and fixed.

Return to the tracker, now that the process underneath it works. What the Pizza Tracker actually does is make an internal process visible to the customer: what researchers call operational transparency. It changes nothing about how fast the pizza is made. It changes what waiting feels like, by converting an opaque interval into an observable sequence of stages.

It also does something quieter and more valuable to the firm. To display a stage, the system must capture that stage. A tracker forces every step of the fulfillment process to emit a timestamped event, which means the company now has data on where time actually goes, per store, per shift, per driver. The customer-facing feature is a by-product of instrumenting the process. That instrumentation is what let Domino's later reason about its operations at all.

Why this generalizes

Package tracking, ride-share maps, bank transfer status bars, and the "your claim is being reviewed" screen are all the same design: expose the stages, capture the events. The business case is always double: it reassures the customer, and it forces the company to record each step as it happens. When you see a status tracker, ask what data it must be collecting to render itself.

Domino's posted a 14.3% same-store sales increase in the first quarter of 2010, and the stock rose 130% between December 2009 and December 2010. By September 2010 the company ranked as the fourth-largest e-retailer in the United States, behind only Amazon, Staples, and Office Depot.

Read that last statistic carefully, because it is the chapter's thesis in one line. A pizza chain became a top-five e-commerce company, not by building better e-commerce software than Amazon, but by fixing the product its e-commerce software was selling. The digital ordering capability had existed, largely unchanged, throughout the collapse.

07What they built after the process worked

The turnaround gets the headlines, but the decade after it is the part with the transferable lesson. Once the product was worth ordering, Domino's spent ten years making ordering the thing it was best at, and the results show what happens when technology lands on a process that is finally sound.

AnyWare: ordering as an interface problem

Beginning around 2015, the company built what it calls AnyWare, a set of ordering paths across platforms that had never been ordering platforms. A Ford dashboard. A Samsung smart TV. An Apple Watch. A pizza emoji sent as a tweet or a text. Amazon's voice assistant. Facebook Messenger and Slack. The stated goal was to let people order using whatever they already had in their hand, and the company now supports well over a dozen such paths.

Look past the novelty at the architectural claim underneath. Every one of those surfaces reaches the same ordering system, which reaches the same store, which triggers the same tracked fulfillment process from Section 05. Adding a new ordering surface is a small piece of work rather than a new company, because the process beneath is one process. That property has a name you will meet in Week 10, and it is what people mean when they say a system is well architected.

The data underneath

Every one of those orders emits data, and the volume is the point. Domino's has described integrating data from thousands of stores along with supply chain, staffing, and third-party sources, running through thousands of separate data pipelines into a central warehouse, to assemble one view of the customer and the operation. A loyalty program launched in 2015 attaches a large share of those orders to identified individuals rather than anonymous transactions.

Recognize that last mechanism, because you met it in Chapter 2 with Starbucks and you are about to spend a month inside it. An order attached to a known customer can be analyzed, predicted, and served. An anonymous order is a single event that teaches the company nothing. Your lab client is a co-op whose entire business model depends on making that attachment happen, and one of whose stores has quietly stopped.

Pizza theater: the tracker, rebuilt in a room

In 2012 Domino's introduced a store format it calls pizza theater: an open view of food preparation from the lobby, seating, and a screen showing the status of carryout orders. Read that as a design decision rather than a decor decision. It is the Pizza Tracker implemented in architecture. The same principle from Section 05, expose the stages so waiting becomes legible, applied to a physical room instead of a screen.

By the late 2010s the company was reporting that a majority of United States sales arrived through digital channels, alongside a long unbroken run of same-store sales growth. Patrick Doyle's line, that Domino's is a technology company that sells pizza, stopped being a provocation and started being a description of the org chart.

The sequence is the lesson

Everything in this section was possible in 2008. The company had online ordering, a tracker, and capable engineers, and sales fell anyway, because the pizza was bad. The technology only compounded once the underlying process produced something worth ordering. Get the order of operations wrong and you build a very efficient way to sell a product people do not want.

From automating steps to software that runs the process

AnyWare multiplied the ways an order could enter the process. The decade's other change is who, or what, executes the process once the order is in, and it is worth naming the ladder, because your career will be spent somewhere on it.

The first rung is workflow automation: software routes the work, humans do the steps. The tracker era Domino's built is exactly this; the system sequences and timestamps, people make the pizza. The second rung is robotic process automation (RPA): software performs the clerical steps itself, clicking through screens the way a person would, ideal for high-volume, rule-following work like re-keying orders between systems. Domino's own early move up this ladder was Dom, a voice-ordering assistant launched back in 2014, which took the order-entry step away from human typing entirely.

The rung businesses are climbing now is the AI agent: software that is given a goal rather than a script, plans the steps itself, executes across multiple systems, and handles the exceptions in between. An agent handling a refund does not follow a flowchart someone drew; it reads the complaint, checks the order history, applies the policy, issues the credit, and writes the customer, deciding the sequence as it goes. Week 10 covers how such systems work. What belongs in this chapter is what they require: an agent can only be trusted with a process that is mapped, measured, and instrumented, which is to say a process that has been through everything this chapter teaches. Nobody should hand a goal to an agent inside a process they cannot draw.

Hammer's warning, at machine speed

The oldest finding in process work is that automating a bad process gives you the same bad process, faster. Hammer's 1990 line, don't automate, obliterate, was aimed at companies paving the cowpath: pouring technology onto a process shape that should have been thrown away. AI agents make this temptation stronger than it has ever been, because automation is now cheap enough to skip the redesign entirely. An agent will execute a broken process with perfect diligence at machine speed, and the bullwhip chapter in Week 7 shows what automated diligence does to a bad number. The order of operations this chapter argues for does not change with the technology: map it, fix it, then automate it. Domino's fixed the pizza before it built the empire of ordering surfaces, and the sequence is the lesson.

08The other side: transparency for whom

Before mapping the components, turn the tracker around. Operational transparency runs one direction: the customer sees the store’s work, and the store, in turn, is seen. Every timestamp that delights a customer is also a performance record of the worker who produced it. A system that measures every stage of every order is a management instrument pointed at the kitchen, and the pressure of being continuously measured is part of what “the process improved” means from the inside. Chapter 2 raised the same point about algorithmic scheduling, and it lands here in a second form.

There is also a customer the system did not see. Domino’s built ordering into phones, watches, TVs, and cars, an ordering surface for practically everyone, and in 2016 a blind customer named Guillermo Robles could not order a pizza from the website or the app with his screen reader. The lawsuit that followed reached the Ninth Circuit and, in 2019, the Supreme Court declined to disturb the ruling against Domino’s. You will study the case properly in Week 9’s software design session; for now, hold the shape of it: the company that made ordering effortless from a Samsung TV had not made it possible from a screen reader. Reach and access are not the same achievement.

10Vocabulary

Business process
Structured activities converting inputs into an output a customer values.
As-is / to-be map
Documentation of the process as it currently runs, and as redesigned.
Handoff
A transfer of work across a boundary; the usual site of delay and lost information.
Bottleneck
The constraint that sets the pace of the entire process.
Swimlane diagram
A process map with one lane per actor; handoffs appear as arrows crossing lanes.
Cycle time
Elapsed time from process start to finish, waiting included.
Throughput
Completed units per unit of time.
Workflow automation
Software routes and tracks the work; humans perform the steps.
Robotic process automation (RPA)
Software performs rule-following clerical steps itself.
AI agent
Software given a goal rather than a script; plans and executes steps across systems. Requires a mapped, measured process to be trusted.
Business process reengineering
Redesigning a process from zero around the outcome, rather than tuning its current shape.
Operational transparency
Deliberately exposing internal process stages to the customer.
Process instrumentation
Capturing timestamped events at each step so the process becomes measurable.
Feedback loop
A path by which process output re-enters as input, enabling correction.
Omnichannel
Many customer-facing surfaces reaching one underlying process and one record of the order.
Data pipeline
An automated route that moves data from where it is produced to where it is analyzed.
Business process redesign
Rethinking a process end to end rather than optimizing steps in place.

11Discussion questions

None of these get handed in. On Wednesday you run the discussion and I steer it, which means the class is only as good as what you walk in with. Read the chapter, pick the question you have the strongest opinion about, and be ready to say it first.

  1. Map the Domino's order-to-delivery process as it stood in 2008. Mark every handoff and identify the bottleneck. Warm-up
  2. The Pizza Tracker launched in 2008 and sales kept falling. State, in one sentence, the general principle this illustrates about technology and process.
  3. What data must a store capture for the tracker to display a stage? List the events, then name one management question that data could answer which has nothing to do with customers.
  4. The thirty-minute guarantee made delivery time the one number the company managed to. Propose a replacement set of two or three metrics that would have caught the quality collapse, and explain what each one would have degraded instead, because every metric set has a blind spot.
  5. Domino's admitted publicly that its product was bad. Under what conditions is that a rational business strategy rather than a reckless one? Name a company for which it would clearly be the wrong move. Hard
  6. Operational transparency reduces perceived wait without reducing actual wait. Is that manipulation or service? Argue one side, then state the strongest objection to your own position. Hard
  7. Suppose the recipe had never changed and only the digital ordering had improved from 2009 to 2013. Sketch what the sales curve looks like and justify your shape from the evidence in this chapter. Open judgment
  8. AnyWare lets people order by tweeting a pizza emoji. Argue that this is a serious business capability rather than a marketing stunt, using the idea that every surface reaches one process. Then argue the opposite.
  9. An AI agent will execute a broken process with perfect diligence at machine speed. Using the as-is map you drew in this chapter, name one step in the 2008 Domino’s process you would never hand to an agent, and one step in the 2013 process you would. What changed? Systems
