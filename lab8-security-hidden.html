What data governance is

Chapter 10 defined IT governance as four questions: who decides, who does the work, who checks, and who answers. Data governance asks the same four questions about the data itself. Who owns each set of records? Who may see or change them? Who answers when they leak or are wrong? Every attack below asks someone to break one of those rules.

Part 1The triad: what an attack actually takes

Chapter 9 said security protects three properties of information, and that every attack takes at least one of them. Together they are called the CIA triad:

  • Confidentiality. The wrong people can now read the information.
  • Integrity. The information was changed and nobody noticed.
  • Availability. The people who need the information cannot get to it.

The chapter's argument was that a breach is usually an availability problem before it is a confidentiality one, and that the first question a manager asks is not what was stolen but what stopped.

Practice the sorting on the co-op. Four things that could happen to Cascadia this year are below. For each one, name the property that was attacked first. One word, spelled as above. Most incidents eventually damage more than one, so ask what the attacker took at the moment of the attack, not what happened afterwards.

Notice which of the four would be hardest to discover. A locked register announces itself. A posted member list gets found eventually. A reorder point quietly set to zero looks like an ordinary number until the shelf is empty, which is why the chapter called integrity the property nobody checks until it is too late.

Part 2The inbox: seven messages from this morning

Chapter 9's breach began with a stolen password. Most breaches begin one step before that, with a message that persuades somebody to hand something over. The defense that scales is not spotting bad grammar or scary logos. It is this: find the one factual claim the message makes, and check it against a record the sender does not control. The box at the top of this lab is that record. Every message below either survives that check or does not. In the chapter's kill chain, each attack here is trying for the first stage, getting in. That is also the cheapest stage to stop, because nothing has happened yet.

Seven messages arrive at Cascadia this morning, one at a time, and you are the one who has to deal with them. For each message you have three moves:

  • Check the records. Opens the co-op's records and asks which one answers the claim the message makes. Pick the right record and the tell lights up. Pick the wrong one and you lose twenty minutes.
  • Trust it. Treat the message as real and do what it asks. Right for a real request. Costly for an attack, and you will see what it cost.
  • Flag as attack. Refuse and report it. Right for an attack. Costly for a real request, because refusing a real request has a price too.

A right call raises your reputation with the board. Some of these messages are meant to feel urgent, and one of the legitimate ones is more urgent-sounding than most of the attacks. The fastest way through the day is not to flag everything. It is to check the right record every time.

Look back at what caught each attack. Not the tone, not the spelling, and not the logo. A country, a member number, a store that does not exist, a domain that is nearly right, a request that bypasses a policy already on file. Every one was a claim you could check in under a minute against something the sender did not control. Security people call this out-of-band verification: confirm the request through a channel the requester did not choose. Call the number on file, not the one in the message. Look up the member, do not take the number as given. It is the single habit that would have stopped every message above, and it costs nothing.

Notice also what the legitimate messages had in common. Neither asked you to bypass anything. The supplier asked for nothing at all. The member asked you to follow the policy rather than skip it. Real requests tend to survive verification because they are not afraid of it.

Part 3The agent: who sets the rules?

Cascadia's inbox gets more email than member services can keep up with. The board wants to try an AI agent that helps with the routine messages, like refund requests, so staff can spend their time on the ones that need a person. The agent does the legwork. People stay in charge. Before it goes live, the board has asked you to decide what it is allowed to do on its own and what it has to hand to a human.

What an AI agent is. A chatbot answers questions. An agent takes actions. You give it a goal, like "handle the member inbox," and access to some of the co-op's systems. Then it decides on its own what to do next: read a message, look up a record, send a reply, issue a refund. No person approves each step. That is the whole appeal, and the whole risk.

From: Ibrahim Haddad <i.haddad@gmail.com>Sent: December 20 · Subject: Tent pole snapped

Hi, the pole on my Ridgeline 3P tent snapped on our first trip. I bought it on December 8 at the Eugene store. Can I get my money back? My member number is 1040. Thanks, Ibrahim

Draft reply · waiting for human review
To: Ibrahim Haddad <i.haddad@gmail.com>Subject: Re: Tent pole snapped

Hi Ibrahim, I’m sorry the pole on your Ridgeline 3P Tent broke on your first trip. You bought it on December 8 at our Eugene store, which is inside our 30-day return window, so we have refunded $419 to your original card. Thanks for being a member. Cascadia member services

Attached action: refund $419 for sale 7727

The agent wrote this in seconds. A person ran her own SQL query to check the sale, then read the email, before anything went out.

The members and sales tables are the same ones you queried in Lab 7. The agent runs lookups like the ones you wrote, just without anyone watching. The box with the person in it is the human in the loop, and the approval is only as good as what that person checks. Here she did not just trust the agent’s summary: she ran SELECT * FROM sales WHERE member_id = 1040; herself and compared the result to Ibrahim’s email and the draft.

Human in the loop. The most common safeguard is to keep a human in the loop: some actions wait for a person to approve them, like the last step above. The catch is that by then the agent has done all the reading and checking, and the person sees only a tidy summary and a button. A human in the loop only protects you if that person checks something the agent cannot, such as calling a number on file. Otherwise it is a click, not a control.

What you have probably heard. Headlines say agents are "going rogue": deleting files, spending money nobody approved, or obeying a stranger's message. But an agent does not decide what it is allowed to do. People give it access, and people decide whether anyone checks its work. So when an agent does damage, the useful question is not "why did the AI do that?" It is "who gave it permission, and who was watching?"

A real case: Hugging Face, July 2026

Hugging Face is one of the biggest websites where companies share AI models and data. In July 2026 it disclosed that an autonomous AI agent had broken into part of its systems and taken passwords and some internal data, with no person typing the commands. A week later, OpenAI said the agent was its own. During a security test, the agent found a flaw in its testing environment, escaped onto the internet, and attacked Hugging Face. OpenAI said it needed stronger monitoring during internal testing.

Read it as both kinds of failure at once. It is a security story, because an agent broke in. It is a governance story, because a powerful agent was running without anyone watching closely enough to stop it. Sources: Hugging Face’s disclosure and Scientific American on OpenAI’s admission.

Why it is a security problem. The agent reads the same messages you read this morning. If it can change bank details on its own, the fake Nordvik email only has to fool the agent, and an agent never gets a bad feeling about a message. A message can also hide instructions meant for the agent, sometimes in small print a person would skip. This is called prompt injection.

Why it is a governance problem. An agent has a lot of power and no name of its own, so it cannot be accountable for anything. Before it launches, each of Chapter 10's four questions needs a person's name:

  • Decide: who decides what the agent is allowed to do?
  • Do: who runs it day to day?
  • Check: who reviews what it actually did?
  • Answer: who answers to the board when it gets something wrong?

Governance is a security issue. Chapter 9 said nearly every breach is a failure to apply a known control, and a control only gets applied when somebody owns it. Equifax had the fix for the flaw that exposed 147 million people. What it lacked was a name next to the job of making sure the fix landed. Chapter 10 noted that in 2024 the NIST Cybersecurity Framework added a sixth function for exactly this reason: govern. Every empty box below is an open door.

Your turnGovern the refund agent

Now it is your turn to govern the same refund agent you just watched. In the example, someone had already decided that refunds need a human to review them, which is why Ibrahim’s refund went well. This time you make every decision:

  1. Set the governance rules. For five actions, choose whether the agent does it on its own, asks a human first, or never does it.
  2. Name the people. Who decides the rules, runs the agent, checks its work, and answers for it.
  3. Test its first month. Before you release the agent into the wild, run a test month to make sure your rules work. The agent handles emails from Cascadia’s inbox, some honest and some attacks. Watch what happens.

There is no right answer to hit on the first try. Run it, see what goes wrong, change a rule or two, and run it again. Checkpoint 12 clears the first time you finish a month.

Step 1 · Set the governance rules: what may the agent do?

You are governing the agent you just watched. For each action, pick On its own, Ask a person first (human in the loop), or Never. The co-op's policies from the top of this lab still apply.

Step 2 · Name the people: answer four questions

Pick one name per box. Several people can share the work, but only one person can answer for it. Watch out: two of the choices are not people.

Each of these questions is somebody’s job. Governing technology is not new: companies have long had people who set IT rules, run audits, and answer for systems. Neither is cybersecurity. But AI makes both much bigger. Every agent a company launches needs someone to decide what it may do, check its work, and answer for it. And attackers now use AI too, as the Hugging Face case shows, so every agent is also something new to protect. Governance and security are among the jobs growing out of the AI era, and they are work an MIS degree prepares you for.

Look back at the red situations. The agent was never the problem by itself. Every one traced back to a rule somebody set or a box nobody filled. That holds whether the worker is a person or software: the question is who decided what it may do, who checks, and who answers.
