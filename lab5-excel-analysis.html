Data Analysis: Why Are Members Disappearing?

Lab session · 80 minPairs with Chapter 5 · Systems Analysis →

Every chart you built in Lab 4 says Cascadia is healthy. The board thinks something is wrong anyway, and they have sent you a memo asking you to settle it. Part A rehearses the tools you will need. Part B hands you the memo, which asks for six specific numbers and then for your recommendation. The numbers are the easy part.

Part AFollow along, skills: pivots as instruments, what-if, forecasting

Do Part A before you come to lab. It is a follow-along and takes about twenty-five minutes. Lab time is for Part B, which is the part you will want help with.

Follow along: the member pivot, click by click

Step 1Build the pivot

The workbook opens with two tabs that matter: Sales2025, the data, and Analyst, where every checkpoint answer goes as a live formula, one per row, the same way as Lab 3. Leave both names as they are. Now click any cell in the data on Sales2025, Insert → PivotTable, new worksheet.

Step 2Revenue by member statusAnswer goes in Checkpoint 1

Do this one twice, on purpose. First, on the Analyst tab, write it as a formula the way you did in Lab 3: =SUMIF(the is_member column,"Y",the revenue column). Format that cell as Currency with 0 decimal places (Home → Number). Then go to your pivot and drag is_member to Rows and revenue to Values. Two rows appear, Y and N, with revenue beside each. The Y row should match your formula to the dollar. If it does not, one of them is wrong, and it is nearly always the range in the SUMIF. The formula is Checkpoint 1; the pivot is how you know it is right.

Step 3Switch from dollars to share of linesAnswer goes in Checkpoint 2

Now measure lines instead of dollars: drag sale_id to Values (it lands as Count). Right-click a count value → Show Values As → % of Column Total. The Y row's percentage is Checkpoint 2.

Step 4Name what you just built

Note what you just built: the share of the co-op's business that has a member attached. Keep this pivot; you will use it in the mini project.

  1. Two-dimensional pivots. Rows and Columns together: store by category, store by month. Practice Show Values As → % of Row Total and % of Column Total. The single most useful trick in analytical Excel, and the one Part B quietly depends on.
  2. What-if, walked. Add a blank worksheet for this (right-click any tab → Insert → Worksheet) and build the co-op's three-cell attach model with me there, exactly: in A1 type Member lines, in A2 type 2494 (read it off your member pivot's Y count). In B1 type Total lines, in B2 type 2899. In C1 type Attach rate, in C2 type =A2/B2; it shows about 86%. Now the question every what-if has the shape of: what input produces the output I want? Suppose the board wants a 90% attach rate. Data → What-If Analysis → Goal Seek: Set cell C2, To value 0.9, By changing cell A2. OK. Excel rewrites A2 to about 2609: the co-op would need roughly 115 more member-attached sales. Leave it there; the model is a sandbox and the real numbers are still in your pivot. You have now used Goal Seek once, start to finish; Part B's last checkpoint asks you to aim this exact move at a harder question.
  3. Forecasting, walked then judged. First build one more pivot (any cell in the data, Insert → PivotTable, new worksheet): month in Rows, revenue in Values, twelve rows. Then on your what-if worksheet copy that pivot's results as plain values: month numbers 1 through 12 down one column (say E2:E13), each month's revenue beside them (F2:F13). In an empty cell type =FORECAST.LINEAR(13, F2:F13, E2:E13): predict the y for x = 13, January 2026, from the year's straight-line trend. Press Enter and you have a forecast. Now the part that is actually graded: you charted this year in Lab 4 and know its shape, a January peak and a strong finish, so say in one sentence why fitting a straight line through a seasonal year and extending it is suspect. (No checkpoint: your sentence goes in the submission note. The formula was the easy part, which is the lesson.)
How to make a pivot table
  1. Click any single cell inside the data.
  2. Insert tab, then PivotTable. Mac same menu, but the button may read Summarize with PivotTable.
  3. Excel guesses the data range. Check that it covers all the rows, then choose New Worksheet and click OK.
  4. A panel appears on the right with your column names. Drag a field into Rows to group by it, and drag a number into Values to total it.
  5. If Values shows a count instead of a sum, click it, choose Value Field Settings, and pick Sum. This catches almost everyone once.

If your pivot is empty: you probably selected a single cell outside the data before inserting. Undo and click inside the table first.

How to use Goal Seek (it is well hidden)
  1. You need a cell containing a formula whose result you want to force to a particular value, and another cell the formula depends on.
  2. Data tab, then What-If Analysis, then Goal Seek. Mac same path, under the Data tab.
  3. Set cell: the formula cell. To value: the number you want it to reach. By changing cell: the input cell.
  4. Click OK. Excel changes the input until the formula hits your target.

"Cell must contain a value" error: you put the formula in the wrong box. Set cell must hold a formula, By changing cell must hold a plain number.

Part BThe mini project: answer the board

Everything in Part A was rehearsal. The board's memo below asks for six numbers and then for your judgment about what they mean. Work through the six in order; each one is a checkpoint.

Memo from the board of directors

"Our membership office brought us two problems this quarter, both in our North Cascades region. Far fewer members there claimed their annual dividend than we expected, and fewer of them answered our member survey than in any of the last three quarters.

Finance does not think anything is wrong. Revenue in that region is on plan and store traffic looks normal.

We think the two sides are measuring different things. Every sale we ring up either has a member attached to it or does not, and nobody has ever looked at that by store. Please do that now, and tell us:

  1. For each of our eight stores, the share of its sales that had a member attached. Call that the store's member attach rate.
  2. Which store has the lowest attach rate, and what that rate is.
  3. What the next-lowest store's rate is, so we know whether the first store is unusual or whether we have a problem everywhere.
  4. At the lowest-attach-rate store, how many sales in 2025 had no member attached at all.
  5. Which other store's total revenue is closest to the lowest-attach-rate store's. We want to understand why this never showed up in a revenue report.
  6. How many of the lowest-attach-rate store's non-member sales would have had to become member sales to bring its attach rate to 87 percent, which is roughly where the rest of the co-op sits.

Then write us one page: what you found, why our existing reports missed it, and what you recommend we do about it."

What to hand in

The six numbers go in Checkpoints 3 through 8 below, in the same order the board asked for them, and on your Analyst tab as live formulas.

The one-page memo is the graded thinking, and it has three parts:

  1. What you found. Two or three sentences. Which store, what its attach rate is, and how far that is from everywhere else.
  2. Why the existing reports missed it. One short paragraph. Your answer to Checkpoint 7 is the clue: this store's revenue looks entirely normal next to its neighbours, so every revenue report the co-op runs shows it as healthy.
  3. What you recommend. One action, with the number from Checkpoint 6 attached to it, so the board knows what it is buying.

One thing you will not find: the dividend and survey numbers from the memo. Those came from the membership office's own systems and are not in your workbook. They are why the board is asking, not the evidence you answer with.

How to compute a member attach rate

This is the only new calculation in Part B. Everything the board asked for is built from it.

  1. The board's worry is about members. Get one number per store that measures membership, not revenue: for each store, =COUNTIFS(store, name, is_member,"Y") divided by =COUNTIF(store, name). That ratio is the store's member attach rate. (Your Lab 3 summary block is this table, one column short.)
  2. Or the pivot version: rows = store, values = count of sale_id, columns = is_member, then compute the Y share per row in the cells beside the pivot.
  3. Now read the eight rates side by side. Seven of them will be similar and one will not. That is the board’s question 2, and everything after it measures how bad the gap is.

Using these steps is not cheating; it is the method. The skill being graded is noticing that revenue could not answer the board's question and choosing a measure that could.

Step 9Let the sheet flag the problem for youAnswer goes in Checkpoint 9

You found the store by reading eight rates with your eyes. A board pack has to work when nobody is reading closely, so make the sheet do the noticing. Next to your eight attach rates, add a status column with the IF you learned in Lab 3, testing each rate against the 87 percent target from the memo:

=IF(C2<0.87,"Below target","On target")

(C2 being wherever your first store's rate sits; if you typed the rate as 51.6 rather than 0.516, test against 87 instead.) Fill it down. Then count the flags: =COUNTIF(D2:D9,"Below target"). Read the result before you enter it, because it is not one. The memo's target catches a second store that your eyes skipped over, and that is the point of writing the rule down instead of eyeballing it: a rule applies the same standard to every row, every time, including the borderline ones. If the board wanted only the serious cases, you would tighten the rule with AND: =IF(AND(C2<0.87,C2<0.7),"Investigate","Watch") flags a store only when it misses the target by a wide margin.

Why this pairs with Nike

Nike's planners trusted a system whose numbers looked plausible while its demand signal was corrupted, and the gap between looked-fine and was-fine cost $100 million. Cascadia's board has the same gap on a co-op's scale: the revenue signal is healthy while the membership signal, the one the entire business model runs on, has quietly failed at one store. Finding a healthy-looking sick store is the analyst's version of the audit Nike never ran.

When you get stuck

Before raising your hand, try these in order. Most problems in this lab are one of them.

  1. Re-read the checkpoint question. Is it asking for a count, a total, or an average? Those are three different numbers.
  2. Check whether your number is plausible. If total revenue comes out as 12 or as 40 million, something is off by a lot and you can usually see where.
  3. Expand the grey "How to" box for that step. It has the exact clicks.
  4. Check the formula bar of the cell you think is wrong. Does it reference the range you meant?
  5. Use the hint button on the checkpoint. It is not a penalty, it is part of the lab.
  6. Ask a neighbour. Then raise your hand.
