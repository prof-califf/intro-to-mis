Chapter 9 · AI, Machine Learning and Bias

Machine Learning: How the Same Idea Built Your Feed and Rejected Her Resume

One evening of swiping teaches a system who you are. Ten years of resumes taught a system that engineers look like men. Those are the same technique, doing the same thing, and the difference between them is not in the mathematics. This chapter is about where the difference actually is, and who is responsible for it.

Open TikTok on a new account and the feed is generic. Within an evening it knows you. That accuracy is machine learning, which also flags fraud before your card clears and folded nearly every known protein. It is also the idea that, pointed at a decade of resumes, taught a hiring engine to prefer men. One technique, both outcomes.

How this chapter runs

Sections 1 and 2 are the concepts: what these systems are, how they learn, and the three ways they go wrong without anyone writing a bad rule. Section 3 puts them to work on three systems built across forty years. Section 4 is the full case, which is really two cases: the feed that learned you and the hiring tool that learned the past. Section 5 is what an organization can actually do about it, which is mostly a management job rather than a technical one.

1What machine learning actually is

Chapter 8 was about choosing the right measure and putting it where decisions get made. This chapter is about what happens when you hand the measuring to a machine that works out its own rules. This section says what that machine is, in plain terms, and why its behavior comes from its data rather than from anyone's intentions. Section 2 is about the three ways it goes wrong.

Locate it in the five components before the vocabulary arrives, because the location is the whole argument. Machine learning is a software component that is almost entirely determined by the data component. Hardware sets what you can afford and networks set who you can serve, and neither decides what the system will conclude. What decides that is the examples you show it, and those were chosen by people.

Which means the weakest component rule lands somewhere uncomfortable here. In earlier chapters a weak component made the system slow, or unreliable, or blind. In this one a weak data component makes the system confidently wrong in a way that looks like an answer, and no amount of quality in the other four corrects it.

Key conceptAI, machine learning, and generative AI

Artificial intelligence is the broad project of making computers do things that seem to require intelligence. For decades the main approach was writing explicit rules. Machine learning (ML), the approach behind nearly everything now called AI, inverts that: instead of writing rules, you show the system many examples and it finds the patterns itself. Generative AI is the newest member of the family. It includes the large language models (LLMs) you have used all quarter under the Lab 1 rules: machine learning trained on so much text that the learned patterns can produce new text, not just score or sort existing things. One family, three generations, and one constant: the system's behavior comes from its training data, not from anyone's stated intentions.

The distinction between rules and learning matters for business because it relocates responsibility. A rule-based system does what someone wrote, and a bad outcome traces to a bad rule someone can find and fix. Nobody at TikTok wrote a rule that says "show this person otter videos"; the preference was learned from behavior, and it lives in millions of model parameters no manager can read. (Nobody at TikTok can read them either. That is not a dodge; it is how the technology works.) That opacity is harmless when the stakes are otters. The rest of this chapter is about what happens as the stakes rise, which is why it is mostly about data, not algorithms.

How a model learns, and what learning buys

Key conceptTraining data, labels, and supervised learning

The workhorse of business ML is supervised learning. You assemble training data, meaning historical examples, and each example carries a label, the outcome you want predicted. The model then adjusts itself until its predictions match the labels. Then comes inference: applying the trained model to new cases. The For You page is a clean specimen. Examples: videos, described by their content and by who engaged with them. Label: did this user keep watching? Inference: run that prediction over candidate videos every time you swipe, and serve the winner. Its sibling, unsupervised learning, finds structure with no labels at all, like clustering the co-op's members into segments nobody predefined.

The lineage of the For You page runs straight through this course's other retail giant. Amazon's "customers who bought this also bought" machinery works the same way, learning from millions of past purchases through a method called collaborative filtering. Its engineers published the core item-to-item method back in 2003. A widely cited McKinsey estimate has put roughly 35 percent of Amazon's sales behind its recommendations. The same learned-pattern machinery drives the demand forecasts that place inventory in warehouses before you order.

And retail is one entry in a long ledger. The same machinery flags credit-card fraud in the milliseconds between swipe and approval, which is why your bank texts you about a suspicious charge before you have left the store. In medicine, models trained on labeled scans have matched specialist performance at detecting diabetic retinopathy and certain cancers in published studies, extending screening to clinics that have no specialist. DeepMind's AlphaFold predicted the three-dimensional structure of essentially every known protein, work that earned a share of the 2024 Nobel Prize in Chemistry and compressed months of laboratory effort per protein into minutes. Machine translation and speech recognition have quietly become accessibility infrastructure for hundreds of millions of people. None of this is hype. It is deployed, audited, and paying for itself, which is why every serious company is asking where learned prediction fits its own operations. Someone with your degree will be in the room for that question.

Before the turn, compress the mechanism into one sentence and keep it: a supervised model is a machine for making the future resemble the labeled past. When the past is what you want more of, that is a superpower. The next two sections are about when it is not.

2Bias, proxies, and feedback loops

There are three ways these systems go wrong without anybody writing a bad instruction, and it is worth having all three named before you meet them in the cases. The examples can carry a skew. The thing you chose to predict can be the wrong thing. And acting on the prediction can change the world that produces the next batch of evidence.

Key conceptTraining-data bias and proxy discrimination

Training-data bias is a skew in the examples that the model faithfully learns and reproduces; the model is not malfunctioning, which is what makes it dangerous. Proxy discrimination is the sequel: remove the forbidden attribute, here gender, and the model finds stand-ins that correlate with it, a word, a college, a verb, a zip code. Amazon could delete the "women's" penalty, but could never certify the absence of every subtler proxy in fifty thousand terms, and that impossibility, not the one embarrassing term, is why the project died.

Two management lessons hide in the ending. First, "the algorithm decided" is never an explanation. A model's decisions are its training data's decisions, laundered through math. Accountability for them belongs to the people who chose the data and shipped the system, which is Chapter 11's distinction between responsibility and accountability arriving a chapter early. Second, Amazon's least celebrated act here was its best one: it tested its own system, found the failure, and killed a project it had wanted badly. The discussion questions will ask whether you would have had the nerve.

The feedback loop

Now take the feed from section 1 one step further. The For You page does not just learn from your behavior; it shapes the behavior it will learn from next. Serve what held your attention, attention becomes the data, the data narrows what gets served. In 2021 the Wall Street Journal ran automated accounts through TikTok and showed that watch time alone steered fresh feeds into narrow tunnels within hours. Accounts that lingered on sad content were fed ever darker variants of whatever had held their attention. Engagement is a proxy for satisfaction, the proxy became the target, and Chapter 8 told you the rest. For an individual user this is rabbit holes and lost evenings, worth taking seriously and mostly recoverable.

Raise the stakes and the same structure stops being recoverable. Predictive policing systems forecast where crime is likeliest and route patrols there. More patrols in a neighborhood observe more offenses there, which enters the data as more recorded crime, which raises the forecast, which sends more patrols. The model's prediction manufactures its own confirmation, and historical over-policing hardens into mathematically laundered permanent policy, applied to people who cannot log off.

Key conceptFeedback loops

A feedback loop exists when a system's outputs alter the inputs it will learn from next. Recommendation engines have gentle ones; predictive systems that direct real-world action, patrols, loans, audits, hiring, have consequential ones, because the model changes the world that generates its evidence. The test to carry into any AI deployment: does acting on this prediction change the data that will judge the prediction? If yes, the model can be confidently, self-certifyingly wrong, and the gap between a rabbit hole and a patrol pattern is only the stakes.

3Three systems nobody meant to build

Before the case, three short ones. In every one of them a system produced an outcome that nobody designed, nobody intended, and nobody could find written down anywhere. They are spread across nearly forty years, and the first happened before the phrase "machine learning" was in common use. That is the point. This is not a new problem that arrived with large language models. What differs between them is where the human decision was hiding.

Mini-case 1 · 1988: the admissions program that learned the school's own habits

In 1979 a doctor at St George's Hospital Medical School in London wrote a program to do the first pass of sorting applicants, a job then done by hand across thousands of applications. It was built to agree with the school's own past decisions, and it did. It agreed so closely that its scores matched the human panel's about as well as the panel members matched each other, which everyone took as evidence that it worked.

It was used through the mid-1980s. In 1986 two senior lecturers at the school raised concerns, and in February 1988 the Commission for Racial Equality concluded that St George's had discriminated on racial grounds. The program deducted points from applicants who were women and from applicants whose names did not appear European. As many as sixty people a year were denied an interview on that basis. The school accepted the finding, changed its process, traced applicants who had been wrongly screened out and offered some of them places.

Nobody had written a rule saying reject women. The program had been built to reproduce the school's existing decisions, and the existing decisions had a skew in them, so the skew was what it learned. The report's most quoted line still holds: the prejudice of a computer is no less than that of its designers.

Where the decision was hiding: in the examples. The component: data. Read this beside section 4 and notice how little the story changes in thirty years. Different technology, same mechanism, same surprise.

Check yourselfThe program was judged successful because it agreed with the human panel. Explain why that test could never have caught the problem, and name the test that would have.

Mini-case 2 · 2019: the algorithm that measured the wrong thing

Two patients are equally ill. Software flags one for extra help and not the other, and nobody in the hospital can see why. American hospitals use software to decide which patients with complex conditions should be enrolled in extra care programs. A widely used system worked well by its own measure and was applied to millions of people a year.

In 2019 researchers publishing in Science took it apart and found something subtler than the St George's case. The system did not predict illness. It predicted future healthcare costs, which is a reasonable-sounding stand-in, because sicker people generally cost more. But less money had historically been spent on Black patients at the same level of illness, for reasons that have nothing to do with how ill they were. So at any given predicted cost, Black patients were in fact sicker than white patients, and the system under-referred them. The researchers calculated that correcting the target would have more than doubled the share of Black patients flagged for extra help.

Nothing in the data was falsified and no group was named anywhere in the model. The failure was in the choice of what to predict. Somebody decided that cost would stand in for need. That is Chapter 8's question, whether a proxy tracks the thing you actually care about, arriving with much higher stakes than a batting average.

Where the decision was hiding: in the target. The component: data, at the level of the label. This is the most easily missed failure in the chapter, because it survives every check that asks whether the model predicts its target accurately. It predicted its target very well. The target was wrong.

Check yourselfName the proxy and the thing it was standing in for, then say why someone building this system in good faith would have chosen it. Now propose a target you would have used instead, and name the practical reason nobody did.

Mini-case 3 · 2024: the airline that blamed its own chatbot

A man booking a flight after a death in his family asked Air Canada's website chatbot about bereavement fares. The chatbot told him he could book now and apply for the discount within ninety days. That was wrong; the airline's actual policy did not allow a retroactive claim. He booked on that basis, applied, and was refused.

When he took the case to a Canadian tribunal in 2024, the airline's argument is the one worth remembering. It suggested the chatbot was a separate legal entity responsible for its own actions. The tribunal did not accept this. The chatbot was part of the airline's website, it said, and the airline was responsible for all the information on it. A customer had no way of knowing which part of a company's own website to trust. Air Canada was ordered to pay.

The sums involved were small. The principle is not, and it is the first clear statement many organizations had heard: a company owns what its systems say, in the same way it owns what its staff say.

Where the decision was hiding: in who answers for it. The component: people. No training data caused this and no bias was involved. What failed was the assumption, never written down and never tested, that deploying a model does not transfer any obligation with it.

Check yourselfAir Canada argued the chatbot was responsible for itself. Say why that argument is tempting for a company to make, then name the specific control that would have stopped the chatbot inventing a policy in the first place.

What the three have in common

Three systems, three decades, and not one biased rule between them. Nobody at St George's wrote down that women should score lower. Nobody at the hospitals chose to under-refer Black patients. Nobody at Air Canada instructed the chatbot to invent a refund policy. In every case the machine did faithfully what it had been set up to do.

What differed was where the human judgment sat. In the first it was in the examples the system learned from. In the second it was in the outcome somebody chose to predict. In the third it was in the question of who owns the answer once the system is live. Two of those three are data problems, which is not a coincidence and is the finding this chapter keeps returning to: the interesting decisions in machine learning are almost never about the algorithm.

Keep all three in view for the case, because it contains all three. A model that learned who you are from your own behavior. A model that learned what a good hire looks like from ten years of hiring. And, underneath both, a company deciding how much authority to hand a system it could not read.

4The feed and the hiring desk

TikTok's For You page is the most personally familiar machine-learning system on earth, which makes it the right place to start. There is no channel to subscribe to and no search you must run; the feed simply decides, video by video, what you see next. Its raw material is your behavior: what you watched to the end, what you rewatched, what you skipped in under a second, what you liked, shared, or lingered on at two in the morning. TikTok's own description of the system lists exactly these signals, weighted by how strongly each predicts your interest, with a full watch counting for far more than a casual like. Every swipe is a training example, which is why a feed that starts generic is uncannily yours within an evening.

Treat it as a business case for a moment, because it is a spectacular one. Short video was not a new idea; the recommender was the product. Incumbents with vastly more money and data cloned the format, and TikTok still grew past a billion monthly users. The differentiator was not the video player, which anyone could copy. It was the learned model, which no one could. When Washington later moved to force a sale of TikTok's US operations, the hardest question in the negotiation was who would control the algorithm. That tells you what everyone involved understood the company to be. A learned model as the core asset of a hundred-billion-dollar business: that is what this chapter's technology can do, and your generation's companies will be full of attempts to repeat it.

Hold one Chapter 8 thought as we go: the model does not optimize for your wellbeing or even your taste. It optimizes for engagement, watch time and return visits, because those are measurable proxies for satisfaction. You know from the Moneyball chapter what eventually happens when a proxy becomes the target. Section 2 showed you the loop that follows; section 5 returns to who pays for it.

The turn: the model that scored people

Now the same idea, aimed one domain too far. Beginning in 2014, a team at Amazon built experimental models to do for recruiting what the company had done for retail. The models would read each incoming resume and score the applicant from one to five stars, the way shoppers score products. One insider described the dream to Reuters: give the engine one hundred resumes, it spits out the top five, you hire those. This was not a rogue project by people who misunderstood the company; it was the company's most proven idea, prediction from history, applied to its own front door.

To learn what a strong applicant looked like, the models trained on ten years of the company's past resumes and how those candidacies had turned out. Here is the whole story in one sentence: the tech industry's past applicants were mostly men, so the model learned that resumes resembling men's resumes were what success looked like. By 2015 the team found the system penalizing resumes containing the word "women's", as in "women's chess club captain", and downgrading graduates of two all-women's colleges. It favored verbs that happened to be more common on male engineers' resumes, like "executed" and "captured".

Amazon edited the model to neutralize those terms. Then it confronted the harder truth. With tens of thousands of terms in play, there was no way to be confident the model had not found other, subtler proxies for gender. The company lost confidence and shut the project down, telling Reuters the tool was never used by recruiters to evaluate candidates. Note the shape of the failure before the next section names it: nobody wrote a biased rule. Nobody wrote any rule. The system did exactly what section 1 said supervised learning does, faithfully. The labels were the problem, because the labels were history, and history had a skew.

The model that found your niche by bedtime and the model that rejected her resume are the same idea. Only the training data, and the stakes, differ.

Generative AI at 1.4 billion

Now the current generation, at industrial scale, through a system you have been studying all quarter. In Week 1 Spotify Wrapped was your "what is MIS" specimen; in Lab 7 you learned its core is a GROUP BY. For Wrapped 2025, Spotify added an Archive. For each of roughly 350 million eligible users, rules scanned the year's listening for up to five "remarkable days": your biggest discovery day, your most nostalgic day. Then a language model wrote a short personalized narrative about each one. About 1.4 billion reports, all pre-generated before launch day, each one a call to a model Spotify fine-tuned and distilled to make the cost survivable.

Two engineering choices carry the lesson. First, grounding: every narrative was generated from the user's actual listening records, passed into the prompt, because an ungrounded LLM will cheerfully invent a touching day you never had. That failure mode has a name you met in Lab 1, hallucination, and the calculator test you passed there, the confident wrong total, is the same phenomenon at desk scale. Second, evaluation at volume. No human can read 1.4 billion reports, so Spotify used an LLM as an automated judge on a sample of about 165,000. That is how they caught, among other things, a timezone bug that attributed plays to the wrong day, before users saw it. Their engineers' own summary of the project is the chapter's thesis in one line: at this scale, the LLM call is the easy part. The system around it, the data pipeline, the grounding, the evaluation, the capacity planning, is the achievement, and every word of that sentence describes MIS work, not model-building work.

5What can be done, and who does it

A chapter that stopped at the last section would leave you with a diagnosis and no treatment. That would be a lie by omission. The treatments exist, they are increasingly required by law, and administering them is a business job. They come in two layers: what a company can do to govern its own models, and what the law now demands.

Key conceptAI governance

AI governance is Chapter 11's discipline applied to models: the accountability structure that decides which AI systems exist, what they may decide, and who answers when they fail. Its working parts are concrete. A model inventory with a named owner for every deployed model, because Equifax taught you what "everyone was responsible" means. Bias audits before deployment: testing whether outcomes differ across groups, the exact test that caught Amazon's tool. Documentation, in industry practice called model cards and datasheets: what the model was trained on, what it is for, and where it must not be used. Human-in-the-loop rules stating which decisions a model may never make alone. And ongoing monitoring, because section 2 showed you that a model can quietly change the world it is being graded on. None of these require writing code. All of them require someone with the authority to say no. That is why the last mechanism is the one Amazon actually used: a kill decision, made by management, against a system the company wanted.

You have been running a miniature version of this all quarter. The Lab 1 traffic lights are an AI use policy: an inventory of uses, a rule for which ones require your own judgment in the loop, and a red tier that is simply banned. Scale that reasoning to an organization and you have written the first page of a real governance program.

The law has begun demanding the same mechanisms, and the clearest example descends directly from this chapter's case. New York City's Local Law 144, in force since 2023, requires an annual independent bias audit of any automated hiring tool used on city candidates. The results must be published, and candidates must be told that an algorithm is screening them. Read that against the Monday activity in section 6: the measurable-condition habit, written into municipal law. Illinois requires consent before AI analyzes video interviews. Federal regulators said the broader point bluntly in 2023 guidance. The guidance has since been withdrawn, but the law underneath it has not: existing employment-discrimination law applies whether a human or a model does the discriminating. "The algorithm did it" is not a legal defense any more than it was an ethical one. And in Europe, the GDPR has long given individuals rights against consequential decisions made solely by machine.

The most comprehensive attempt is the European Union's AI Act, adopted in 2024, which regulates by risk tier rather than by technology. Some uses are banned outright, such as social scoring of citizens. High-risk uses, and hiring sits explicitly on that list alongside credit and law enforcement, are permitted only with documentation, human oversight, and accuracy and bias testing: roughly the governance checklist above, made mandatory. Lower tiers carry lighter duties, like disclosing that a customer is talking to a chatbot. Even that law bent under its own weight. In July 2026 the EU pushed the high-risk obligations, hiring tools included, from August 2026 to December 2027, because the standards and regulators needed to enforce them were not ready. The United States has so far taken the opposite architecture: no comprehensive federal law, a voluntary framework from NIST that many companies adopt, sector-by-sector enforcement, and a patchwork of state laws. Colorado passed the first broad statute against algorithmic discrimination in 2024, modeled on the European approach. It never took effect. In December 2025 an executive order directed the Justice Department to challenge state AI laws. An AI company sued Colorado, the federal government joined the suit, and a court paused enforcement. In May 2026 Colorado repealed the law and replaced it with a narrower disclosure statute due to start in 2027.

Which architecture is right is a live design debate, and it is worth having with Chapter 3's vocabulary. The European approach buys protection up front, and is criticized for slowing deployment and burdening small firms. The American approach preserves speed, and is criticized for arriving only after the harm, one lawsuit at a time. And both architectures spent 2026 retreating: Europe delayed its rules and Colorado withdrew its own. Underneath both sit two honest open problems. The pacing problem: legislation moves in years while model generations move in months, so any specific technical rule is born aging, and, as 2026 showed, may be delayed or repealed before it ever applies. And audit quality: a bias audit is only as good as its auditor and its access, and a rubber-stamp industry would launder exactly the harms it was created to catch. The durable answer, in both architectures, is the pair of skills this course keeps handing you: measurement that is valid, from Chapter 8, and accountability that has a name attached, from Chapter 11. Regulation, at its best, is those two things with legal force.

What business people do with AI

As with security in Chapter 10, students assume AI jobs are engineering jobs they are unqualified for, and as with security, the decisions that determine outcomes are mostly business decisions. The roles a business graduate actually fills:

Evaluation and audit. Somebody decided to test whether the resume tool was gender-neutral, and somebody designed Spotify's judge-and-sample scheme. Deciding what "working correctly" means for a model, and how you would know, is analytical work built on exactly the measurement-validity thinking of Chapter 8: an unmeasured model is an unmanaged one.

Human-in-the-loop design. Deciding which decisions a model may make alone, which require a human to confirm, and which a model may only inform. You have run this discipline on yourself since Lab 1; designing it for an organization is the same three lights, drawn as policy.

AI governance and compliance. Someone has to run section 5's machinery: keep the model inventory current, commission and read the bias audits, map each deployment against the EU's risk tiers and the state-law patchwork, and hold the kill authority. It is compliance work with a hard legal edge, not a philosophy seminar, and it is hiring now.

Vendor and use-case selection. Most companies will buy AI, not build it, which makes "which model, whose cloud, at what cost, for which process" a procurement-and-strategy question, one Wednesday's chapter prices out in detail.

The other side: the people in the data

Every system in this chapter runs on data generated by people who never signed up to be training examples, and one of them, the feed, pays for itself with the attention it harvests. The applicants in Amazon's ten years of resumes were job-seekers, not volunteers for an experiment that would score people like them. The residents of a heavily patrolled neighborhood never agreed to have their arrest records forecast their children's police coverage. Spotify's Archive drew on years of retained listening history, and thoughtful engineering coverage of the launch raised exactly that question: delight built on how much a platform remembers about you. This is Chapter 11's "data about people who never agreed", resurfacing one layer up the stack.

Behind the models sits labor the demos never show: data-labeling workers, often contractors in lower-wage countries, tagging the examples supervised learning requires, including the disturbing content that moderation models train on. And generative AI produces its own pollution for other people to absorb. Spotify itself reported removing some 75 million spam and AI-generated tracks in 2025 to protect artists and royalties: the same company using LLMs as a feature while fighting them as an infestation. When a company weighs an AI deployment, most of these costs sit off its books. That is an externality, and it is the same reason Chapter 10 gave for why security cannot be left to private cost-benefit math alone.

One more cost sits behind every system in this chapter, and it is physical. Training a large model and then serving it to hundreds of millions of people consumes serious electricity, and the data centers doing the work drink water to stay cool. The AI boom is a major force behind the current wave of data-center construction, with the bills, the emissions, and the siting fights that come with it. This chapter names the cost and deliberately stops there, because Wednesday's session exists to price it properly: energy, water, emissions, and e-waste, from Netflix's cloud to the model calls you made this quarter. Carry one question across the two days: when the For You page or a chatbot answer feels free, who is paying, and in what currency?

Key takeaways
  • A supervised model is a machine for making the future resemble the labeled past. When the past is what you want more of, that is a superpower.
  • A model's decisions are its training data's decisions, laundered through math. Accountability belongs to the people who chose the data and shipped the system.
  • Remove the forbidden attribute and the model finds stand-ins. Amazon could delete one word; it could not certify the absence of every subtler proxy.
  • If acting on a prediction changes the data that will judge the prediction, the model can be confidently, self-certifyingly wrong.
  • Governance is a business job: keep the inventory, commission the audits, decide what the model may never do alone, and hold the authority to say no.

6Vocabulary

Artificial intelligence
Computers doing tasks that seem to require intelligence; today, mostly via machine learning.
Machine learning
Learning patterns from examples instead of following hand-written rules.
Training data / label
The historical examples a model learns from, and the outcome attached to each.
Supervised / unsupervised
Learning from labeled outcomes vs. finding structure with no labels.
Inference
Using the trained model on new cases; where models earn their keep.
Collaborative filtering
Predicting what you will want from patterns across everyone's past behavior; the recommendation engine's core.
Training-data bias
A skew in the examples, faithfully learned and reproduced.
Proxy discrimination
The model rediscovering a removed attribute through its correlates.
Feedback loop
Outputs that shape the future training data, letting a model confirm itself.
Generative AI / LLM
Models that produce new content; large language models are the text-generating kind.
Fine-tuning / distillation
Adapting a model to a task, and compressing a large one into a cheaper one that keeps what matters.
Grounding
Tying generated output to verified data so the model narrates reality instead of inventing it.
Hallucination
Fluent, confident output that is simply wrong; Lab 1's calculator test at scale.
AI governance
The accountability structure for models: inventory, audits, documentation, oversight rules, and a named owner with kill authority.
Bias audit
Testing whether a model's outcomes differ across groups; annual and independent by law for NYC hiring tools.
Risk-tier regulation
The EU AI Act's architecture: obligations scale with the stakes of the use, from banned to barely regulated.
Human-in-the-loop
Deciding which model outputs require a person's judgment before they act on the world.
Monday activity · The tool works. Now what?20 minutes

It is 2015 and you run recruiting technology at Amazon. The engine is fast, the top-five lists look plausible, and it would save thousands of screening hours a year. Your ML team has just shown you the finding: it penalizes the word "women's", and they can edit that term out but cannot promise there are no subtler proxies among fifty thousand others.

Kill it, fix it, or keep it with a human reviewing every recommendation? Commit to one, and name the single condition that would have to be true for a model to ever touch a hiring decision.

Think 3 min

Choose, and write your condition as a testable sentence, something an auditor could check, not a vibe.

Pair 5 min

Find someone who chose differently. Attack each other's condition: what failure slips through it?

Share 12 min

We will sort the conditions on the board into measurable ones and unmeasurable ones, and notice which pile "a human reviews it" lands in once you ask what the human actually checks.

Where this landsAmazon chose kill, and the reasoning generalizes: a model you cannot evaluate is a model you cannot deploy, whatever it saves. "Human in the loop" is a real answer only when the human has the information, time, and authority to overrule the machine, otherwise it is a rubber stamp with a pulse. The measurable-condition habit is the one to keep; it is Chapter 8's validity question, aimed at AI.
Green · AI as coach and sparring partner

Fitting week for it: have an AI quiz you on this vocabulary, then run one live experiment on the chapter's own subject. Ask it a question about your hometown or a hobby you know deeply, and audit the answer for confident errors. You are performing evaluation, the first job in section 5, on the tool itself. One standing rule extends the red light from Lab 1: never paste real people's personal or identifying data, resumes included, into an AI tool. You would be adding them to somebody's training data, which after this chapter needs no further explanation.

7Discussion questions

None of these get handed in. On Wednesday you run the discussion and I steer it, which means the class is only as good as what you walk in with. Read the chapter, pick the question you have the strongest opinion about, and be ready to say it first.

  1. Amazon's tool learned that resumes resembling men's resumes were what success looked like. Explain to somebody with no technical background why deleting the word "women's" did not fix it, and what would have had to change instead. Analyze
  2. Pick a decision an organization you know makes about people: hiring, admissions, credit, shift assignment. Say what the training data would be if you automated it, and name one skew already present in that history. Apply
  3. The test for a feedback loop is whether acting on a prediction changes the data that will later judge it. Apply that test to two systems in this chapter, one where the answer is no and one where it is yes, and say what a manager should do differently in the second case. Systems
  4. Design the governance for one specific model: name the model, its owner, the decision it may never make alone, the test that would prove it is working, and who holds the authority to switch it off. Five lines, all concrete. Apply
  5. Amazon tested its own tool, found the failure, and killed a project it wanted badly, and nobody outside the company would have known if it had not. Would you have had the nerve, and what would have made it easier? Then say whether that should be left to nerve at all, and what the alternative costs. Ethics
