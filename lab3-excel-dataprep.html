Lab 3 · Week 3

Excel Functions: One Question, One Formula

Lab session · 80 minPairs with Chapter 4 · Business Processes →

Week two of the engagement, and word has gotten around that the new analyst answers emails with exact numbers. Three are waiting this morning. Last week's questions were about everything at once, totals and averages of whole columns; this week's are sharper: this store, that category, members only. Excel has a family of functions built for exactly that shape, and they all work the same way: which column to look in, what to look for, what to do with the rows that match. Part A answers the first email together, keystroke by keystroke. Part B hands you the other two.

AI use is not allowed in these four labs. That means no formula help, no error explanations, and no AI checking your work. An AI tool can produce accurate responses to this lab in seconds, which is exactly why it is not allowed: the AI's work never becomes your own.

These four labs build your Excel skills, which are critical in business settings. So, even though it is tedious, this means typing every formula yourself. Step 1Find your two tabsAnswer goes in Checkpoint 1 · cell B5

Open the workbook and look at the tabs along the bottom of the window. The Analyst tab is where your answers go, laid out the same way as last week: a numbered row for each checkpoint, your answer in column B, room for a note in column C. Checkpoint 1 goes in B5, Checkpoint 2 in B6, and so on down to Checkpoint 10 in B14.

The Sales2025 tab holds the data: every 2025 sale line, one per row. Click that tab now. Press Ctrl + End on Windows, or ⌘ + Fn + → on a Mac. Excel jumps you to the last cell that holds anything, and the Name Box in the top left tells you where you landed.

What you just learned: the sheet ends at row 2900, and row 1 is the header, so there are 2,899 sales in front of you. Knowing that number is how you will catch a formula that quietly went wrong later. If a count comes back larger than 2,899, the formula is counting something it should not be.

Step 2Her first number, with COUNTIFAnswer goes in Checkpoint 2 · cell B6

The manager wants a count of Bellingham's sales. Build this formula by clicking rather than typing the whole thing. Clicking is how you will write formulas once tab names get long, and it is how you avoid misspelling a sheet reference.

  1. Go to the Analyst tab and click cell B6.
  2. Type =COUNTIF( and stop. Do not press Enter yet.
  3. Click the Sales2025 tab at the bottom of the window. Excel follows you there and holds your formula open.
  4. Click the letter C at the top of the store column. The whole column highlights, and Excel writes the reference for you: Sales2025!C:C.
  5. Type ,"Bellingham") and press Enter. Excel returns you to the Analyst tab with the answer sitting in B6.

Your finished formula reads =COUNTIF(Sales2025!C:C,"Bellingham").

What the formula is doing: COUNTIF reads down column C of the Sales2025 tab one row at a time. Each time a cell says exactly Bellingham, it adds one to a running count, and every other row is skipped. Bellingham is inside quotation marks because it is text rather than a number. Notice what COUNTIF does not do: it never looks at revenue or any other column. It only counts rows.

The Sales2025! in front of C:C is the tab name. It is there because your formula lives on the Analyst tab and the data lives somewhere else, so Excel has to be told which sheet to read. Clicking the tab put it there for you.

Step 3What a typo looks likeAnswer goes in Checkpoint 3 · cell B7

Before you trust a formula, find out how it fails. In B7, type this exactly as printed, misspelling and all:

=COUNTIF(Sales2025!C:C,"Portland Parl")

Press Enter. Excel does not warn you. Nothing turns red, no error code appears. You get a number back, and the number is wrong.

What the formula is doing: COUNTIF matches text exactly. There is no store called Portland Parl, so no row matches, so the count is zero. A misspelled word, a trailing space, or Bellingham typed as Bellingahm all produce the same quiet result. This is the most common error in this lab and the hardest to notice, because a zero looks like a real answer instead of a broken formula.

Fix the spelling to "Portland Pearl" for a moment and watch the number change, then put the misspelling back. Row 7 is the one place in this workbook where a wrong formula is the right answer. In C7, write a few words about what happened.

Step 4Her second number, with SUMIFAnswer goes in Checkpoint 4 · cell B8

Her second question is not how many, it is how much: total revenue at Portland Pearl. Counting rows will not answer that. You need to add up a column, but only for the rows that match.

  1. On the Analyst tab, click cell B8.
  2. Type =SUMIF(.
  3. Click the Sales2025 tab, then click the letter C to select the store column.
  4. Type ,"Portland Pearl", and include both commas.
  5. Click the letter K to select the revenue column.
  6. Type ) and press Enter.

Your finished formula reads =SUMIF(Sales2025!C:C,"Portland Pearl",Sales2025!K:K).

What the formula is doing: SUMIF needs three pieces of information where COUNTIF needed two. Column C is where to look. "Portland Pearl" is what to look for. Column K is what to add up. For every row where column C says Portland Pearl, Excel takes that row's revenue out of column K and adds it to a running total. Rows belonging to other stores contribute nothing at all. Her email is now answered.

Those two formulas are the pattern for the rest of the lab. Every function below changes one thing at a time: what happens to the matching rows, or how many conditions a row has to meet before it counts.

FunctionThe question it answersWhat goes inside the parentheses
COUNTIFHow many rows match?where to look, what to match
SUMIFAdd up a column, only for matching rowswhere to look, what to match, what to add up
AVERAGEIFAverage a column, only for matching rowswhere to look, what to match, what to average
COUNTIFSHow many rows meet two or more conditions?where to look, what to match — repeated for each condition
SUMIFSAdd up a column, for rows meeting two or more conditionswhat to add up first, then where to look and what to match for each condition

Read the last row of that table twice. SUMIFS moves the column you are adding up from last position to first. It is the single most common place students lose ten minutes in this lab.

If a formula misbehaves
  1. A zero you did not expect: the text you typed does not match the data exactly. Check the spelling against the Sales2025 tab, and check that your quotation marks are straight (") rather than curly. This is Step 3 happening to you for real.
  2. #NAME?: the function name is misspelled, or the tab name in front of the column is wrong. It is Sales2025, with no space and no underscore. Click the tab instead of typing it.
  3. #VALUE! in SUMIFS: the column you are adding up is probably not first. The S versions reorder the arguments; the table above shows where everything goes.
  4. A count larger than 2,899: your formula is matching more than it should, or is pointed at the wrong column.

Part BNow you: two emails, six formulas, on your own

The numbering keeps going. Steps 5 through 10, checkpoints 5 through 10, rows 9 through 14 on the Analyst tab. Each task names the function to use, the column to look in, and the text to match. You write the formula. Build them by clicking the way you did in Part A; the tab reference is far easier to get right that way than by typing it.

Step 5Planner: how many Snow sales?Answer goes in Checkpoint 5 · cell B9

In B9, write a COUNTIF that looks in the category column (F) and matches "Snow". Same shape as Step 2, different column and different text.

Step 6Planner: average revenue on a Snow saleAnswer goes in Checkpoint 6 · cell B10

In B10, write an AVERAGEIF that looks in the category column (F) for "Snow" and averages the revenue column (K). Three pieces of information, in the same order as Step 4's SUMIF. The only change is what Excel does with the rows that match: it averages them instead of adding them.

Step 7Planner: Snow revenue at the Seattle FlagshipAnswer goes in Checkpoint 7 · cell B11

Two conditions this time, so the function gains an S. In B11, write a SUMIFS with the revenue column (K) first, then category (F) matching "Snow", then store (C) matching "Seattle Flagship". A row has to satisfy both conditions to be included. Your answer should be smaller than Step 6's total for all Snow sales everywhere; if it is not, check your argument order.

Step 8Coordinator: Tacoma's member salesAnswer goes in Checkpoint 8 · cell B12

In B12, write a COUNTIFS with two conditions: the store column (C) matching "Tacoma", and the is_member column (N) matching "Y". COUNTIFS has nothing to add up, so it is only pairs of where-to-look and what-to-match.

Step 9Coordinator: total revenue, every storeAnswer goes in Checkpoint 9 · cell B13

The coordinator's second question asks for a share: Portland Pearl's revenue as a percentage of the whole co-op's. A share is one number divided by another, so build the second number first and give it a cell of its own.

In B13, write a plain =SUM of the entire revenue column (K) on the Sales2025 tab. No condition and no matching; every row counts.

Why this gets its own cell: you now have a total you can look at and sanity check on its own, and one you can point other formulas at. When the next email asks for a different store's share, you reuse this cell instead of rebuilding the total inside a longer formula. Long formulas that compute several things at once are hard to check and harder to fix.

Step 10Coordinator: Portland Pearl's shareAnswer goes in Checkpoint 10 · cell B14

In B14, divide your Step 4 answer by your Step 9 answer by pointing at the two cells: =B8/B13. Click the cells rather than retyping the formulas inside them.

The result arrives as a decimal, something like 0.175. Do not multiply it by 100. Format the cell as a percentage instead. With B14 selected, go to the Home tab, click the % button in the Number group, then click Increase Decimal once so one decimal place shows.

Why formatting instead of arithmetic: the cell still holds the true ratio. Formatting changes how the number is displayed, not what it is. Multiplying by 100 changes the value itself into something you can no longer safely divide, average, or chart alongside other ratios, and anyone who later builds on your cell inherits the error. Getting the number right and getting it readable are two separate jobs, and formatting is how a spreadsheet does the second one without breaking the first.
