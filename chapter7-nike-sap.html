Days before announcing that quarterly earnings would miss by at least a quarter, Nike's chairman Phil Knight summed up his company's new supply chain software on a conference call: "This is what you get for $400 million, huh?" What Nike got was a warehouse arrangement of too many of the wrong sneakers, too few of the right ones, and a nine-figure lesson in what an enterprise system actually is.

01The setup

By the late 1990s Nike was a global company still running on systems it had outgrown. The company had 27 different ordering systems scattered around the world, planning cycles of nine months or longer, and product that reached stores too early or too late: rarely on time. The answer was the $400 million Nike Supply Chain project: SAP for ERP, Siebel for customer tools, and first out of the gate, predictive demand-planning software from i2 Technologies, with a goal of cutting lead times from nine months to six.

Note the ambition's shape. Nike was not buying a program. It was replacing the systems that connected the whole company: how orders, forecasts, factories, and finances talk to each other. That is what this chapter's vocabulary is for.

02What enterprise systems are

Key conceptERP, CRM, and SCM

An ERP (enterprise resource planning) system is one shared database and one set of processes for the whole company: finance, orders, inventory, and manufacturing reading the same numbers. CRM (customer relationship management) does the same for everything customer-facing; SCM (supply chain management) for the flow of materials from suppliers to shelves. The point of all three is the same: replace departmental versions of the truth with one version, so decisions in one corner of the company are visible in every other.

An enterprise system is software that runs a company's core operations on a single shared database, so that a sale, a purchase, and a paycheck are all facts in one place instead of copies in twenty. Three families dominate:

  • ERP (Enterprise Resource Planning), the spine: finance, inventory, orders, manufacturing, HR. SAP and Oracle are the giants.
  • CRM (Customer Relationship Management), everything about customers: contacts, pipeline, service history. Salesforce is the archetype.
  • SCM (Supply Chain Management), demand forecasting, procurement, logistics; the category i2 led in 2000.

Why do these systems exist at all? Because organizations grow into information silos: each department buys or builds its own system, each with its own copy of the truth, and soon the same customer exists three times with three addresses and the same product has two prices. Nike's 27 ordering systems were 27 silos. An ERP's whole premise is the demolition of silos into one shared database, which is also why implementing one is so violent: it forces every department to surrender its private version of reality.

Two ideas explain most enterprise-system grief. First, configuration versus customization: configuring means setting the switches the vendor built; customizing means rewriting the software to match your habits. Customization feels reasonable and compounds forever. Every upgrade, patch, and integration now has to survive your modifications. Second, process fit: an ERP embeds a definition of how ordering, invoicing, and fulfillment should work. Either you change your process to match the software, or the software to match your process. Both are expensive; only one is expensive forever.

Why "big bang" keeps appearing in this course

You met the rollout taxonomy in Chapter 5: direct cutover, parallel, phased, pilot. Enterprise systems raise the stakes on that choice, because the system being replaced is the one currently running the company. A failed website launch loses enrollments. A failed ERP cutover can mean the company temporarily cannot ship, bill, or plan, while the old system it might have fallen back to is already gone.

03The promise, and the shortcuts

i2's demand planner promised something very appealing: feed it history, and it predicts what to make. Nike wanted the prediction fine-grained, forecasts by style, color, and size, which meant thousands of forecasts generated quickly against shifting consumer preferences, and pushed the product past its design. i2 had warned Nike to keep customization within 10–15%; Nike, having spent $400 million, felt entitled to modify the software to accommodate its own messy data. The application was never designed for that. It became unstable, deleted orders, slowed down, and the custom features made bugs harder to trace. A Gartner analyst noted Nike stood up the system in about a year: roughly half the customary time for a deployment of that scale.

And the rollout? A big-bang cutover across the U.S. and European divisions, replacing the spreadsheets and legacy tools at once. Compressed timeline, deep customization, single-instance data problems, no staged fallback. Every element of the Chapter 5 pattern, now aimed at a system that decides what physically gets manufactured.

04Where it broke

Key conceptThe bullwhip effect

Small changes in real demand amplify as they travel up a supply chain, because each link adds its own safety margin to a number that was already padded. A store that sells 100 orders 110 to be safe; the distributor orders 125; the factory tools for 150. Sharing actual demand data across links is the only cure, which is what enterprise systems promise. Nike’s failure ran the amplifier in software: bad forecasts propagated across every store and factory at once, at machine speed. Automation did not create the bullwhip. It removed the humans who would have questioned it.

The system went live and began quietly corrupting the one thing it existed to produce: the demand signal. Forecasts for products and locations came out overestimated or underestimated. Orders duplicated. Orders vanished. Factories in Asia built to those numbers anyway. The result arrived in warehouses months later as physical fact: too much of the slow-selling models the system had over-ordered, and too little of the high-demand product it had under-ordered. Revenue took the hit from both directions at once.

Supply chain theory has a name for what happened next: the bullwhip effect. Small errors in the demand signal amplify as they travel upstream: a store over-orders slightly, the distributor over-orders more to be safe, the factory over-produces more still, each layer adding its own safety margin to a number that was already wrong. It happens with humans and fax machines. Nike ran it with software, across every store and factory at once, at machine speed. Automation did not create the bullwhip. It cracked the whip harder.

Sit with the mechanism, because it is the chapter's core. Nothing looked broken. The software produced numbers; the numbers were plausible; planners planned. The failure was invisible at decision time and undeniable only at delivery time: months of manufacturing lead later, when the mistake was already cast in rubber and foam. Information systems that feed physical processes have this property: the feedback loop is as long as the supply chain.

05The aftermath

Nike went public on February 27, 2001, and the market's verdict was immediate: the stock fell 20% in a single trading session, erasing roughly $4 billion in market capitalization, with the CFO stating plainly that the demand-planning system was the primary cause of the miss. Class-action lawsuits followed, and Nike and i2 blamed each other, i2's own stock dropped over 22% on the news. A useful detail for your careers: the announcement happened partly because it had to. Nike was obligated to report the shortfall to stakeholders to avoid SEC repercussions, material system failures at public companies become disclosure events, which is why CFOs and general counsels now sit in ERP steering meetings.

06The recovery

The fix was not sophisticated. Nike built manual workarounds: programmers downloaded data from i2's demand predictor and reloaded it into the supply chain planner weekly, and by fall the software glitches were resolved. i2 was folded into the larger SAP ERP system by spring 2001, repointed at orders and invoices rather than predictive modeling. And here is the part most retellings skip: Nike did not abandon the project. The company held to its single-instance strategy, kept the same project leadership in place, and pressed on, because the business case for the overall supply chain program still cleared the cost of the disaster. Pre-building of shoes fell from about 30% of manufacturing units toward 3%, and lead times moved from nine months toward six. The failure was real and so, eventually, was the return. Both facts belong in your analysis.

07Modern coda: it still happens: close to home

It would be comforting to file Nike under "early-2000s growing pains." The record refuses. Consider a company you can drive to from Bellingham in four hours: Lamb Weston, the frozen-potato giant whose Columbia Basin plants process more than 11 billion potatoes a year into french fries for restaurants worldwide.

  • Nov 2023Lamb Weston takes SAP S/4HANA live across its North American operations, replacing a decades-old legacy system, warehouse integrations included.
  • Jan 2024The CFO characterizes the issues as "usual bumps" with no expected material impact: a statement later central to securities litigation.
  • Apr 2024Q3 results reveal the damage: $135 million in lost sales in a single quarter. The stock collapses 19.4%.
  • Jun 2024Securities class actions filed, alleging misrepresentations about ERP readiness.
  • Oct 2024A new CIO is appointed, and the strategy is revised from simultaneous multi-site deployment to sequential, plant-by-plant rollout: nearly a year after go-live.

Read that last line against Chapter 5's rollout taxonomy. The remediation for a failed big bang was to adopt, retroactively, the phased rollout that was available all along. Twenty-three years after Nike, with the pattern taught in every business school on earth.

Nor is Lamb Weston alone in its cohort. Zimmer Biomet, an $8 billion medical-device maker, sued its implementation partner Deloitte for $172 million over an SAP S/4HANA rollout that consolidated nine legacy ERPs and went live on July 4, 2024 after repeated postponements, a suit filed in September 2025 calling the rollout a "disaster," after the company had told investors in 2024 that the impact was roughly 1% of revenue. The pattern the litigation record shows (operational failure, underplayed disclosure, stock drop, securities suit) also ran through Revlon's 2018 SAP disaster and Lamb Weston itself. The technology has improved beyond recognition since 2001. The organizational failure modes have not moved an inch, which tells you where the failure actually lives.

The pattern

Nike, Lamb Weston, Zimmer Biomet, Revlon: different decades, industries, and vendors, and the same failure each time. Compressed timelines. Big-bang cutovers. Master data not ready. Optimistic status reporting right up until the quarter that couldn't be reported optimistically. When a pattern survives 25 years of better software, the software was never the variable.

08The other side: who absorbs a failed rollout

The $100 million Nike lost is the number the case is named for, and it is the shallowest measure of the damage. Follow the failure outward. Factories in Asia received orders for shoes nobody wanted and cancellations for shoes they had already made; the workers on those lines absorbed the whiplash as overtime followed by idled shifts. Retailers were shipped the wrong mix and marked down the excess. Inside Nike, planners spent a year manually overriding a system they had been told to trust, doing two jobs for one salary. When Lamb Weston’s rollout stumbled in 2023, the shortfalls landed on restaurant customers and on warehouse crews reconciling inventory by hand. ERP failures are always narrated as money and stock price, because those are the measurable components. The five components framework insists on the fifth: somewhere under every failed implementation is a person doing manually, under pressure, what the software was supposed to do, and they are rarely in the case study.

09The case on the five components

The Five Components · Nike, 2000–2001
Hardware

Servers and factory systems performed exactly as specified.

Software

i2 was capable inside its design envelope, and customized far past it: unstable, dropping orders.

Data

Messy legacy data fed in raw; the demand signal corrupted at the source.

Networks

Factory links across Asia delivered the bad numbers flawlessly, which made it worse.

People

Planners trusted plausible numbers; training and adoption an afterthought on a one-year timeline.

10Vocabulary

Enterprise system
Software running core operations on one shared database across the whole firm.
ERP / CRM / SCM
The resource-planning spine; the customer system; the supply chain system.
Configuration vs. customization
Setting the vendor's switches vs. rewriting the vendor's software. The second compounds forever.
Process fit
The degree to which the software's embedded processes match the organization's real ones.
Information silo
A department-level system holding its own copy of the truth; ERP exists to demolish them.
Bullwhip effect
Small demand-signal errors amplifying upstream as each layer adds safety margin.
Master data
The reference facts (products, prices, customers, locations) every module depends on. Garbage here propagates everywhere.
Single instance
One global system of record instead of regional copies; Nike's strategic bet, kept even after the failure.
Go-live / big bang
The cutover moment; moving everyone at once, with no fallback.
Change management
The people side of implementation: training, adoption, and trust. The line item that gets cut first and billed last.
Demand planning
Forecasting what to make and stock; the module that failed at Nike.
Material disclosure
The obligation of public companies to report events, including system failures, that investors would consider significant.

Every one of these companies could read about the previous failure. Why does this keep happening?

Where this landsThe competence constraint is what makes this work, because it blocks the easy answer. What survives is structural: the people who authorise these projects are rarely the people who ran the last one, vendors sell timelines that assume nothing goes wrong, and the cost of stopping midway is visible while the cost of continuing is not. Land on the idea that organisations do not inherit each other’s scar tissue.

11Discussion questions

  2. Nothing looked broken until warehouses filled with the wrong sneakers. Explain, using the concept of feedback-loop length, why supply chain systems fail differently than websites.
  3. Nike and i2 blamed each other; Zimmer Biomet sued Deloitte. When a customized system fails, how should responsibility divide between the vendor who warned against customization and the customer who paid for it anyway? Take a side and defend it.
