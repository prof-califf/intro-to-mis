Chapter 7 · Enterprise Systems

Enterprise Systems: How Nike Spent $400 Million to Make the Wrong Shoes

Nike replaced twenty-seven ordering systems with one, so that every part of the company would finally read the same numbers. It worked. The numbers were wrong, factories in Asia built to them anyway, and nobody found out until the shoes arrived. This chapter is about what an enterprise system asks of a company, and what happens when the company says no.

Days before announcing that quarterly earnings would fall well short of what Wall Street expected, Nike's chairman Phil Knight summed up his company's new supply chain software on a conference call: "This is what you get for $400 million, huh?" What Nike got was a warehouse full of too many of the wrong sneakers and too few of the right ones.

01What enterprise systems are

Chapter 6 argued that the shape you store facts in decides which questions are cheap to ask. This chapter is about the largest version of that decision a company ever makes: whether every department will keep its own records, or whether the whole company will run on one.

Put it in the five components before the vocabulary arrives. An enterprise system is mostly a data decision wearing a software costume. The software is bought, and what it actually does is impose one structure on records that used to live in twenty places. Which means the people component carries the whole cost: every department that had its own way of recording a customer, an order or a price now has to record it the way the system does. Nobody buys new hardware and calls it a transformation. They buy an ERP and call it one, and they are right, because what is being transformed is not the technology.

Keep the weakest component rule from Chapter 1 in view, because this chapter is where it bites hardest. When one shared database feeds every decision in the company, a fault in the data component is no longer contained in one department. It reaches the factory.

Key conceptERP, CRM, and SCM

An ERP (enterprise resource planning) system is one shared database and one set of processes for the whole company: finance, orders, inventory, and manufacturing reading the same numbers. CRM (customer relationship management) does the same for everything customer-facing; SCM (supply chain management) for the flow of materials from suppliers to shelves. The point of all three is the same: replace departmental versions of the truth with one version, so decisions in one corner of the company are visible in every other.

An enterprise system is software that runs a company's core operations on a single shared database, so that a sale, a purchase, and a paycheck are all facts in one place instead of copies in twenty. Three families dominate:

  • ERP (Enterprise Resource Planning), the spine: finance, inventory, orders, manufacturing, HR. SAP and Oracle are the giants.
  • CRM (Customer Relationship Management), everything about customers: contacts, pipeline, service history. Salesforce is the archetype.
  • SCM (Supply Chain Management), demand forecasting, procurement, logistics; the category i2 led in 2000.

Why do these systems exist at all? Because organizations grow into information silos. Each department buys or builds its own system, each with its own copy of the truth, and soon the same customer exists three times with three addresses and the same product has two prices. Nike's 27 ordering systems were 27 silos. An ERP's whole premise is the demolition of silos into one shared database, which is also why implementing one is so violent: it forces every department to surrender its private version of reality.

02Why they are hard to install

Two ideas explain most enterprise-system grief. (If you end up on an ERP project, and many of you will, these two ideas are what the meetings will be about.) First, configuration versus customization: configuring means setting the switches the vendor built; customizing means rewriting the software to match your habits. Customization feels reasonable and compounds forever. Every upgrade, patch, and integration now has to survive your modifications. Second, process fit: an ERP embeds a definition of how ordering, invoicing, and fulfillment should work. Either you change your process to match the software, or the software to match your process. Both are expensive; only one is expensive forever.

Why "big bang" keeps appearing in this course

You met the rollout taxonomy in Chapter 5: direct cutover, parallel, phased, pilot. Enterprise systems raise the stakes on that choice, because the system being replaced is the one currently running the company. A failed website launch loses enrollments. A failed ERP cutover can mean the company temporarily cannot ship, bill, or plan, while the old system it might have fallen back to is already gone.

Key conceptThe bullwhip effect

Small changes in real demand amplify as they travel up a supply chain, because each link adds its own safety margin to a number that was already padded. A store that sells 100 orders 110 to be safe; the distributor orders 125; the factory tools for 150. Sharing actual demand data across links is the only cure, which is what enterprise systems promise. Nike’s failure ran the amplifier in software: bad forecasts propagated across every store and factory at once, at machine speed. Automation did not create the bullwhip. It removed the humans who would have questioned it.

If the vendor built a setting for it, it is configuration. If somebody has to write or change code to make it happen, it is customization, and it will need to be done again at every upgrade.

03Three companies, three refusals

Before the case, three companies that installed this class of system and were badly hurt by it. An enterprise system makes three demands of the organization that buys it, and each of these companies refused a different one. Read them with the Nike case coming, because Nike managed to refuse all three at once.

Mini-case 1 · 1996: the distributor that could not process its own orders

Imagine a warehouse that used to ship hundreds of thousands of orders a day, now shipping a fraction of that, with pharmacies calling to ask where their medicines are. FoxMeyer was the fourth-largest pharmaceutical distributor in the United States, moving medicines from manufacturers to pharmacies and hospitals at a scale of several billion dollars a year. In the early 1990s it committed to a new ERP system alongside a heavily automated warehouse, a project meant to cut costs enough to serve a very large new customer it had already signed.

The system could not handle the volume. The operation it was replacing processed hundreds of thousands of orders a day, and the new one managed a fraction of that. Orders went unfilled, inventory went missing, and the savings the company had already promised never arrived. FoxMeyer filed for bankruptcy in 1996 and was sold for a small fraction of what it had been worth. It later sued both the software vendor and its implementation consultants, which is how these stories usually end and tells you less than the beginning does.

The demand it refused: be honest about scale. The component: hardware and software. Nobody proved the new system could carry the volume the old one was already carrying, and the company had committed the savings before it had them.

Check yourselfFoxMeyer signed a large contract on the assumption that the new system would deliver savings. Name the phase from Chapter 5 where that assumption should have been tested, and say what specific number would have told them to renegotiate rather than proceed.

Mini-case 2 · 2021 to 2026: eleven million items that did not match

In 2021 a supermarket chain with hundreds of stores was told, in effect, that the systems it ran on belonged to someone else and it had three years to move out. Walmart had sold the British chain Asda and agreed to keep running its computer systems for three years while Asda built its own. Chapter 6 described what those Walmart systems are. Asda had been living inside them, and now had to move out.

The program was called Project Future, and the scale of it is worth stating: separating more than two and a half thousand systems and rebuilding the company's entire enterprise core on SAP. The initial budget was about £189 million. By the end of 2023 the cost had reached about £430 million. By the time the last stores moved in August 2025, the company had spent about £1.2 billion.

The trouble was not really the software. In 2024, part way through the move, an internal incident report showed that Asda's new warehouse system and its new SAP system disagreed about how much stock the company held. The mismatch was about ten million items in June and eleven million in July, worth around £21 million, between two systems that were supposed to be reading the same records. Not eleven million wrong prices, and not a failure of one program: eleven million places where two databases disagreed about what the company owned. The plan was to have the finance team work through the warehouse data and post corrections to stores by hand.

Cutover dates were cancelled in December 2024 and postponed again in January 2025. The last stores and depots moved in August 2025, and shelves went short and the website slowed for months afterwards. In late 2025 the chairman told reporters the problems were self-inflicted, blamed poor integration, insufficient testing and a lack of capacity planning, and said the disruption had set the company's turnaround back about six months. Sales fell while it worked through them, and the 2025 accounts carried a £284 million charge for the disruption alone.

The demand it refused: know what your records actually contain. The component: data. Asda did the rollout carefully, in stages, over years, which is what Nike did not. It was still badly hurt, because a phased rollout protects you from a bad cutover and does nothing at all about records that disagree.

Check yourselfChapter 6 argued that a shared database is worth more than each department keeping its own. Asda's eleven million mismatches were found by a routine reconciliation report and corrected by hand. Say what that report is doing, in Chapter 6's vocabulary. Then name what a company would have to change so that the system refuses a disagreement when it is written, rather than finding it in a report a month later.

Mini-case 3 · 2018: seven years, half a billion euros, and back to the old system

Seven years and half a billion euros into a project, somebody at Lidl had to stand up and recommend switching everything back to the old system. Lidl, the German discount grocery chain, had begun replacing its own merchandise management system with SAP in 2011. SAP publicly named Lidl among its notable customers along the way. In 2018, after seven years and roughly €500 million, Lidl cancelled the project and returned to the system it had been trying to replace.

The reported cause was not a defect. Lidl's business runs on its own way of handling stock and pricing, built up over decades and central to how it competes on cost. The standard software did it differently. Rather than change the practice, Lidl set about changing the software to match it. The modifications spread into the core of the system until what they had was neither the standard product nor something they could maintain.

That is the configuration-versus-customization decision from section 02, taken one reasonable concession at a time across seven years. Notice that no single decision along the way looks obviously wrong. Each one was a company protecting something it was good at.

The demand it refused: change yourself, not the software. The component: people. An enterprise system carries a definition of how the work ought to be done. You may reject that definition, but then you should not buy the system.

Check yourselfSection 02 says only one of the two options is expensive forever. Say which, explain the mechanism, and then name a situation where customizing anyway would be the right business decision. There is one; be specific about what makes it so.

What the three have in common

Three demands, three refusals, and each one landed on a different component. Be honest about scale, which is hardware and software. Know what your records actually contain, which is data. Change yourself rather than the software, which is people. Not one of the three failures was caused by bad code, and in all three the vendor's software worked as designed for the customers who used it as designed.

Notice something else about that list. Every one of the three demands is about the organization rather than the technology, and every one has to be met before the software is switched on. An enterprise system is unusual among purchases in that it requires you to change in order to use it. That is why buying one is a decision about the company rather than about the software, and why the people who approve it will never log in to it.

What follows is a company that refused all three at once, and added a fourth. Nike customized past the point its vendor had warned about, fed the result data from twenty-seven systems it had not cleaned up, and asked the software to work at a scale and speed nobody had tested. Then, on top of all that, it ran a big-bang cutover. Then it manufactured shoes based on what came out.

04Nike: $400 million, huh?

Picture a Nike warehouse in the winter of 2001. One aisle is stacked to the ceiling with a shoe nobody is buying. Two aisles over, the shelf for the shoe every store is begging for is empty, and the factory that makes it is on the other side of the Pacific with a lead time measured in months. Nobody in the building did anything wrong. To see how a company arrives at that aisle, go back three years. By the late 1990s Nike was a global company still running on systems it had outgrown: 27 different ordering systems scattered around the world, planning cycles of nine months or longer, and product that reached stores too early or too late, rarely on time. The answer was the $400 million Nike Supply Chain project: SAP for ERP, Siebel for customer tools, and, first to go live, predictive demand-planning software from i2 Technologies. The goal was to cut lead times from nine months to six.

Note the ambition's shape. Nike was not buying a program. It was replacing the systems that connected the whole company: how orders, forecasts, factories, and finances talk to each other. That is what this chapter's vocabulary is for.

The promise, and the shortcuts

i2's demand planner promised something very appealing: feed it history, and it predicts what to make. Nike wanted the prediction fine-grained: forecasts by style, color, and size. That meant thousands of forecasts generated quickly against shifting consumer preferences, and it pushed the product past its design. i2 had warned Nike to keep customization within 10 to 15 percent. Nike, having spent $400 million, felt entitled to modify the software to accommodate its own messy data. The application was never designed for that. It became unstable, deleted orders, slowed down, and the custom features made bugs harder to trace. A Gartner analyst noted Nike stood up the system in about a year: roughly half the customary time for a deployment of that scale.

And the rollout? A big-bang cutover across the U.S. and European divisions, replacing the spreadsheets and legacy tools at once. Compressed timeline, deep customization, single-instance data problems, no staged fallback. Every element of the Chapter 5 pattern, now aimed at a system that decides what physically gets manufactured.

Where it broke

The system went live and began quietly corrupting the one thing it existed to produce: the demand signal. Forecasts for products and locations came out overestimated or underestimated. Orders duplicated. Orders vanished. Factories in Asia built to those numbers anyway. The result arrived in warehouses months later as physical fact: too much of the slow-selling models the system had over-ordered, and too little of the high-demand product it had under-ordered. Revenue took the hit from both directions at once.

Supply chain theory has a name for what happened next: the bullwhip effect. Small errors in the demand signal amplify as they travel upstream. A store over-orders slightly, the distributor over-orders more to be safe, the factory over-produces more still, and each layer adds its own safety margin to a number that was already wrong. It happens with humans and fax machines. Nike ran it with software, across every store and factory at once, at machine speed. Automation did not create the bullwhip. It cracked the whip harder.

Stay with the mechanism for a moment, because it is the chapter's core. Nothing looked broken. The software produced numbers; the numbers were plausible; planners planned. The failure was invisible at decision time and undeniable only at delivery time: months of manufacturing lead later, when the mistake was already cast in rubber and foam. Information systems that feed physical processes have this property: the feedback loop is as long as the supply chain.

The aftermath

Nike made the problem public on February 27, 2001, and the market's verdict was immediate. The stock fell 20% in a single trading session, erasing several billion dollars in market capitalization, and the CFO said plainly that the demand-planning system was the primary cause of the miss. Class-action lawsuits followed, and Nike and i2 blamed each other, i2's own stock dropped over 22% on the news. A useful detail for your careers: the announcement happened partly because it had to. Nike was obligated to report the shortfall to stakeholders to avoid SEC repercussions, material system failures at public companies become disclosure events, which is why CFOs and general counsels now sit in ERP steering meetings.

The recovery

The fix was not sophisticated. Nike built manual workarounds: programmers downloaded data from i2's demand predictor and reloaded it into the supply chain planner weekly, and by fall the software glitches were resolved. i2 was folded into the larger SAP ERP system by spring 2001, repointed at orders and invoices rather than predictive modeling. And here is the part most retellings skip: Nike did not abandon the project. The company held to its single-instance strategy, kept the same project leadership in place, and pressed on, because the business case for the overall supply chain program still cleared the cost of the disaster. Pre-building of shoes fell from about 30% of manufacturing units toward 3%, and lead times moved from nine months toward six. The failure was real and so, eventually, was the return. Both facts belong in your analysis.

It still happens, and close to home

It would be comforting to file Nike under "early-2000s growing pains." The record refuses. Consider a company you can drive to from Bellingham in four hours: Lamb Weston, the frozen-potato giant whose Columbia Basin plants process more than 11 billion potatoes a year into french fries for restaurants worldwide.

  • Nov 2023Lamb Weston takes SAP S/4HANA live across its North American operations, replacing a decades-old legacy system, warehouse integrations included.
  • Jan 2024The CFO characterizes the issues as "usual bumps" with no expected material impact: a statement later central to securities litigation.
  • Apr 2024Q3 results reveal the damage: $135 million in lost sales in a single quarter. The stock collapses 19.4%.
  • Jun 2024Securities class actions filed, alleging misrepresentations about ERP readiness.
  • Oct 2024A new CIO is appointed, and the strategy is revised from simultaneous multi-site deployment to sequential, plant-by-plant rollout: nearly a year after go-live.
  • May 2026A federal judge lets the securities case proceed against the company and its former CEO and CFO, finding the complaint adequately alleged that the ERP statements were misleading.

Read that last line against Chapter 5's rollout taxonomy. The remediation for a failed big bang was to adopt, retroactively, the phased rollout that was available all along. Twenty-three years after Nike, with the pattern taught in every business school on earth.

Nor is Lamb Weston alone in its cohort. Zimmer Biomet, an $8 billion medical-device maker, sued its implementation partner Deloitte for $172 million over an SAP S/4HANA rollout that consolidated nine legacy ERPs and went live on July 4, 2024, after repeated postponements. The suit, filed in September 2025, called the rollout a "disaster." In 2024 the company had told investors the impact was roughly 1% of revenue. The pattern the litigation record shows (operational failure, underplayed disclosure, stock drop, securities suit) also ran through Revlon's 2018 SAP disaster and Lamb Weston itself. The technology has improved beyond recognition since 2001. The organizational failure modes have not moved an inch, which tells you where the failure actually lives.

The pattern

Nike, Lamb Weston, Zimmer Biomet, Revlon: different decades, industries, and vendors, and the same failure each time. Compressed timelines. Big-bang cutovers. Master data not ready. Optimistic status reporting right up until the quarter that couldn't be reported optimistically. When a pattern survives 25 years of better software, the software was never the variable.

05Who absorbs a failed rollout

The $100 million Nike lost is the number the case is named for, and it is the shallowest measure of the damage. Follow the failure outward. Factories in Asia received orders for shoes nobody wanted and cancellations for shoes they had already made; the workers on those lines absorbed the whiplash as overtime followed by idled shifts. Retailers were shipped the wrong mix and marked down the excess. Inside Nike, planners spent a year manually overriding a system they had been told to trust, doing two jobs for one salary. That is change management arriving as a bill rather than a plan. Change management is the work of getting an organization to actually operate the new way. Either you budget for it in advance, or the people who had no say in it pay for it afterwards in overtime. When Lamb Weston’s rollout stumbled in 2023, the shortfalls landed on restaurant customers and on warehouse crews reconciling inventory by hand. ERP failures are always narrated as money and stock price, because those are the measurable components. The five components framework insists on the fifth: somewhere under every failed implementation is a person doing manually, under pressure, what the software was supposed to do, and they are rarely in the case study.

Key takeaways
  • An enterprise system is a data decision wearing a software costume. Buying one means agreeing that every department will record things the same way.
  • Configuration sets the switches the vendor built. Customization rewrites the software to match your habits, and every later upgrade has to be rewritten too.
  • Integration means one version of the truth becomes mandatory. You gain a company that agrees with itself and lose the ability to be wrong in only one place.
  • Nike's software worked. Its data was too fine-grained for the model, its rollout was too fast, and the errors amplified up the supply chain into a warehouse of the wrong shoes.
  • The cost of a failed rollout is paid by the people who have to change how they record things, in overtime, and by customers who never see the software at all.

06Vocabulary

Enterprise system
Software running core operations on one shared database across the whole firm.
ERP / CRM / SCM
The resource-planning spine; the customer system; the supply chain system.
Configuration versus customization
Setting the vendor's switches vs. rewriting the vendor's software. The second compounds forever.
Process fit
The degree to which the software's embedded processes match the organization's real ones.
Information silo
A department-level system holding its own copy of the truth; ERP exists to demolish them.
Bullwhip effect
Small demand-signal errors amplifying upstream as each layer adds safety margin.
Master data
The reference facts (products, prices, customers, locations) every module depends on. Garbage here propagates everywhere.
Single-instance
One global system of record instead of regional copies; Nike's strategic bet, kept even after the failure.
Go-live / big bang
The cutover moment; moving everyone at once, with no fallback.
Change management
The people side of implementation: training, adoption, and trust. The line item that gets cut first and billed last.
Demand-planning
Forecasting what to make and stock; the module that failed at Nike.
Disclosure
The obligation of public companies to report events, including system failures, that investors would consider significant.

Where this landsThe competence constraint is what makes this work, because it blocks the easy answer. What survives is structural. The people who authorize these projects are rarely the people who ran the last one. Vendors sell timelines that assume nothing goes wrong. And the cost of stopping midway is visible while the cost of continuing is not. Land on the idea that organizations do not inherit each other’s scar tissue.

Then information silos, with Nike's twenty-seven ordering systems as the concrete example. The bridge from Chapter 6 is one sentence, that an enterprise system is what happens when a company insists on one database instead of twenty-seven.

Wednesday session plan · 80 minutes · the Nike case, second half of the Week 7 double
0:00OpenPut Knight's line on the screen and nothing else: "This is what you get for $400 million, huh?" Ask what they think went wrong before anyone has read anything back to them. Most rooms say the software was bad. Write that on the board.
0:06Walk the caseThe setup, the shortcuts, the break. Name the three refusals from Monday as each appears: the compressed timeline, the customization past the vendor's warning, the big bang. Then retire the opening answer, because the software worked inside the envelope it was designed for.
0:20The mechanismThe bullwhip, run in software. Slow down here. Nothing looked broken, the numbers were plausible, the planners planned, and the error was undeniable only at delivery time, months of manufacturing later. Ask how long the feedback loop was, and get to the answer that it was as long as the supply chain.
0:32The recoveryThe part most retellings skip. Nike kept the strategy and the leadership, and the program eventually delivered: pre-building fell sharply, lead times came down. Ask whether that makes the project a success and refuse to settle it for them.
0:40It still happensLamb Weston, four hours from here, in 2023. Walk the timeline and land the last line: the remediation for a failed big bang was to adopt, a year late, the phased rollout available from the start. Then the question that matters, which is why a pattern taught in every business school survives twenty-five years of better software.
0:50ThinkAll five discussion questions on screen. Silent individual work, one sentence on at least two. Protect this step.
0:56PairGroups of three or four settle on positions they can defend. Circulate for a group that landed somewhere unusual on the recovery question or on who absorbs the failure, and call on them first.
1:06ShareGroups take the questions in front of the room. Ask for disagreement rather than answers.
1:16Close and bridgeBack to the opening board: does anyone still think the software was the problem? Then the sentence to leave them with, that when a pattern survives twenty-five years of better software, the software was never the variable. Preview Chapter 8, where a team gets the numbers right and has to fight its own organization to act on them.
