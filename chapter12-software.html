Chapter 12 · Software Design and Selection

Software: The Pizza You Could Not Order

Guillermo Robles is blind and uses software that reads a screen aloud. At least twice he tried to order a pizza from Domino's, on the website and in the app, and could not, because neither had been built to work with a screen reader. He sued, and the case reached the door of the Supreme Court. This chapter is about the two software decisions every business makes: how it is designed and how it is chosen. It is also about why the interface is the part of the system that decides who gets to be a customer.

01Software, and the interface that decides who gets in

Every chapter so far has treated software as one of five components, the one that holds the rules. This chapter looks at it from the only angle a customer ever sees: the surface where a person meets the system. That surface is where the software component touches the people component, and it is where most of a system's value is either delivered or lost.

Two decisions follow, and both belong to managers rather than programmers. The first is how software is designed, meaning what the interface lets a person do and who it lets do it. The second is how software is chosen, because most organizations buy far more of it than they build. The case at the end of the chapter is a company that built its own front door, for a process it had already made the center of its business, and forgot to ask who could open it.

Key conceptKinds of software, and where software comes from

System software, the operating systems and utilities that run the machine, versus application software, the programs that do the business's actual work: a register, a payroll system, a pizza-ordering app. Applications arrive three ways. Custom software is built for you, fits exactly, and costs the most to create and maintain. Packaged (off-the-shelf) software is bought and installed, cheaper, and fits approximately, which is why Chapter 7 spent a whole case on the configure-versus-customize decision. Software as a service (SaaS) is rented: it runs on the vendor's computers, you reach it through a browser, and you pay by subscription. Most new business software today is SaaS, for reasons the next chapter prices out in full.

Domino's ordering system sits at the expensive end: custom software, built and owned, because Chapter 4 established that the ordering pipeline is the business, exactly the situation where custom is worth it. Which makes the case sharper, not softer: this was not a neglected vendor product the company barely controlled. It was the software Domino's chose to build itself, and the gap in it was a gap in the company's own design decisions.

The interface decides who gets in

For the first decades of computing, the interface was a command line: a blinking cursor awaiting typed instructions in a syntax you had to already know. The population of people who could use a computer was, in effect, the population willing to memorize it. The graphical interface, windows, menus, a mouse, things you could recognize instead of recall, did not make computers more powerful. It made them usable by more people, and the personal-computing industry is the direct result. Hold that historical fact as a principle: every interface decision is a decision about who is in your market.

Key conceptUsability

Usability is not aesthetic preference; it is a set of measurable properties. Learnability: how quickly can a new user do the core task? Efficiency: how fast is the task once learned? Error tolerance: how easily do users make mistakes, and how gracefully can they recover? You can put numbers on each, by watching real users attempt real tasks, which is what Lab 9 will have you do. The business stakes compound quietly. A confusing checkout leaks customers one abandoned cart at a time. Inside the company, hard-to-use enterprise software taxes every employee every day, a cost that never appears on the invoice of the system that causes it. The tax is measurable. One of this book's authors studied nurses using electronic medical records and found that the same system produced two different kinds of stress: the good kind, where a demanding tool feels worth mastering because it is useful and somebody helped, and the bad kind, where it feels imposed and pointless, which predicts wanting to leave the job. Usability is not a nicety. It is the difference between those two.

Accessibility is not a feature request

Key conceptAccessibility and WCAG

Accessibility is usability's widest circle: designing software that works for people with disabilities, including blind and low-vision users on screen readers, deaf users needing captions, and people navigating by keyboard or voice because they cannot use a mouse. The World Wide Web Consortium's Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG) are the de facto standard, organized around four properties: content must be perceivable, operable, understandable, and robust. In practice much of it is unglamorous discipline: labeling images and buttons so a screen reader has something to say, making every function reachable by keyboard, maintaining contrast a low-vision user can read.

Two facts reframe accessibility from charity to strategy. First, scale: the World Health Organization estimates about one in six people worldwide live with a significant disability, a market no retailer would knowingly lock out. Second, the curb-cut effect: features built for disability keep turning out to serve everyone, the way sidewalk curb cuts built for wheelchairs now serve strollers, suitcases, and delivery carts. Captions serve the noisy gym; voice interfaces serve drivers; high contrast serves your phone in sunlight. Accessible design is, reliably, just better design under constraints, and building it in from the start costs a fraction of retrofitting it under a court deadline, which brings us back to the pizza.

02How businesses choose software

Design is one software decision. Selection is the other, and by far the more common one, because most companies buy far more software than they build. Done badly it produces the systems everyone hates, and Chapter 7 showed you the nine-figure version. The professional process is unglamorous and learnable, and it ends with a question that decides how much of your company's technology you should own at all.

Design is one software decision; the other, far more common one is selection, because most companies buy far more software than they build. Selection done badly is how organizations end up with systems everyone hates, and Chapter 7 showed you the nine-figure version. The professional process is unglamorous and learnable. (Learnable is the word to notice. Nobody is born knowing how to buy software, and most managers never get taught.) Here it is:

Key conceptSoftware selection and total cost of ownership

Start with requirements, written before any vendor demo, split into must-have and nice-to-have, because a good demo will otherwise write your requirements for you. Shortlist, then run scripted demos: every vendor performs your tasks on your sample data, not their rehearsed happy path. Compute total cost of ownership (TCO): the license or subscription is often the minority of the real cost once implementation, integration, training, support, and the eventual cost of leaving are added. Check references from companies your size, then pilot with real users before committing. And after this chapter, one line belongs in every requirements document: conformance with WCAG, verified in the demo with a screen reader running, because section 04 establishes what that line is worth.

Build, buy, or subscribe

The strategic layer of selection is the oldest question in corporate IT: build it, buy it, or rent it? The working rule falls straight out of Chapter 3. If the capability is a commodity, something every business does the same way, payroll, email, accounting, buy or subscribe, because custom-building it buys you no advantage and all the maintenance. If the capability is your differentiator, the thing customers choose you for, owning it can be worth the enormous cost. That is why Domino's built its ordering pipeline, and why Chapter 4's whole story exists. The judgment call is knowing which is which, and the honest test is Porter's: would doing this better than rivals actually change what customers do?

Hold this rule for next week. The next chapter's case is a company that pushed it to the extreme: it rents essentially all of its computing from its fiercest competitor, and spends heavily to build the one piece that touches the customer experience most. The rule scales from a co-op's point-of-sale choice to a $100 billion streaming service without changing shape.

Try it · Build, buy, or subscribe?

Five software needs at the Cascadia co-op. Decide what it should do about each one before you check.

Ask two questions. Is this the thing customers choose us for? If yes, owning it can be worth the cost. If no, is it standard enough that a rented version exists? Subscribe if it is; buy a license if you need it on your own machines.

03Three interfaces that decided the outcome

Before the case, three short ones. In each, the system underneath worked exactly as built, and the interface decided the outcome anyway. That is the claim section 01 makes in the abstract; these are what it looks like when it costs something.

Mini-case 1 · 2000: the ballot that was hard to read

In the 2000 United States presidential election, Palm Beach County in Florida used a ballot that listed candidates on two facing pages with a single column of punch holes running down the middle. To vote for the second candidate on the left page, you punched the third hole, because the second hole belonged to the first candidate on the right page. It was designed that way to fit ten candidates in large type, by an election official trying to help older voters see the names.

The result was thousands of votes for a minor candidate whose name sat opposite the second hole, far more than he received in comparable counties. Many thousands more ballots were punched twice and discarded. The presidential election was decided in Florida by a margin of 537 votes.

The voting machines counted correctly. The tabulation was accurate. The interface, a piece of card and a row of holes, determined what people were able to express. No test of the counting system would ever have caught that.

What decided the outcome: the layout. The component: hardware. This is the purest version of section 01's claim that every interface decision is a decision about who is in your market, and here the market was an electorate.

Check yourselfThe official who designed the ballot did it to make the names larger for older voters. Say what she should have done before printing it, using the vocabulary in section 01, and say why that step is so routinely skipped.

Mini-case 2 · 2018: the menu with no undo

At 8:07 on a Saturday morning in January 2018, every phone in Hawaii received a message telling people to seek immediate shelter from an incoming ballistic missile, and that this was not a drill. It was not true. It took 38 minutes to send a correction, during which people said goodbye to their families, drove into ditches, and put children into storm drains.

An employee running a routine shift-change drill had selected the live alert instead of the test alert. The two were adjacent options on a dropdown menu. There was a confirmation prompt, and it looked identical for both. There was no way to cancel a live alert from the same screen, which is why the correction took 38 minutes. Nobody had built the interface for the case where the thing you had just done was wrong.

The state's emergency management administrator resigned. The federal communications regulator investigated. The findings were about training and procedure, and they were also about a screen that made the most dangerous action in the building as easy as the most routine one.

What decided the outcome: the menu. The component: software. Section 01's error tolerance is not an abstraction. It is the question of what happens in the second after somebody clicks the wrong thing, and this interface had no answer.

Check yourselfSketch, in words, the three changes to that screen that would have prevented this or shortened it. Then name one system you use where the destructive action and the routine one sit next to each other with the same confirmation.

Mini-case 3 · 2024: the app that made the speakers stop working

One morning in May 2024, people who owned expensive speakers found that their alarms had not gone off, their sleep timers were gone, and in some cases the speakers would not respond at all. Nothing in the hardware had changed. Sonos makes wireless speakers that are controlled entirely through its app, and that month it released a rebuilt version of that app, timed to accompany the launch of its first headphones. The new app was missing features customers used every day: sleep timers, editing a queue, searching a local music library. Alarms did not fire. Some systems could not be controlled at all. And support for screen readers, which the previous app had, went backwards.

The hardware was unchanged and working. The speakers on people's shelves were exactly as good as the week before. What changed was the only door customers had into them, and the company's first response was to defend the redesign as a better experience.

Eight months later the chief executive resigned. By then the company had said fixing the app would cost between $20 million and $30 million. It had cut about six percent of its staff, reported a quarter in which revenue fell by around sixteen percent, and appointed an ombudsperson to publish reports on quality. It also said it could not go back to the old app, because the new one had changed the architecture underneath.

What decided the outcome: a launch date. The component: people. The app shipped when the headphones were ready rather than when the app was, and that was a decision made by named executives. Notice, too, that accessibility was the first thing to fall off, which is exactly the pattern section 01 describes and the case that follows turned into a lawsuit.

Check yourselfSonos had a testing process and shipped anyway. Using section 02's selection vocabulary, say what a scripted demo with real customers doing real tasks would have shown, and say why a company that knew that would still choose the launch date.

What the three have in common

A ballot, a dropdown menu and a mobile app. In each, the system underneath was correct: the votes were counted accurately, the alert system worked as designed, the speakers played. And in each, the interface decided what people could actually do, which is the only thing they experience.

Hold the third one especially close for the case, because it is the same failure at a different scale. Sonos removed screen-reader support and lost a chief executive. Domino's never added it and ended up at the door of the Supreme Court. The interface is where a business meets a person, and it is the part of the system a court can see.

04Robles v. Domino's: the ADA meets the web

Guillermo Robles tried to order a pizza. He is blind, he uses software that reads a screen aloud, and Domino's website and app would not let him finish. Domino's, the company from Chapter 4, had rebuilt itself as "a tech company that happens to sell pizza," and by the mid-2010s a large share of its orders arrived through its website and app. That is the front door Guillermo Robles walked up to. His screen reader converts on-screen text and controls into speech, and it works only when software is built with the underlying labels and structure the reader needs. Domino's site and app were not. On at least two occasions he could not complete an order or claim an online-only discount, which meant the company's digital front door, its proudest system, was closed to him. In September 2016 he sued under the Americans with Disabilities Act.

Domino's defense is worth stating fairly, because your discussion questions will ask you to weigh it. The company made three arguments. The ADA, a 1990 law about physical places of public accommodation, does not reach websites and apps at all. No federal regulations existed telling businesses what an accessible website even is. And Mr. Robles was not shut out of Domino's, because he could always order by phone. Keep those three arguments in mind; a federal appeals court is going to answer each one, and the answers now bind every business with a website. But first, the concepts the case sits on.

The ruling, and what it now binds

The district court initially sided with Domino's on procedure, reasoning that without federal web-accessibility regulations, courts should wait for the Department of Justice to define the rules. In January 2019 the Ninth Circuit Court of Appeals reversed, and its two holdings are the ones that matter. First: Title III of the ADA applies to the websites and apps of a business where they connect customers to the goods and services of its physical locations. Domino's, with thousands of stores, plainly had that connection, which the court calls a nexus. The court put it directly: the website and app facilitate access to the goods and services of a place of public accommodation. Second: the absence of formal regulations is not a due-process escape hatch; the statute's obligation to communicate effectively with disabled customers was itself fair notice. In October 2019 the Supreme Court declined to hear Domino's appeal, leaving the ruling standing, and the case later ended in settlement.

Notice the structure of the legal lesson, because you have seen it before. In Chapter 9, regulators' position on AI hiring tools was that existing discrimination law applies no matter how novel the technology. Here, a 1990 statute written before the web reached the web anyway, because courts read laws by their purpose, not by the technology of their era. "There are no rules for this yet" is one of the most expensive sentences in business, and the aftermath proved it. With the precedent standing, web-accessibility suits, already climbing, now number in the thousands per year. The practical standard companies are held to is the WCAG guidance that was freely available the whole time. The cheapest moment to meet it was before the lawsuit.

The interface is a legal surface. Domino's learned it from a pizza order; every business with a website inherited the lesson.

Since the ruling

The Ninth Circuit's decision stood, and the practical standard businesses are held to is the WCAG guidance that was freely available the whole time. Web-accessibility suits in the United States have run to several thousand a year since, many of them against small businesses that never imagined their website was a legal surface.

The law has moved too, unevenly. In 2024 the United States Department of Justice issued a rule requiring state and local government websites and apps to meet a specific version of WCAG by April 2026. In April 2026, days before that deadline, it pushed the dates back a year, to 2027 and 2028, and said it might revise the rule itself. The National Federation of the Blind sued over the delay. The standard did not change and the private lawsuits did not pause: more than three thousand federal suits were filed against businesses in 2025 alone. In 2025 the European Union's accessibility act came into force, extending requirements to private-sector products and services including websites, apps and self-service terminals. And the Sonos case in section 03 shows the market's version of the same lesson. A company that went backwards on accessibility in 2024 lost customers, revenue and a chief executive without any lawsuit at all.

The direction is not in doubt, even where the deadlines slip. What was arguable in 2016 is settled in 2026: the interface is a legal surface, and the cheapest moment to meet the standard is before anybody has to ask.

05Who software forgets

Robles is the visible case of a quiet, general pattern: software forgets the users its builders did not picture. Disabled users are the sharpest example, but the circle is wider. Older users meet interfaces tuned to reflexes trained by two decades of apps they never used. Low-income users meet sites built and tested on new phones over fast connections. Non-native speakers meet error messages written in idiom. None of this is malice; it is the default outcome of teams building for themselves, which is why deliberate practices, accessibility standards, usability testing with people unlike the builders, exist at all.

And one forgotten user lives inside the company: the employee. The person who selects enterprise software is almost never the person who will use it eight hours a day, and the gap shows. The warehouse worker fighting a fifteen-click process, the nurse clicking through screens designed by a billing department, pay daily for a selection decision they were never consulted on. Section 02's pilot step exists precisely to close that gap. The best single question to ask about any software purchase is the one this chapter has been circling: who was in the room when this was chosen, and who has to live with it?

Key takeaways
  • The interface is the part of the system that decides who gets to be a customer. Everything behind it can work and the front door can still be closed.
  • Accessibility is usability's widest circle, and the WCAG guidance was freely available the whole time Domino's argued there were no rules.
  • Build your differentiator; buy or subscribe to your commodities. Custom-building a commodity buys you no advantage and all the maintenance.
  • Write the requirements before the demo, or the demo will write them for you. Then pilot with real users doing real tasks.
  • "There are no rules for this yet" is one of the most expensive sentences in business. The law caught up, unevenly, and the private suits never paused.

07Discussion questions

None of these get handed in. On Wednesday you run the discussion and I steer it, which means the class is only as good as what you walk in with. Read the chapter, pick the question you have the strongest opinion about, and be ready to say it first.

  1. Domino's made three arguments: the ADA is about physical places, there were no regulations to follow, and Mr. Robles could have phoned. Take the strongest of the three and make its best case, then explain what the court said and whether you find it convincing. Analyze
  2. Pick an app you use daily and score it on the three usability properties from section 01, with a number and a reason for each. Then say what it would take to score it with a screen reader, and whether you think anyone at the company has. Apply
  3. Sonos removed screen-reader support and shipped; Domino's never added it. One lost a chief executive to the market, the other lost in court. Explain why a company with a testing process still ships an interface that fails some of its users, and name the decision that would have to change. Systems
  4. Write the accessibility line for Cascadia's next software requirements document, and describe exactly how you would verify it in a vendor demo. Then say what you would do if the best system on every other criterion failed that line. Apply
  5. "There are no rules for this yet" was Domino's defense in 2016 and it lost. Is it reasonable to hold a business to a standard that no regulator had written down? Argue one side, state the strongest objection, and say what the answer implies for the next technology that arrives before its rules do. Ethics
