Chapter 6 · Business Analytics

Business Analytics: How the Oakland A's Won by Measuring a Different Thing

In 2002 the Oakland Athletics lost their three best players, fielded one of the cheapest rosters in baseball, and won 103 games. They did it with data everybody already had, in a sport that had been recording it for a century. This chapter is about why choosing the measure is the decision that matters, and why the advantage did not last.

Every organization you will work for has more data than it can use. This chapter is about the part that is still scarce: a person who knows which question to ask of the data, which number to trust, and how to show the answer so that somebody acts on it.

Learning objectives

By the end of this chapter you should be able to:

  1. Explain what big data is, and the difference between business intelligence and business analytics.
  2. Place a question on the four types of analytics (descriptive, diagnostic, predictive, prescriptive), give a business example of each, and say what each costs when it is wrong.
  3. Explain why every metric is a proxy, and test a metric for validity against the thing it is supposed to measure.
  4. Recognize correlation without causation, a confounding variable, and the small-sample problem in a business claim.
  5. State Goodhart's law, and explain how a dashboard and a chart can each steer what a decision-maker sees.
  6. Explain how the Oakland A's gained an advantage from the same data everyone had, and which component carried it.

1Big data, and what business analytics does with it

You have probably heard of big data. No, not the band. Since cloud computing made storage cheap, companies and other organizations have been able to collect and keep data on almost anything. Every click on a website, every swipe of a loyalty card, every scan at a register, and every reading from a sensor on a delivery truck can be stored. That is big data. People usually describe it with three words. There is a lot of it (volume). It arrives constantly (velocity). And it comes in many shapes, from tidy tables to photos and free text (variety).

About the band: Big Data named itself after the same idea, and its 2014 hit “Dangerous” makes decent background music for this chapter.

Collecting all of that is no longer the hard part. The hard part is what happens next, when somebody asks the data a question. So this chapter looks inside one component of an information system, the data component, and follows what happens there after the data is stored.

In five-component terms, everything in this chapter sits on top of the data and is decided by the people. The hardware and software are ordinary and you can buy them, which Chapter 3 already told you means they cannot be the advantage. What you cannot buy is a person who asks whether the number everybody is staring at measures what everybody assumes it measures. That question runs through the whole chapter.

1.1 Business intelligence and business analytics

Two names get used for this work, and students mix them up because professionals do too. Here is the difference.

Business intelligence (BI) is the set of systems and reports that turn an organization's data into information about what is happening: the data warehouse, the standard reports, the dashboards. Business analytics is the wider practice of using that data to answer questions, including why something happened, what will happen next, and what to do about it.

The simplest way to hold the two apart is this. BI is mostly the pipes and the display. Business analytics is the questioning. In practice the line is blurry, and many companies use either name for the whole thing. This book uses business analytics for the practice, and keeps "BI" for the tools, as in a BI dashboard or the BI team.

You have been doing business analytics in the labs for four weeks without calling it that. Lab 2's totals described what happened. Lab 5's mini project, where something was wrong at Cascadia and you had to find out what, asked why. Lab 5's forecasting section asked what would happen next. Those are three of the four types of analytics, and the four types are the backbone of this chapter.

Analysts sort questions into four types of analytics, in rising order of ambition. Descriptive analytics asks what happened. Diagnostic analytics asks why it happened. Predictive analytics asks what will happen. Prescriptive analytics asks what you should do about it. The figure shows them as four steps, and sections 1.2 to 1.5 take one step each.

Read the figure from left to right and two things grow together. Each type is worth more than the one before, because its answer sits closer to a decision. Each also costs more, in data, in skill, and in damage when the answer is wrong. Most organizations live on the first step, with more descriptive reports than anyone reads. (Most of you will spend your first job on the first two steps. That is not a demotion. It is where most of the work is.) The organizations that move to the right are the ones that turn data into different decisions rather than prettier summaries.

1.2 Descriptive analytics: what happened?

Descriptive analytics summarizes past data to answer one question: what happened? Totals, averages, counts, rankings, and trends over time are all descriptive.

Almost every report a business produces is descriptive: weekly sales by store, last month's website visits, this quarter's customer complaints. You have already seen a polished version of it. Spotify Wrapped, from Chapter 1, is descriptive analytics turned into a product. It counts what you played and ranks it, and it never asks why you played it. A monthly sales report does the same job for a store manager.

Descriptive work rests on three ideas you will use for the rest of the chapter. A metric is a number chosen to stand in for something you care about. A key performance indicator (KPI) is a metric the organization has decided to manage by, usually with a target attached. And almost every metric is a proxy: a stand-in for the real thing, not the real thing itself. A hospital cares about patients getting well, and it counts patients seen. A store cares about loyal customers, and it counts revenue.

That makes the most important descriptive question a question about the measure itself. Measurement validity asks whether a metric actually tracks the thing it is supposed to stand for. Revenue can rise while loyal customers drift away, if one-time shoppers cover the gap. At Cascadia, revenue looked healthy at the Bellingham store while the member attach rate was collapsing, and you needed the second number to see it.

Baseball in 2001 was a perfect specimen of a descriptive-analytics industry. It had more data per event than almost any business on earth, a box score for every game since the 1870s, and it used that mountain of data mostly to describe, not to question. The questioning, when it came, came from outside. A night-shift statistician named Bill James had spent two decades publishing analyses, under the name sabermetrics, showing that some of baseball's most cherished numbers barely predicted winning. The Oakland A's were simply the first team desperate enough to act on it.

The most cherished of those numbers was batting average, the headline measure of a hitter for a century. It ignores walks entirely. A walk puts a runner on base without using up an out, and batting average records it as if it never happened. That is a validity problem hiding in plain sight, and section 4 shows what one team did when it noticed.

When descriptive analytics goes wrong, the usual damage is a misleading slide or a report nobody questions. That sounds small, and it is the cheapest step in the figure. But every other type of analytics is built on top of a description, so a bad one travels.

1.3 Diagnostic analytics: why did it happen?

Diagnostic analytics looks for the cause of something descriptive analytics found. It compares, breaks the numbers into smaller pieces, and rules explanations out until one is left.

Diagnostic work starts when a number surprises somebody. Say online sales at a sporting goods chain fell 8 percent last month. The analyst breaks that total apart: by region, then by product, then by device, then by day. BI tools call this a drill-down, which means starting from one total and opening up the pieces that make it. Maybe the whole drop sits in one region, on phones, starting on one date. That points toward a cause. Perhaps a website update that week broke the checkout button on one mobile browser.

Three habits make diagnostic work reliable. Segmentation means splitting customers or transactions into groups that might behave differently, such as new and returning customers. Comparison against a baseline means asking "compared with what?" every time: last month, the same month last year, the other stores. And a root cause is the explanation that is left after the others have been ruled out, not the first one that fits.

Your Lab 5 mini project was diagnostic analytics. Bellingham's member numbers were down, and you compared the store with seven others until the problem had a shape. Diagnostic analytics is also where the most common reasoning mistake in business lives: seeing two numbers move together and deciding that one caused the other. Section 2 takes that mistake apart.

A wrong diagnosis costs more than a wrong description, because people act on it. If you decide the online sales drop was caused by prices and cut prices, you spend money and the broken checkout stays broken.

1.4 Predictive analytics: what will happen?

Predictive analytics uses patterns in past data to estimate what will happen next, or what is true about a case you have not seen yet.

You meet it every day. A grocery chain forecasts how much milk each store will sell next week, so it can order enough without throwing the rest away. A lender looks at your credit history and estimates how likely you are to repay a loan, which is what a credit score is. An airline estimates how many passengers will not show up for a flight, which is why it sells a few more seats than the plane holds.

Every prediction rests on one assumption: the future will look enough like the past that the past's patterns still hold. A forecast is a prediction about a quantity over time, like next quarter's sales. A model is the rule, simple or complicated, that turns past data into the prediction. Your Lab 5 forecast was a simple model. Chapter 12 covers the machine learning models behind most of the predictions you meet online, and its argument starts here: a model learns from the data it is given, so it can only be as good as that data.

When a prediction is wrong, the plan built on it is wrong. The milk spoils, or the shelf is empty. Predictions also fail suddenly when the world changes in a way the past data never saw, which is why an honest forecast always comes with a sense of how far off it might be.

1.5 Prescriptive analytics: what should we do?

Prescriptive analytics recommends an action, and sometimes takes it. It combines a prediction with a goal and the rules of the business, and searches for the choice that best meets the goal.

One well-known example is UPS. Its routing system, called ORION, works out the order in which each driver should make the day's stops. A route of about 120 stops can be run in an enormous number of orders, and the system compares more than 200,000 of them before choosing one. In 2015 UPS said the system was expected to cut about 100 million miles of driving a year once fully deployed. The company has also said that one mile less per driver per day is worth about $50 million a year. Prices that change by the hour on airline tickets, and the reorder quantities a store's system suggests, are prescriptive analytics too.

The key idea at this step is authority: how far a recommendation can travel before a person checks it. Some prescriptive systems advise, and a person decides. A UPS driver still drives the truck, and still uses judgment on the street. Other systems act directly, with nobody in between.

Zillow shows what happens when that distance gets long. Zillow is a property website, and for years its price estimate was a prediction shown to readers: a guess that a person weighed against everything else they knew. In 2018 Zillow started buying houses itself, using its own estimates to decide what to pay, renovating the houses and selling them on. The estimate was no longer advice for a reader. It was an instruction to spend money.

The model was not wildly wrong. It was wrong by a few percentage points, in a business where a few percentage points is the entire margin, and it was wrong in the same direction across thousands of houses at once. In November 2021 Zillow shut the business down, announced writedowns of up to $569 million, and cut about a quarter of its staff. The decision that cost the money was not made by the model. It was made by the executives who decided how far the model's answers could travel without a human in between. That is the same decision Chapter 4 asked you to make about handing steps to an agent.

This is the right-hand end of the figure, where the bar is tallest. A bad description produces a bad slide. A bad prediction produces a bad plan. A bad prescription has already spent the money by the time anyone notices.

2Reading and showing numbers honestly

Choosing the right measure is the first job of business analytics. Reading it without fooling yourself is the second, and showing it so that other people see what it says is the third. The first four subsections here are traps that catch people who are perfectly good at the arithmetic. The last three are about where the numbers live and how they reach the person making the decision.

2.1 Correlation: when two numbers move together

Two numbers are correlated when they tend to move together. If one goes up when the other goes up, the correlation is positive. If one goes up when the other goes down, it is negative.

Analysts measure correlation with a number called the correlation coefficient, written r, which runs from −1 to 1. Close to 1 means the points fall near a rising line. Close to 0 means there is no straight-line pattern at all. The figure shows three made-up examples.

Correlation is useful. It is how you find leads: which customers buy more, which stores struggle, which products sell together. A grocery chain that notices barbecue sauce and charcoal sell together can put them near each other without knowing anything deeper. But correlation only tells you that two numbers move together. It does not tell you why, and it does not tell you that changing one will change the other.

2.2 Causation: when one number drives the other

Causation means one thing actually produces the other. Change the first, and the second changes because of it.

The gap between correlation and causation is where expensive mistakes happen. Suppose a coffee chain finds that customers who use its mobile app spend far more than customers who do not. The tempting conclusion is that the app makes people spend more, so the chain should push everyone onto the app. But there is another explanation. The chain's most loyal customers were always going to spend more, and they are also the people most likely to bother downloading the app. Loyalty drives both numbers, and the app may be doing very little.

That hidden third factor has a name: a confounding variable, something that drives both of the numbers you are comparing. Two other explanations are worth checking every time. The cause might run the other way: heavy spenders might download the app because they already visit so often. Or the pattern might be coincidence, which becomes likely when somebody has compared enough pairs of numbers.

The way a business gets from correlation to causation is an experiment. Give a change to a randomly chosen half of customers and not to the other half, then compare the two groups. Online companies call this an A/B test, and they run them constantly, on page layouts, prices, and email subject lines. Random assignment does the work: loyal and casual customers end up in both groups, so the confounder cancels out. When an experiment is not possible, the next best thing is a correlation with a believable mechanism behind it, which is exactly what the main case in section 4 relies on.

2.3 Sample size: how much data is enough?

The sample size is how many cases a number is based on. The small-sample problem is that a pattern built on a few cases can be produced by luck alone.

A/B tests run into this immediately. Suppose a store tests a new checkout page. After the first 50 visitors, the new page has converted twice as many shoppers as the old one. That looks like a win. After 500 visitors the difference has almost disappeared, because there never was one. The figure is a simulation of exactly that: two pages with the same true conversion rate, drawn as the running result after each visitor.

Small samples fool rankings too. Rank a chain's stores by return rate, and the smallest stores will tend to show up at both the top and the bottom of the list. That is not because small stores are better or worse. With few sales, a handful of returns moves the rate a lot. A manager who rewards the top store and visits the bottom one may be responding to luck.

Business runs on small samples more often than it admits: one viral month, one bad quarter, one focus group of nine people. Sports runs on them too. A baseball team plays 162 regular-season games, which is enough for skill to show. A playoff series is five or seven games, which is small enough for luck to decide it, and the main case will need you to remember that. The honest analyst's question, in Lab 5 and everywhere else, is whether the sample behind a pattern is large enough to bet on.

2.4 When the metric becomes the target

There is a trap waiting inside every successful metric, and it has a name: Goodhart's law, usually stated as when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure. The moment you pay people on a number, they optimize the number, not the thing it was a proxy for, and the gap between the two becomes the business's blind spot.

Call centers that grade agents on average handle time get shorter calls and angrier customers, because the fastest way to end a call is to not solve the problem. Schools graded on test scores drift toward teaching the test. A sales team paid on units shipped will push extra stock onto retailers in December and take it back as returns in January. In every case the metric was a reasonable proxy right up until it became the target, and then the measured number improved while the real thing decayed.

Goodhart's law is why choosing metrics is a management decision, not a reporting chore. The professional habit is to pair every target with a counter-metric that catches the gaming: handle time with resolution rate, units shipped with returns. Section 3 opens with a case where this happened inside a hospital system, with patients waiting on the other side of the number.

2.5 Where the numbers live: the BI stack

Before a number can reach a manager, it has to live somewhere, and that is usually not where it was first recorded.

Operational systems, the registers, websites, apps, and databases you will meet in the next two chapters, are built to record transactions quickly, one at a time. Analysis asks the opposite kind of question: patterns across millions of rows and years of history. Running those heavy questions against the registers' own database would slow the registers down. So organizations copy the data out.

The copying step is usually called ETL, for extract, transform, load. Pull the data from each operational system, clean it and make it consistent, and load it into a data warehouse, a database organized for analysis rather than for recording transactions. Dashboards and analysis tools sit on top of the warehouse. Together these layers are often called the BI stack.

Each layer is a place where the number can change. The transform step decides what counts as a sale, how a return is matched to its purchase, and what happens to a record with a missing store code. After Lab 3 you know that raw data and a cleaned copy are not the same thing, and a manager reading a dashboard sees only the cleaned copy. In five-component terms, the warehouse is data and the ETL and dashboard tools are software, but the rules inside the transform step were written by people. That is why "where did this number come from?" is a fair question to ask about any figure on any screen.

2.6 Dashboards: putting the number where the decision is made

A dashboard is a visual display of a small set of metrics, updated regularly, built so that a particular person can see at a glance whether something needs their attention.

The name comes from a car, and the comparison is useful. A car's dashboard shows a few things: speed, fuel, and warning lights. It does not show the engine's full sensor log, because the driver has one job and a few seconds to look. A business dashboard works the same way, and most bad dashboards fail by forgetting it.

Good dashboards share four habits. First, they are built for one audience. A store manager checking every morning needs different numbers from an executive checking every month, which is why organizations often separate operational dashboards, used to run today's work, from strategic dashboards, used to track direction over months. Second, they show few numbers, mostly KPIs tied to decisions somebody can actually make. Third, they give every number a comparison, such as a target, last year, or the other stores, because a number alone cannot tell you whether to worry. Fourth, they pair each target with its counter-metric from section 2.4, so gaming shows up on the same screen.

Building a dashboard used to require a programmer. Self-service BI tools, such as Tableau and Power BI, let an analyst or a manager connect to the warehouse and build the view directly. That is this week's lab, and it closes the course's longest-running thread. At Cascadia, revenue was the comfortable number: Bellingham's looked fine on every standard report. The member attach rate was the valid one, and it exposed the problem. You found it in a pivot table in Lab 5. Now you will make it impossible for a manager to miss, which is what a dashboard is for.

One warning comes with the tool. A dashboard is a decision about what people will look at, and so it is also a decision about what they will not look at. Whoever chooses the six numbers on the screen has quietly decided which problems the organization can see.

2.7 Data storytelling: a chart is an argument somebody built

Data storytelling is presenting data as an argument: the data itself, a visual that makes the important pattern easy to see, and a short narrative that says what the pattern means and what to do about it.

Numbers do not speak for themselves. Somebody decides which ones to show and which to leave out, what scale to draw them on, what to compare them against, and what sentence goes in the title. Every one of those choices pushes a reader toward a conclusion. That is not a flaw to remove. It is the job. A chart with no point leaves the decision-maker to find one, and they will usually find the wrong one or none at all. The goal is to make choices that show what the data actually says.

A good data story answers four questions before anyone else sees it. Who is it for, and what decision are they making? What is the one point? Compared with what? And what should the reader do next? A dashboard tile that says "Member attach rate: 49.8%" is a number. A title that says "Bellingham signs up members at about half the rate of every other store" is a story, and a manager can act on it.

The cost of getting this wrong has rarely been clearer than in 1986. The night before the Challenger launch, engineers at the contractor that built the shuttle's rocket boosters argued against flying. It was going to be unusually cold in Florida, and they believed the rubber seals between booster sections would seal less well at low temperature. They had data. Seals had been damaged on earlier flights, and the damage had been recorded.

What they did not have was a picture that made the relationship visible. The charts taken into that teleconference presented the damaged flights. They did not plot damage against temperature across all the flights, including the ones with no damage at all. Read that way, the evidence looked like scattered incidents rather than a trend. The launch went ahead the next morning in near-freezing conditions, and the shuttle was destroyed, killing all seven people aboard.

The statistician Edward Tufte later redrew the evidence with every flight on one temperature axis, and in that version the danger is obvious at a glance. His redrawing became a standard example in how to show quantitative information, because the engineers' argument was in their data and was not in their charts. Leaving out the flights where nothing happened removed the comparison that would have made the point. Nobody lacked the numbers, and nobody miscalculated. The display decided what a room full of decision-makers could see.

The same power works in the other direction. If the choices in a chart decide what a room sees, then a person who wants the room to see something can make those choices on purpose. Section 3's second case is about exactly that.

The one-sentence version

Business analytics is not having data. Everyone has data. It is choosing the measure that tracks what you actually care about, reading it honestly, and showing it where decisions get made.

3Mini-cases: when people shape the numbers

Before the main case, here are two short ones. In both, the data passed through people before it reached a decision, and those people changed what the decision-maker saw. The first is a metric that became a target, from section 2.4. The second is a chart built to tell one story, from section 2.7. Neither failure needed a calculation error.

3.1 Mini-case 1 · 2014: the wait that shrank on paper

The Department of Veterans Affairs runs one of the largest health care systems in the United States. In the years before 2014, it judged its medical centers partly on one number: how long veterans waited for an appointment. The target for new appointments was 14 days, and wait times counted toward managers' performance reviews and awards.

The wait was calculated by the VA's electronic scheduling system, using a field called the "desired date," meaning the date the veteran wanted to be seen. That design had a weak point. If a scheduler typed a later date into the field, the recorded wait got shorter, and the veteran's actual wait did not change at all. Veterans could also be kept on paper or unofficial lists instead of the official electronic wait list. As far as the system knew, their wait had not started.

In 2014 Sam Foote, a doctor who had worked at the Phoenix VA, went public with allegations of long delays and hidden lists. The VA's inspector general investigated. For a sample of veterans in Phoenix, the official data showed an average wait of about 24 days for a first primary care appointment. The actual average for that same sample was 115 days. The inspector general also found about 1,700 veterans waiting for appointments who were not on the official wait list at all, and said they were at risk of being "forgotten or lost."

Phoenix was not unusual. The Secretary of Veterans Affairs, Eric Shinseki, resigned at the end of May. A nationwide VA audit published in June covered 731 facilities. Thirteen percent of the scheduling staff it interviewed said they had been told to enter a date other than the one the veteran asked for, and schedulers at 90 clinics said they had done it to improve performance measures. The audit concluded that the 14-day target was not attainable with the doctors and appointment slots the VA had, and the VA removed the 14-day goal from employee performance contracts.

Reports that veterans had died while waiting for care made this a national story. The inspector general later said it could not conclude that the delays caused those deaths, while documenting serious delays and concerns about the quality of care. Both halves of that finding matter. The veterans were real, and so were the waits, whatever the records said.

The type: descriptive. The component: data, corrupted by people under pressure. Nothing was wrong with the scheduling software's arithmetic. Leaders set a target that could not be met, tied rewards to it, and measured it with a field that the people being measured could edit. Goodhart's law did the rest. The measure became a target, and it stopped measuring.

Check yourselfThe VA's wait-time metric was the gap between two dates. Say who controlled each date, and explain why that made the metric easy to game. Then name one counter-metric you would have put beside it.
Show the answer

One date was the appointment itself. The other, the desired date, was typed in by a scheduler working inside the facility whose performance the number judged. A metric that the people being measured can change by typing is not a measure of their work. It is a measure of how much pressure they are under. Good counter-metrics are ones nobody inside the clinic can edit. One is the date the veteran first called, stamped automatically by the phone or request system. Another is a survey asking veterans how long they actually waited. A third is a regular audit counting veterans who are waiting but not on the official list. The rule from section 2.4 holds: pair every target with a number the people being measured cannot move.

3.2 Mini-case 2 · 1995 to 2007: the chart that made a drug look gentler

In December 1995 the U.S. Food and Drug Administration approved OxyContin, an opioid painkiller made by Purdue Pharma. Its selling point was a timed-release design meant to spread one dose over about twelve hours. The label the FDA approved said that this delayed absorption was "believed to reduce the abuse liability" of the drug. Purdue had no studies showing that it did.

The label also carried a graph of the drug's level in the blood over time, drawn on a logarithmic scale. On that kind of axis, each step up multiplies the value, usually by ten, instead of adding a fixed amount. A level that falls steeply after its peak can look like a gentle, steady slope. To a busy doctor glancing at the page, the picture said smooth and steady. Redrawn on an ordinary scale, the levels rise quickly and fall quickly.

The sales force then pushed the visual argument further. In 2007 Purdue Frederick, the Purdue company that sold OxyContin, pleaded guilty to a federal felony charge of misbranding the drug, and three of its executives pleaded guilty to a misdemeanor charge. In the statement of facts filed with the court, the company admitted that its sales representatives had told some health care providers that OxyContin had less euphoric effect and less abuse potential than other opioids. It admitted that this message was delivered partly through graphs that exaggerated the differences in blood levels between OxyContin and other pain medications. The company and the executives paid more than $600 million.

The opioid crisis that followed has killed hundreds of thousands of people in the United States, and it has many causes. This chapter does not try to say how much of it one drug or one chart produced. The narrower point is the one this chapter is about. The numbers in a graph can be accurate and the graph can still tell a false story, because a person chose the scale, the comparison, and the words beside it. Here, one version of that story passed a regulator's review, and the sales training carried a stronger version into doctors' offices.

The type: descriptive. The component: people. The measurements were real. The story around them was built, and it was built by people who knew what they wanted the reader to conclude. This is data storytelling with its purpose reversed.

Show the answer

The scale itself was not the problem. A log scale is the right choice when values range from very small to very large, or when the reader cares about rates of change, and a pharmacologist reads one easily. The problem was the match between the chart and its reader. A busy prescriber looking at a sales graph sees a flat line and reads "steady." An honest chart picks a scale its audience will read correctly, labels it plainly, and says in words what the reader should take away. The misleading use depended on the reader not noticing the scale, and paired the picture with a claim, less abuse potential, that the data did not support. The questions from section 2.7 catch it: who is the reader, and what will they conclude in five seconds?

3.3 What the two have in common

Two cases, and neither was caused by bad arithmetic. The VA's software subtracted dates correctly. Purdue's blood-level data came from real measurements. In both, the failure sat in the people component: in who set the target and who could edit the field, and in who chose the scale and what claim went beside it.

That is the argument of this chapter, stated before the main case. Business analytics is not a calculation problem. It is a series of judgments about what to measure, how to show it, what to compare it against, and how far to let it act on its own. The case that follows is what it looks like when an organization gets the first of those judgments right while an entire industry gets it wrong.

4Main case · The Oakland A's: the team that measured differently

Everything so far in this chapter has been a concept with a short example. This case puts all of it in one place, in one organization, over one season and the twenty years after it. You will watch a baseball team with very little money use descriptive data that everyone owned, ask a diagnostic question nobody else asked, build a simple predictive model, and turn it into prescriptive decisions about whom to hire. You will see the five components of its information system, the fight inside the organization over whether to believe the numbers, and what happened once every competitor copied the idea.

Start with the trailer for Moneyball, the 2011 film made from Michael Lewis's book about this season. Brad Pitt plays general manager Billy Beane. The film is a dramatized version, and it changes names and compresses events, but it captures the problem and the argument quickly. Watch it, then read what the film leaves out.

4.1 The problem: three stars gone and a third of the money

After the 2001 season, the Oakland Athletics lost their best hitter, Jason Giambi, to the New York Yankees on a seven-year, $120 million contract.10 The same winter they lost outfielder Johnny Damon to Boston and closer Jason Isringhausen to St. Louis.12 Giambi had led the American League in on-base percentage in 2001, so Oakland lost exactly the kind of player this case is about.11

Oakland could not outbid anyone to replace them. Its 2002 payroll of about $40 million was the third lowest in Major League Baseball.13 The Yankees, the team Oakland had to beat to reach a World Series, spent roughly $125 million.14 By every conventional measure, the 2002 A's were supposed to collapse. Here is what happened instead.15

The result was 103 wins, a division title, and a twenty-game winning streak that set an American League record at the time. The streak ended its twentieth game on a walk-off home run by Scott Hatteberg, a catcher the A's had converted into a first baseman.15 The Yankees also won 103 games that year.16 Put those two facts next to the payrolls and you have the question this case answers.

General manager Billy Beane and his assistant Paul DePodesta could not buy the players everyone agreed were good, so they asked a more basic question: is everyone right about who is good? Baseball had a century of statistics and a settled consensus about which ones mattered. Beane and DePodesta treated that consensus as a hypothesis rather than a fact, tested it against the data, and found it wrong in a way they could use.17 In the language of section 1, they took the industry's descriptive numbers and asked a diagnostic question of them.

4.2 The question nobody was asking

Before you can buy an undervalued player, you have to know what value is, and that is where the Oakland argument actually starts. Baseball in 2001 judged hitters mainly by batting average, home runs, and runs batted in. It also relied on scouts, who had watched thousands of players and could tell you who looked like a ballplayer.17

Beane and DePodesta asked a narrower question: what actually causes a team to score runs? Work back from the rules. A team keeps batting until it makes three outs, so outs are the scarce resource, and anything that does not use one up has value. That reframing does something specific. It makes a walk nearly as valuable as a single, because both put a runner on base without spending an out. The conventional measures counted the single and ignored the walk.

On-base percentage (OBP), which counts every way a batter reaches base, walks included, tracked run-scoring better than batting average did. You can check this yourself with real numbers from that season.16

This was not Oakland's discovery. As section 1.2 said, Bill James and other analysts outside the game had been publishing versions of it for two decades, and the data was in the same record books everyone else owned.18 What Oakland did was believe it and spend money on it.

That is the part to take into business. The A's did not have better data. They had the same data as thirty other clubs, and a century of it in the public record. Their advantage was a better model of what the data meant, held while everyone else held a worse one. Chapter 2 said information has value only when it changes a decision. Here is the cleanest example in the book: the same numbers, on everybody's desk, changing exactly one organization's decisions.

4.3 Buying what the market mispriced

A quality that is measured badly becomes an asset that is priced badly. Because the market paid for batting average, home runs, and how an athlete looked in uniform, players whose main skill was getting on base were cheap. Hatteberg, coming off an injury that had damaged his throwing arm, was exactly that. His on-base skills made him a bargain, and in 2002 he posted a .374 OBP.15 Reliever Chad Bradford threw nearly underhand, looked unusual, got outs, and cost little.17 The roster was built on a market inefficiency: a price that had drifted away from the value it was supposed to track.

The A's also could not replace Giambi with one star. Instead, in Lewis's account, they replaced his production in the aggregate, with a combination of cheaper players whose on-base skills added up to something close to what they had lost.17 That is prescriptive analytics from section 1.5: a prediction about runs, plus a budget, plus a goal, turned into a decision about whom to sign.

One honest correction belongs here, because section 2.7 says every chart and every story is built by somebody. The book and the film both center the hitters. But the 2002 A's team on-base percentage ranked only seventh in the majors, and its team batting average sixteenth. The club also had three of the best young starting pitchers in baseball, Barry Zito, Tim Hudson, and Mark Mulder, and Zito won the Cy Young Award that year.13 The on-base insight was real and it was cheap, which is why it matters for this chapter. It was not the only reason Oakland won.

Connect this to Chapter 3. The A's found a competitive advantage grounded in information. Everyone had the same raw data, but Oakland turned it into a truer model of value than its rivals used. That let a payroll of about $40 million compete with teams spending three times as much.

4.4 The five components of the 2002 A's

Students expect a business analytics case to involve expensive technology, so it is worth saying plainly how ordinary Oakland's system was. Walk through it the way you have mapped every case in this course, and notice which components were common and which were scarce.

Data. The A's worked mostly from data every club could see: box scores, season statistics, and the record books. Their most distinctive use of data was in the 2002 draft, where Lewis describes the front office leaning on college players' statistics rather than on high school players whom scouts could only watch.17 A college player had several seasons of numbers, which made him a larger sample, in exactly the sense of section 2.3. The scouts' written reports were data too, just a different kind.

Hardware and software. Ordinary computers, spreadsheets, and statistical analysis. DePodesta, a Harvard economics graduate, did much of the analysis himself.17 Nothing in this layer was secret or expensive. Any club could have bought it.

Networks. The statistics were published, so every rival could read them. The A's also relied on a second, human network: the scouts who traveled to games and reported back. The front office's job was to decide how much to trust each network.

People. This is where the advantage lived. Beane chose to act on a model that contradicted a century of professional consensus, and DePodesta could build and defend that model. The manager, Art Howe, and the scouts were people in the system too, and as the next section shows, the model's answer had to pass through them before it reached the field.

Put the four types of analytics from sections 1.2 to 1.5 against this system. Descriptive analytics is what baseball already had, as section 1.2 said. Oakland's diagnostic question was why runs happen. Its model was predictive: it estimated how many runs a set of skills would produce, and so how many wins a player was worth. Then it became prescriptive, because each projection came with a price, which told the front office whom to sign and whom to let go. The calculation was the easy part.

4.5 The fight inside the building: analytics meets culture

An information advantage has to survive the organization that holds it, and this one nearly did not. The reason is the part of business analytics that no software vendor sells.

Organizational culture is the set of shared, mostly unwritten beliefs in an organization about what counts as good work, what counts as evidence, and who gets to decide.

Analytics is a technology, but it always runs inside a culture, and the culture decides whether its answers change anything. Baseball's culture in 2001 trusted the experienced eye. It prized home run hitters and high batting averages, it valued players who looked the part, and it gave the final word to scouts and managers who had spent their lives in the game. A spreadsheet that said a walk was nearly as good as a hit was not just new information. It was a claim that the people with the most status had been measuring the wrong thing.

The scouts had spent their careers learning to judge players by watching them, and the new method told them their expertise was mispriced. In Lewis's account the meetings were hostile.17 It is worth being fair to the scouts rather than treating them as the villains. Each kind of evidence sees something the other misses, which is why most clubs today use both.

The sharper problem was that the analysis could be overruled downstream. The front office wanted Hatteberg at first base. The manager preferred a different player and kept writing a different name on the lineup card. Lewis describes Beane resolving it by trading the other player away in the middle of the season, which removed the option rather than winning the argument.17

Read that as a management case, not a baseball story. A measure that decision-makers can ignore is not a decision system. It is a report. Section 2.6 said the work is putting the number where the decision is made. Oakland's front office found out that the number also has to arrive with the authority to be acted on. When it does not, somebody ends up removing choices instead of changing minds, which is expensive and damages relationships. That is what happens when analytics is installed beside an organization instead of inside it.

Businesses face the same fight. Google's engineers were famously skeptical that managers added any value at all. In the account Harvard Business Review published in 2013, Google did not win that argument with a memo. Its people-analytics team studied its own managers with the kind of data and rigor the engineers respected, found that better managers mattered, and turned the findings into specific behaviors and feedback surveys.19 The lesson runs both ways. Analytics changes a culture fastest when it speaks the culture's own language of evidence. And a culture that will not accept any evidence against its beliefs will beat the best model in the building.

4.6 What's next: baseball catches up

Chapter 3 predicted the ending of this story. An advantage built on information lasts only as long as the information stays scarce. This one did not stay scarce, partly because Michael Lewis published a book explaining it in 2003.

The Boston Red Sox hired Bill James as an adviser in November 2002, and in 2004 they won their first World Series since 1918.20 Within about a decade every club in the league employed analysts. Once every team measured correctly, measuring correctly stopped being an edge. The advantage flowed back to the clubs with the most money, which now spent it efficiently. Oakland never won a World Series under Beane, and by the 2020s it was again among the lowest-payroll teams in the sport. The team played its last game in Oakland in September 2024 and moved to a minor-league park in West Sacramento. It is due to open a stadium in Las Vegas in 2028.21

The raw material changed too. Since 2015 a tracking system called Statcast has measured the speed and spin of every pitch and the movement of every player in every major-league park.22 This is big data from section 1, in a sport. When data becomes abundant, the scarce thing moves, and you should expect the same shift in any industry you join. It stops being measurement, which anybody can now buy, and becomes judgment about which of thousands of measures matters.

Then the analysis worked so well that the sport changed its rules to stop it. Teams had used data to reposition fielders, moving three infielders to one side of the field against hitters who almost always hit the ball there. It worked, and it also removed base hits that fans had paid to see. Before the 2023 season Major League Baseball banned the practice, requiring two infielders on each side of second base. Batting average on balls in play rose that season.23 A later study that compared affected and unaffected hitters, the kind of comparison section 2.2 recommends, found the ban raised left-handed hitters' results by a modest amount.24

4.7 Beyond baseball: other sports follow

Basketball went through the same change on a bigger scale. A three-point shot is worth 50 percent more than a two-point shot, and analysts pointed out that a long two-point jump shot is usually the least efficient shot on the floor. Daryl Morey, general manager of the Houston Rockets, built his team around that arithmetic. In the 2017–18 season the Rockets became the first team in NBA history to attempt more three-pointers than two-pointers, and they won 65 games.25

The rest of the league copied it. Teams averaged about 22 three-point attempts a game around 2015, and partway through the 2024–25 season the figure was about 37.5.25 Then the same pattern as baseball's shift appeared. Morey himself said in 2025 that the three-pointer had reached the point where it was starting to make the game worse.25 When every competitor optimizes the same number, the product the customer watches can change in ways nobody chose.

Other sports now employ analysts too, and players in several major leagues wear sensors or are tracked by cameras. That raised a question Oakland never faced: who owns the data about a player's body? Section 5 comes back to the players' answer.

4.8 Beyond sports: what organizations took from Oakland

"Moneyball" became business shorthand for finding value that a market misprices because it measures the wrong thing. Strip away the sport, and four patterns from this case show up in ordinary organizations.

Find the measure everyone trusts and test it. Every industry you will work in has its batting average: a number everyone watches because it has always been watched. Revenue per store, hours billed, calls handled, test scores. The first move is to ask whether it tracks the outcome the organization actually wants, which is the measurement validity test from section 1.2.

Expect people to resist, and plan for it. Oakland's scouts and Google's engineers both resisted data that challenged their expertise. Adoption is a people-component problem, and it is solved with evidence the culture respects and with authority over the decision, not with a better dashboard alone.

Expect the edge to be copied. Analytics advantages get copied faster than almost any other kind, because methods are easy to describe and data is increasingly easy to buy. What lasts is the habit of asking the question, held by people, not any single answer.

Watch what happens when everyone optimizes the same number. The shift ban and the three-point debate are Goodhart's law at the level of a whole industry. In business the same thing happens when every company optimizes for clicks, or for the same hiring score, or for the same delivery speed. Chapter 12 looks at what that does when the optimizing is done by a machine learning model.

4.9 What a business technology manager takes from this case

Strip the Oakland case down to what you can reuse in any company. Four decisions came up in it, and each one is a decision you will be in the room for.

Choose the measure before you build the dashboard. Baseball had a century of accurate statistics and measured the wrong one. Everyone had the same data; Oakland asked a different question of it. When someone shows you a report, the first question is not whether the numbers are right but whether they are the numbers that matter for the decision, and that is a judgment the software cannot make.

Show the data so that the danger is visible. The Challenger engineers had the right numbers and drew a chart that hid the relationship. A description that leaves half the evidence off the page is not a description. Before a chart goes to a decision-maker, ask what a reader would conclude from it in five seconds, and whether that is what the data says.

Put the analysis inside the decision, not beside it. Oakland's model mattered only when its answer reached the lineup card. Decide who has the authority to act on a number, and decide it on purpose. Zillow is the warning on the other side: an estimate that was fine as information was ruinous as an instruction, because nobody checked it before it acted.

Expect the advantage to end when everybody catches up. Within about a decade every team measured what Oakland measured, and the edge was gone. By 2023 the league had changed the rules to undo what the measuring produced. A measurement advantage is real, and it is never permanent. The lasting advantage is the habit of asking whether the consensus is right, and that habit is a person, not a system.

5The downside: when a person becomes a row of numbers

Every measurement system turns people into rows. A row is useful: it lets a club compare a thousand players, or a company compare ten thousand workers. But a row only holds what somebody chose to record, and the person it describes has no say in what that was. This section asks what that costs, in sports and at work.

Start with the people Moneyball celebrates. Analytics helped players the old system had overlooked. Hatteberg and Bradford were exactly the kind of players a scout's eye had discounted, and a measure that ignored how an athlete looked gave them a chance. Prejudice about appearance was part of the noise that on-base percentage filtered out. That is a real benefit, and it is worth saying first.

The cost is the other side of the same coin. To a model, a player is the numbers it records, and qualities the numbers do not capture, such as leadership or playing through pain, stop being priced. Players and their unions have noticed. When NBA teams began asking players to wear sensors that track their bodies, the players' union negotiated limits in its 2017 agreement with the league. Wearing a device is voluntary, and data from it cannot be used in contract negotiations, trades, or other player transactions.26 In other words, the people being measured insisted on a rule about what the system could be used to decide. That is a direct answer to whether athletes feel reduced to a number: enough of them did to write it into their contract.

The same tension runs through ordinary jobs, usually with less bargaining power. Warehouse workers at large retailers are measured continuously by systems that track how fast they work and how long they spend away from their task. At Amazon, that measure has been called "time off task."27 In 2021 California passed AB 701, the first state law of its kind. It requires large warehouse employers to tell workers what quotas they are measured against, lets workers request records of their own speed, and bars firing workers for missing a quota because they took rest or bathroom breaks.27 Retail groups opposed the law, arguing it would raise costs and slow deliveries.27

Notice the shape of both responses. Neither banned measurement. Both set rules about what the measurement could decide, and who could see it. That is the systems question this chapter has been building toward. Ask five questions about any metric that describes a person, including the ones you will be judged by. What does it record, and what does it leave out? Who sees it? Can the person see their own number? And what decisions is it allowed to drive? Those are design choices about the data component, made by people, and they are as much a part of business analytics as choosing on-base percentage over batting average.

Two honest footnotes belong in the Oakland case. The 2002 A's, for all 103 wins, lost in the first playoff round, in a five-game series small enough for luck to decide, as section 2.3 explained. Beane's Oakland teams never won a World Series. Analytics bought an edge, not a championship, and edges decay. Measuring right is necessary. It is not, on its own, sufficient, and it is never permanent.

Key takeaways
  • Business analytics is not a calculation problem. It is a series of judgments about what to measure, how to show it, what to check it against, and how far to let it act alone.
  • Every metric is a proxy. The first question is never what the number says; it is whether the number tracks the thing you actually care about.
  • When a measure becomes a target, it stops being a good measure. Pair every target with a counter-metric that catches the gaming.
  • A chart is an argument somebody built. Before you accept what it seems to say, ask who chose the scale, the comparison, and the title.
  • The A's did not have more data than the Yankees. They had a truer model of value, and the market inefficiency it revealed lasted only until everyone else adopted it.
  • A dashboard is a decision about what people will look at. The people who choose the metric, and how far its answers may travel without a human, are making the management decision.

6Vocabulary

Big data
Data collected in huge volume, at high velocity, and in a wide variety of forms, made possible by cheap storage.
Business intelligence (BI)
The systems and reports that turn organizational data into information about what is happening: warehouse, reports, dashboards.
Business analytics
The wider practice of using data to answer questions: what happened, why, what will happen, and what to do.
Descriptive / diagnostic / predictive / prescriptive
The four types of analytics: what happened, why, what will happen, what to do.
Metric / KPI
A number chosen to stand for something the business cares about; a key performance indicator is a metric elevated to a target.
Proxy
What every metric secretly is: a stand-in for the real thing, valid only as far as it tracks it.
Measurement validity
Whether a metric actually measures what it claims to.
Sabermetrics
The empirical analysis of baseball; the movement that found the industry's measurement error.
On-base percentage
Share of plate appearances reaching base; the undervalued, more valid measure.
Market inefficiency
A gap between price and value, here created by an industry-wide measurement error.
Correlation vs. causation
Moving together vs. one driving the other; trust correlations with a mechanism and a large sample.
Confounding variable
A hidden factor that drives two numbers you are comparing, making them look causally linked.
A/B test
An experiment that gives a change to a random half of customers and compares the two halves.
Small-sample problem
Patterns built on few cases can be produced by luck alone.
Goodhart's law
When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure.
ETL
Extract, transform, load: copying data out of operational systems, cleaning it, and loading it into a warehouse.
Data warehouse
A database organized for analysis, fed by copies of operational data.
Dashboard / self-service BI
Visual metric displays, and the tools (like Tableau) that let analysts build them directly.
Data storytelling
Presenting data as an argument: the data, a visual that shows the pattern, and a narrative saying what it means.

One warm-up worth doing: ask it to name three proxy metrics in an industry you care about, then judge for yourself which would survive this chapter's validity test.
