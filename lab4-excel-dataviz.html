Lab 4 · Week 4

Data Visualization: Charts That Tell the Truth

Lab session · 80 minPairs with Chapter 3 · Business Processes →

A chart is an argument, not decoration. The wrong chart makes a true finding unreadable, and a carefully wrong chart makes a false finding look obvious. Today you learn to pick the right one, and then you practice catching charts that lie while telling the truth.

Open cascadia-sales-2025.xlsx. You will build everything from pivot tables, so this is also good pivot practice before Exam 1.

From lecture to lab

Lab professor's intro (10 min): Connect to this week's Domino's case. The tracker made a process visible, and visibility is what lets anyone manage anything. Charts are how visibility reaches a manager, which means a misleading chart is a broken instrument. Part 1 of this lab is quick, matching the chart to the question. Part 3 is the real work, four charts built from true Cascadia numbers that each mislead in a different way. Worth doing the first one on the projector: show Chart A, ask the room how much bigger Bellevue is than Bellingham, let them answer from the picture, then reveal it is 8 percent. That moment lands better than any explanation.

Part 1The question decides the chart

Almost every chart mistake is really a question mistake: someone picked a shape before deciding what they were asking. Learn the mapping.

Bar chart: comparing across categories

One number measured across separate named things. Revenue by store. Units by product. The bars sit apart because the categories are unrelated, and length is what your eye compares, which is why bars work. Humans read length accurately.

Sort by value unless the categories have a natural order, and start the axis at zero. Both rules come back to bite you in Part 3.

Line chart: change over time

The horizontal axis is time and the points are steps in a sequence. Revenue by month. Enrollments by day. The connecting line is doing real work: it tells the reader these values belong to one continuous story, which is exactly why you must never use a line for categories.

Scatter plot: the relationship between two numbers

Two measurements per thing, one dot each, position carrying both. Store size against store revenue. Scatter plots answer a question no other chart can: they show you the outlier sitting off the pattern, which is usually the most interesting item in the dataset.

Pie chart: parts of one whole

Only when the slices sum to a meaningful 100 percent, and only with a handful of them. Humans compare angles badly. If you are ranking things, use a bar chart. Most pie charts in business decks should have been bar charts.

The test before you chart anything

Say the question out loud in one sentence. "How do the stores compare?" is a bar. "What happened over the year?" is a line. "Does bigger mean richer?" is a scatter. If you cannot say the question in a sentence, you are not ready to chart yet.

Part 2Build two, properly

The first chart we build together, the second is yours. Same rhythm as Labs 2 and 3: follow Part A's steps exactly, then repeat the moves alone.

Follow along: revenue by store, as a sorted bar

  1. Step 1. Click any single cell inside the data, then Insert → PivotTable (the how-to below has every click if this is your first pivot). Put store in Rows and revenue in Values.
  2. Step 2. Click inside the pivot, then Insert → Recommended Charts → Bar (or Column). A chart appears wired to the pivot.
  3. Step 3. Sort it: right-click any revenue value in the pivot → Sort → Largest to Smallest. The bars fall into order.
  4. Step 4. Check the axis starts at zero (it will by default; Part 3 shows you why that matters), then click the title and replace it with a sentence that states the finding, not the topic: "Seattle Flagship leads; the gap to last place is wide," not "Revenue by Store." Checkpoints 1 and 2.

Now you: revenue by month, as a line

Repeat the four steps alone with month in Rows instead of store, and choose the chart type yourself; Part 1 told you which shape time takes. Twelve points, time on the horizontal, title that states what the year did. Look at the shape of the co-op's year before moving on, because you will need it later. Checkpoints 3 and 4.

How to make a pivot table
  1. Click any single cell inside the data.
  2. Insert tab, then PivotTable. Mac same menu, but the button may read Summarize with PivotTable.
  3. Excel guesses the data range. Check that it covers all the rows, then choose New Worksheet and click OK.
  4. A panel appears on the right with your column names. Drag a field into Rows to group by it, and drag a number into Values to total it.
  5. If Values shows a count instead of a sum, click it, choose Value Field Settings, and pick Sum. This catches almost everyone once.

If your pivot is empty: you probably selected a single cell outside the data before inserting. Undo and click inside the table first.

How to insert and clean up a chart
  1. Select the pivot table results, including the row labels.
  2. Insert tab, then the chart type you want. Hover any icon to see its name before clicking.
  3. To sort a bar chart: sort the pivot table itself. Click the dropdown arrow on the Row Labels header, then More Sort Options, then sort by your value column, descending.
  4. To set the axis to zero: right-click the vertical axis, choose Format Axis, and set Minimum to 0.
  5. To retitle: click the chart title once, then type. To delete a legend you do not need, click it and press Delete.

Mac Chart tools live in the Chart Design and Format tabs that appear when a chart is selected.

Part 3Four charts that lie

Here is the part that matters. Each of the four charts below is built from Cascadia's real 2025 data. Every number in them is correct. Every one of them misleads. Build each one yourself so you can see how easy it is, then answer the checkpoint that exposes it.

Follow along: diagnosing Chart A, together

Diagnosis is a routine, not a talent. Run these four steps on Chart A with me; then Charts B, C, and D are yours to run it on alone.

  1. Step 1 · Read the axis before the bars. Look at the vertical axis's bottom number. It is $80,000, not zero. Any bar chart whose axis starts above zero is showing you differences, not sizes, while your eye reads sizes.
  2. Step 2 · Get the real numbers. Ignore the picture. From your own store pivot (you built it in Part 2), read Bellevue's revenue and Bellingham's revenue as digits.
  3. Step 3 · Compute the honest gap. In any cell: =(Bellevue - Bellingham)/Bellingham*100, using the two numbers from Step 2. That percentage is the truth. Checkpoint 5.
  4. Step 4 · Name the trick. Compare your Step 3 number to what the picture implied (Bellevue's bar looks roughly six times taller). The gap between those two is entirely manufactured by the axis. Say the mechanism in words: a truncated axis turns a small difference into a dramatic one. That sentence, mechanism named plainly, is what a diagnosis is.

Now you: Charts B, C, and D. Same routine, three charts, checkpoints 6 through 8. Step 1 changes shape each time, the thing to read first might be the chart type, the slice count, or the title, but the discipline is identical: picture last, numbers first, mechanism named.

Chart A: the truncated axis

Bar chart of Bellevue, Tacoma, and Bellingham revenue, with the vertical axis starting at $80,000 instead of zero. Build it. Bellevue's bar towers over Bellingham's, looking several times taller.

Then compute the actual gap between them. The distance between what the picture says and what the arithmetic says is the whole lesson, and this is the single most common way charts mislead in business, usually without anyone intending it.

Chart B: the line that invents a trend

Put the eight stores on a line chart, left to right, revenue on the vertical. The line slopes downward and looks like decline.

Nothing is declining. A line says these points are steps in a sequence, and stores are not a sequence. The proof: reorder the stores alphabetically and the "trend" changes shape entirely. Any pattern that depends on the order you happened to type things in was never a pattern.

Chart C: the unreadable pie

Pie chart of revenue by individual product. Build it and try to answer a simple question from it, such as which product is fourth largest. You cannot, and neither can anyone in your audience.

The chart is accurate and useless, which is its own category of failure. A chart that cannot be read is not a neutral choice; it consumes attention and returns nothing.

Chart D: the honest chart with the dishonest title

A clean line chart of monthly revenue, correctly built, titled "Revenue Collapsing After January." Revenue really does fall about 25 percent from January to February. The chart is accurate, the number is accurate, and the conclusion is false.

Look at the full year and you will see why. This is the most dangerous of the four, because there is nothing to catch by inspecting the chart itself. The lie lives in the framing, and the only defence is knowing enough about the business to notice that a seasonal dip has been renamed a collapse.

Where this connects to Monday

Healthcare.gov's leadership was not lied to. They received status reports that were accurate about what they measured: contract deliverables completed, milestones marked green. Nothing in those reports was false. What none of them said was whether the assembled system could serve the number of people who would arrive on October 1st. Accurate information, wrong question, catastrophic outcome. Every chart in this part of the lab is a small version of that.

Part 4Fix one

Pick whichever of the four you find most misleading and rebuild it honestly: right chart type, axis from zero, readable number of items, and a title that states what the data actually supports. Put the before and after side by side on one sheet.

How to actually fix a chart (the clicks)
  1. Fix a truncated axis: right-click the vertical axis → Format Axis → under Bounds, set Minimum to 0. Mac same right-click path.
  2. Fix a wrong chart type: click the chart → Chart Design tab → Change Chart Type → pick the shape Part 1 says the question needs.
  3. Fix an unreadable pie: change it to a sorted bar (previous step), or group the small products into an "All others" row in the pivot first.
  4. Fix a spun title: click the title once, select the text, and type a sentence the whole chart supports, not just one segment of it.

Then write one sentence naming who would benefit from the misleading version. Sometimes the answer is nobody and it was carelessness. Often it is not, and being able to say so plainly is part of the job you are training for.

When you get stuck

Before raising your hand, try these in order. Most problems in this lab are one of them.

  1. Re-read the checkpoint question. Is it asking for a count, a total, or an average? Those are three different numbers.
  2. Check whether your number is plausible. If total revenue comes out as 12 or as 40 million, something is off by a lot and you can usually see where.
  3. Expand the grey "How to" box for that step. It has the exact clicks.
  4. Check the formula bar of the cell you think is wrong. Does it reference the range you meant?
  5. Use the hint button on the checkpoint. It is not a penalty, it is part of the lab.
  6. Ask a neighbour. Then raise your hand.
